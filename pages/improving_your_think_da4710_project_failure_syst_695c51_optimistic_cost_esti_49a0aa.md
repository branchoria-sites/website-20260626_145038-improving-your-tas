## Introduction

Large projects rarely begin with forecasts that everyone knows are impossible. More often, they begin with estimates that are genuinely optimistic, selectively optimistic, or deliberately understated because optimistic proposals are more likely to secure approval than cautious ones. This creates a system-level problem: even if individual participants believe they are acting rationally, the overall funding process can reward inaccurate forecasts over realistic ones. Understanding this mechanism is an important analytical skill because it shifts attention away from blaming forecasting [mistakes]({{ 'mistakes/' | relative_url }}) alone and towards examining the incentives that shape forecasts before a project is approved.

Research on transport infrastructure provides one of the strongest evidence bases for this phenomenon. Decades of data show that cost overruns are not merely random forecasting errors. Instead, the persistence and direction of those errors suggest that the institutional environment often favours projects whose initial estimates appear unusually attractive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/09654310701747936" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online+2arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Taylor &amp; Francis OnlineCurbing Optimism Bias and Strategic Misrepresentation in...by B Flyvbjerg · 2008 · Cited by 696 — Second, the pap...</span></span></span>

## Why do weak estimates repeatedly win approval?

The striking feature of many large infrastructure projects is not simply that estimates are wrong. It is that they are wrong in a consistent direction. Initial costs tend to be too low while projected benefits are often too high.

If forecasting errors arose mainly from technical uncertainty, the mistakes would be expected to scatter around the true value. Some projects would prove cheaper than expected, others more expensive, with no systematic pattern. Instead, large datasets of transport projects show persistent underestimation of costs across countries and over long periods, suggesting that more than technical forecasting limitations are at work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/235953571_How_Optimism_Bias_and_Strategic_Misrepresentation_in_Early_Project_Development_Undermine_Implementation" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate+2arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) How Optimism Bias and Strategic Misrepresentation...12 May 2016 — People tend to judge future events inaccurately in t...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2016</span></span></span>

This matters because approval decisions are typically competitive. Governments and investors rarely fund every proposed railway, road or airport. Projects therefore compete on apparent affordability and expected returns. In such contests, promoters who present conservative estimates may lose funding to promoters whose forecasts appear more attractive, even if those forecasts are less realistic.

The incentive structure therefore rewards confidence before it rewards accuracy.

## Technical error versus strategic misrepresentation

Researchers commonly distinguish between two broad explanations for persistent cost underestimation.

### Technical error

The technical explanation assumes that planners are trying to estimate honestly but face imperfect information.

Sources of error include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* incomplete geological information;
* immature engineering designs;
* uncertain material prices;
* changing regulations;
* limited historical data.

</div>

Under this explanation, improving forecasting models, collecting better information and hiring more experienced estimators should gradually eliminate systematic errors.

Yet this prediction is difficult to reconcile with decades of evidence showing remarkably persistent cost overruns despite advances in engineering, software and project management.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/235953571_How_Optimism_Bias_and_Strategic_Misrepresentation_in_Early_Project_Development_Undermine_Implementation" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) How Optimism Bias and Strategic Misrepresentation...12 May 2016 — People tend to judge future events inaccurately in t...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2016</span></span></span>

### Strategic misrepresentation

The second explanation focuses on incentives rather than capability.

Here, promoters recognise that optimistic forecasts increase the probability of receiving approval, funding or political support. Costs may therefore be understated, consciously or semi-consciously, because realistic estimates would make the project appear less competitive.

Bent Flyvbjerg and colleagues describe this as **strategic misrepresentation**: forecasts become advocacy documents rather than neutral predictions. Rather than asking "What is most likely?", decision-makers begin asking "What estimate is most likely to secure approval?"<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1057/9780230236837_8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer+2Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Optimism and Misrepresentation in Early Project Developmentby B Flyvbjerg · Cited by 71 — This chapter identifies optimism bias a...</span></span></span>

The distinction is analytically important.

If inaccurate forecasts result primarily from technical limitations, the solution is better forecasting methods.

If they result primarily from incentive problems, technical improvements alone cannot solve them because the underlying reward structure remains unchanged.

## What transport cost-overrun data suggests

Transport infrastructure has become the best-studied field for examining forecasting bias because many comparable projects exist across different countries.

Large datasets assembled by Flyvbjerg and collaborators found substantial average cost overruns across rail, bridge, tunnel and road projects, with rail projects exhibiting particularly large overruns on average. More importantly, the pattern remained remarkably stable over many decades, despite improvements in forecasting techniques.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://documents.worldbank.org/curated/en/968761468141298118/pdf/wps3781.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: documents.worldbank.org">[World Bank+2Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">documents.worldbank.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">•. The persistence of these findings weakens the claim that inaccurate forecasts are mainly the product of immature forecasting methods. If technical capability were the dominant cause, one would expect forecasting accuracy to improve steadily over time.

Instead, the evidence suggests that institutional behaviour changes much more slowly than forecasting technology.

More recent reviews have also argued against reducing every cost overrun to optimism bias alone. Reviews published in 2023 note that although optimism bias clearly contributes to inaccurate estimates, its interaction with organisational incentives, governance structures and political pressures remains insufficiently understood. Rather than treating every inaccurate forecast as a psychological mistake, current research increasingly examines how behavioural and institutional mechanisms reinforce one another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/87569728231180268" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[Sage Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The article identifies...Read more...</span></span></span>

## How funding contests distort early forecasts

Competitive funding creates several predictable pressures.

**Selection effects.** Among many proposed projects, the ones with the most attractive benefit-cost ratios naturally receive greater attention. If forecasts contain uncertainty, the projects with the largest underestimates of cost are also more likely to appear attractive enough to be approved.

**Political timing.** Decision-makers often seek approval before uncertainty has been fully resolved. Early estimates therefore receive greater political attention than later, more accurate estimates.

**Escalation after approval.** Once substantial political commitment has been made, revising estimates upwards becomes easier than cancelling the project entirely. Costs therefore tend to rise after approval rather than preventing approval in the first place.

These mechanisms do not require widespread dishonesty. Even without explicit deception, organisations learn which styles of forecasting are rewarded. Over time, optimistic [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}) become embedded in routine [practice]({{ 'practice/' | relative_url }}) because they improve the probability of obtaining resources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://documents.worldbank.org/curated/en/968761468141298118/pdf/wps3781.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: documents.worldbank.org">[World Bank+2Project Management Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">documents.worldbank.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">•. ## Why optimism can be individually rational but socially harmful

A useful systems insight is that behaviour benefiting one participant may damage the wider decision-making system.

For an individual project sponsor, presenting an optimistic estimate may increase the chance of winning funding.

For the funding system as a whole, however, repeated optimism produces distorted priorities.

The consequences include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* projects entering construction without adequate contingency;
* budgets that systematically underestimate future public expenditure;
* reduced trust in official forecasts;
* fewer resources available for alternative projects that may have offered better value under realistic assumptions.

</div>

Viewed this way, persistent cost overruns are not merely forecasting failures. They become allocation failures: resources are directed using systematically biased information.

## Improving judgement through the outside view

One practical response is to replace purely project-specific forecasting with **reference class forecasting**, sometimes called taking the "outside view".

Instead of asking only how the current project differs from previous ones, analysts begin by examining how similar completed projects actually performed. Historical base rates provide an external benchmark against which optimistic assumptions can be tested.

The United Kingdom incorporated this idea into public project appraisal guidance through optimism-bias adjustments in the Green Book, recommending evidence-based uplifts to estimated costs and schedules when robust project-specific evidence is lacking. The intention is not to assume every project will fail equally badly, but to counter the predictable tendency for early estimates to be overly favourable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/green-book-supplementary-guidance-optimism-bias" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK+2GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Green Book supplementary guidance: optimism bias</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 21, 2013 — 21 Apr 2013 — This guidance provides cost and time uplift percentages for generic project categories which should be use...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 21, 2013</span></span></span>

For analytical thinking, the wider lesson extends beyond transport projects. Whenever forecasts determine which proposals receive scarce resources, ask not only whether the estimate appears technically sound, but also whether the surrounding incentive structure rewards realism or rewards optimism. That question often explains persistent forecasting failures better than the forecast itself.
