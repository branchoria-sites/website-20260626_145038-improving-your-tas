## Introduction

A plain-English explanation becomes genuinely useful when it does more than state that one thing influences another. It shows the missing middle: the sequence of changes that connects a cause to its effect. This is the essence of a causal-chain explanation. Instead of saying, “[Stress]({{ 'stress/' | relative_url }}) reduces performance,” a stronger explanation identifies what changes first, what changes next, and why those intermediate steps matter. Building these chains is one of the most reliable ways to test your own understanding because it forces you to replace vague associations with a concrete mechanism. Research on the illusion of explanatory depth shows that people often believe they understand a process until they are asked to [explain]({{ 'explain/' | relative_url }}) its causal steps in detail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3062901/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by L Rozenblit · 2002 · Cited by 1504 — We argue that the illusion of depth seen with explanatory knowledge is a separate phenomenon f...</span></span></span>

Within the broader [practice]({{ 'practice/' | relative_url }}) of explaining ideas in plain English, causal chains answer a specific question: *Can you show how the outcome happens, rather than merely asserting that it does?*

## How causal gaps appear in explanations

Many explanations sound convincing because they connect two ideas with words such as *causes*, *leads to*, *affects*, or *results in*. Those words often hide a missing mechanism.

Compare these explanations:

* **Weak:** "Exercise improves mood."
* **Stronger:** "Exercise raises heart rate and stimulates the release of signalling chemicals associated with reward and stress regulation. It also improves sleep and creates repeated experiences of competence, all of which can contribute to improved mood over time."

The second explanation is not simply longer. It identifies intermediate changes instead of treating cause and effect as adjacent events.

The same pattern appears in everyday reasoning:

* "Higher prices reduce demand."
* "[Feedback]({{ 'feedback/' | relative_url }}) improves learning."
* "Competition increases innovation."

Each statement may often be true, but none explains *why*. Until the intermediate steps are supplied, the explanation remains incomplete.

Research on the illusion of explanatory depth demonstrates that this is exactly where [overconfidence]({{ 'overconfidence/' | relative_url }}) appears. People frequently believe they understand mechanisms because they recognise familiar labels or outcomes. Only when asked to explain each step do they discover that several links in the chain are missing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3062901/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by L Rozenblit · 2002 · Cited by 1504 — We argue that the illusion of depth seen with explanatory knowledge is a separate phenomenon f...</span></span></span>

A useful warning sign is any explanation that could replace the words "causes" or "affects" with "somehow". If the sentence still sounds grammatically acceptable, the mechanism may not yet be understood.

## Turning abstractions into step-by-step examples

The simplest way to improve an explanation is to replace a direct arrow with a sequence of smaller changes.

Instead of writing:

> Lack of sleep reduces concentration.

Expand it into a causal chain:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Sleep is shortened.
2. Fatigue increases.
3. Sustained attention becomes harder.
4. More distractions interrupt working memory.
5. Mistakes become more frequent.
6. Performance on concentration-heavy tasks declines.

</div>

Every step should answer the question, "What changed between the previous stage and the next?"

The same approach works for abstract concepts.

### Example: Why does compound interest become powerful?

A weak explanation says:

> Compound interest makes savings grow faster.

A causal-chain explanation shows the mechanism:<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/causal-explanation-science/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: plato.stanford.edu">[plato.stanford.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">plato.stanford.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">causal explanation science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">For a discussion of earlier accounts of explanationRead more...</span></span></span>


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--example" markdown="1">

* Interest is added to the original savings.
* Future interest is calculated on both the original savings and previously earned interest.
* The amount generating interest therefore becomes larger after each period.
* As the base grows, each future increase becomes larger than the last.

</div>

The explanation has shifted from a slogan to a process.

### Example: Why does misinformation spread quickly?

Rather than saying:<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://jonwilliamson.uk/documents/ECitSS.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jonwilliamson.uk">[jonwilliamson.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jonwilliamson.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">s a tool that helps us to predict, explain and control our world, rather than as a relation.Read more...</span></span></span>

> Social media spreads misinformation.

A mechanism-based explanation might be:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--example" markdown="1">

* A surprising claim attracts attention.
* More users react and share it.
* Recommendation systems detect high engagement.
* The content is shown to more people.
* Greater exposure creates additional sharing.
* The cycle repeats before careful verification catches up.

</div>

Notice that each stage describes an observable change. The explanation becomes testable because each link could, in principle, be examined separately.

## Building explanations one link at a time

A useful discipline is to imagine that every arrow in a diagram must be replaced with a sentence.

Instead of:

> A → B

Ask:

* What changes first?
* What does that change directly affect?
* Why does that produce the next change?
* Which step depends on [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}) rather than evidence?

Often one sentence becomes four or five.

For example:

> Better education increases economic growth.

might become:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* People acquire more knowledge and skills.
* Workers perform tasks more efficiently.
* Firms can produce more output with similar resources.
* Higher productivity supports greater incomes.
* Greater income contributes to overall economic growth.

</div>

Whether every link is equally strong is a separate question. The important point is that the reasoning has become visible instead of remaining hidden inside a single verb.

This mirrors how mechanistic explanations are treated in many scientific disciplines: understanding improves by identifying the intermediate processes that connect an intervention to an outcome, rather than treating cause and effect as isolated facts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/causal-explanation-science/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: plato.stanford.edu">[Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">plato.stanford.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">causal explanation science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">For a discussion of earlier accounts of explanationRead more...</span></span></span>

## Where to stop without oversimplifying

A common concern is that every explanation could be expanded indefinitely. That is true. Every mechanism eventually contains smaller mechanisms.

The goal is therefore not to explain everything. It is to explain enough that each step feels understandable without requiring another unexplained leap.

A practical stopping point is reached when:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* each transition follows naturally from the previous one;
* specialised terms have either been avoided or briefly explained;
* no step depends on "and then it happens";
* the remaining details belong to a lower level of explanation than your audience currently needs.

</div>

For example, explaining why a bridge stays upright usually requires discussing forces and load distribution. It does not require deriving the equations of material science unless the audience is studying engineering.

Good explanations therefore match the depth of the reader while preserving the integrity of the causal chain.

## Common mistakes when explaining mechanisms

Several habits create the illusion of explanation without providing one.

**Replacing mechanisms with labels.** Saying "because of motivation", "because of inflation", or "because of neural activity" often substitutes a category name for an explanation. The next question should be, "How does that produce the outcome?"

**Skipping invisible transitions.** Many explanations jump from an initial event to a distant consequence, leaving several causal links implicit.

**Confusing correlation with mechanism.** Two variables may regularly occur together without one producing the other. Showing a causal chain requires explaining the process, not merely reporting the association.

**Packing multiple changes into one sentence.** Long explanations sometimes hide several independent causal steps inside a single clause. Separating them often reveals assumptions that deserve closer examination.

## Using causal chains as a thinking test

When [teaching]({{ 'teaching/' | relative_url }}) yourself or someone else, a simple exercise can reveal hidden gaps.

Choose one statement you believe you understand, such as:

> "Remote working increases productivity."

Then repeatedly ask:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* What changes immediately?
* What changes because of that?
* What happens next?
* Which link is weakest?
* Could another mechanism produce the same outcome?

</div>

If you reach a point where you can only repeat the original claim in different words, you have probably found the boundary of your understanding rather than the end of the explanation.

This habit aligns closely with research on explanatory understanding.
