## Introduction

Some problems remain difficult not because the facts are unknown, but because people cannot agree on what the problem actually is. This is a defining feature of many “wicked problems” — complex social, organisational and public-policy challenges where different groups hold different values, priorities and interpretations of reality. In these situations, disagreement begins before solutions are discussed. Stakeholders may frame the same situation as a safety issue, a fairness issue, an economic issue, a rights issue or a governance issue, with each framing implying a different path forward. Rittel and Webber’s foundational work on wicked problems highlighted that there is no definitive formulation of such problems and that different explanations lead to different responses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://urbanpolicy.net/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/Rittel-Webber_1973_DilemmasInAGeneralTheoryOfPlanning.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: urbanpolicy.net">[Urban Policy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">urbanpolicy.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Rittel Webber 1973 DilemmasInAGeneralTheoryOfPlanning</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">160. For anyone seeking to improve their thinking and analytical skills, this matters because reasoning quality depends heavily on how the problem is framed. If stakeholders are arguing about different versions of the problem, evidence alone rarely resolves the conflict.

## Why Wicked Problems Resist Final Wording

Many technical problems can be defined with reasonable precision. A machine either fails to meet a specification or it does not. Wicked problems are different because their boundaries, causes and goals are themselves disputed.

Rittel and Webber argued that there is no definitive formulation of a wicked problem. The way a problem is described depends on which stakeholders are consulted, which consequences are emphasised and which values are treated as important. In [practice]({{ 'practice/' | relative_url }}), defining the problem is inseparable from proposing a solution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://urbanpolicy.net/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/Rittel-Webber_1973_DilemmasInAGeneralTheoryOfPlanning.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: urbanpolicy.net">[Urban Policy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">urbanpolicy.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Rittel Webber 1973 DilemmasInAGeneralTheoryOfPlanning</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">160. III. Planning Problems are Wicked Problems. A great many barriers keep us from...Read more...</span></span></span>

Consider a city facing rising housing costs. Different stakeholders might frame the issue as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A shortage of housing supply.
* Excessive speculation and investment.
* A failure of wage growth.
* An environmental sustainability challenge.
* A planning and zoning problem.
* A social justice issue.

</div>

Each framing highlights some facts and downplays others. None is necessarily false, but each creates a different decision agenda.

Research on wicked problems consistently emphasises that stakeholder perceptions, interests and values shape how issues are scoped and prioritised. The conflict is not merely about evidence; it is about which interpretation deserves to guide action.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/policyandsociety/article/38/2/180/6407919" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic+2Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicForty years of wicked problems literature: forging closer links...by BW Head · 2019 · Cited by 740 — The wicked problems per...</span></span></span>

This explains why debates over climate policy, urban development, healthcare reform or organisational change often appear to stall. Participants may seem to be discussing solutions while actually disagreeing about the nature of the underlying problem.

## Values, Trade-Offs, and Disputed Success

A central reason for stakeholder conflict is that wicked problems contain value judgements that cannot be settled scientifically.

For many organisational and social issues, stakeholders care about different [outcomes]({{ 'outcomes/' | relative_url }}):


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* Efficiency versus equity.
* Security versus privacy.
* Economic growth versus environmental protection.
* Short-term stability versus long-term resilience.
* Individual freedom versus collective welfare.

</div>

These are not simply factual disagreements. They reflect competing views about what ought to matter.

The wicked-problems literature repeatedly notes that solutions are rarely judged as true or false. Instead, they are judged as better or worse according to different stakeholder values. Because people use different standards of evaluation, they can look at the same outcome and reach opposite conclusions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wicked_problem" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wicked problem</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wicked problem</span></span></span>

For example, a new public transport project may be celebrated by environmental advocates because it reduces emissions, criticised by local residents because it changes neighbourhood character, and questioned by taxpayers because of cost. The disagreement is not merely about transport engineering. It concerns what success should mean.

This is why attempts to produce a single “objective” definition of many social problems often fail. Stakeholders are not only disagreeing about facts; they are disagreeing about which goals deserve priority. Research on governance and systems thinking highlights that wicked problems commonly involve actors with conflicting interests and values, making consensus on problem definition inherently difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2020/02/systemic-thinking-for-policy-making_a95b3226/879c4f7a-en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oecd.org">[OECD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oecd.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Systemic Thinking for Policy Making (EN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Systemic Thinking for Policy Making (EN)May 15, 2025 — complex and ill-defined problems with multiple interacting actors, often with...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 15, 2025</span></span></span>

## How Frames Create Winners and Losers

[Problem framing]({{ 'problem-framing/' | relative_url }}) is not neutral. Every frame highlights certain interests and makes others less visible.

A useful way to understand stakeholder conflict is to ask three questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Who benefits if this framing becomes dominant?
2. Whose concerns become central?
3. Which alternatives become easier or harder to consider?

</div>

Suppose an organisation experiences declining employee morale.

If leaders frame the issue as a productivity problem, the response may focus on performance management and incentives. If employees frame it as a workload problem, attention may shift towards staffing levels and expectations. If human resources frames it as a culture problem, solutions may focus on leadership behaviour and communication.

The same observable situation can produce different interventions because each frame identifies a different root cause.

This dynamic is particularly important in governance contexts. Policy scholars note that pluralistic societies naturally contain multiple legitimate viewpoints. Wicked problems emerge partly because stakeholders possess different experiences, interests and moral commitments, making competing frames unavoidable rather than exceptional.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/policyandsociety/article/38/2/180/6407919" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicForty years of wicked problems literature: forging closer links...by BW Head · 2019 · Cited by 740 — The wicked problems per...</span></span></span>

Recognising this helps analysts avoid a common mistake: assuming that disagreement reflects ignorance. Often the disagreement reflects genuine differences in priorities and worldviews.

## How Framing Can Make Disagreement Visible

The goal is not always to force agreement on a single definition. In many cases, better thinking begins by making competing frames explicit.

Problem-structuring approaches were developed partly to help groups work with uncertainty and conflict before rushing into solution design. Rather than asking participants to agree immediately, these methods encourage them to surface [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}), concerns and interpretations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/283311585_Problem_Structuring_Methods" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate+2cci.drexel.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Problem Structuring MethodsProblem structuring methods (PSMs) are a broad group of model-based problem handling approac...</span></span></span>

Useful questions include:

* What problem do different stakeholders believe they are facing?
* What outcomes does each group consider successful?
* Which values are driving those preferences?
* What assumptions support each framing?
* What evidence would [change minds]({{ 'change-mind/' | relative_url }}), and what disagreements are fundamentally value-based?

Answering these questions often reveals that apparent factual disputes are actually conflicts over goals or priorities.

For example, a debate about whether a company should require office attendance may initially appear to concern productivity data. Closer examination may reveal competing values: flexibility, collaboration, autonomy, fairness, career development and organisational [identity]({{ 'identity/' | relative_url }}). Once those values are visible, the disagreement becomes easier to understand even if it is not fully resolved.

## Thinking Better When Frames Conflict

When faced with a wicked problem, analytical skill involves more than collecting evidence. It requires recognising that multiple stakeholders may be reasoning from different definitions of the situation.

Several habits improve judgement:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--checklist" markdown="1">

* **Separate factual disputes from value disputes.** Evidence can help with the former but not fully resolve the latter.
* **Treat problem definitions as hypotheses rather than facts.** Ask what assumptions each frame contains.
* **Look for excluded stakeholders.** Missing perspectives often reveal hidden dimensions of the problem.
* **Compare success criteria.** Different groups frequently disagree because they are measuring different outcomes.
* **Expect reframing over time.** As conditions change, stakeholders may redefine both the problem and the desired solution.

</div>

The key lesson is that wicked problems are often sustained by conflicts in framing rather than by a lack of intelligence or information. Better reasoning begins when analysts recognise that the first challenge is not choosing a solution.
