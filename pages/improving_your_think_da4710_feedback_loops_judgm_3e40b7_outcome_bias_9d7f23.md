## Introduction

A good decision can produce a bad result, and a poor decision can produce a good one. This simple fact is one of the biggest obstacles to improving judgement. When people evaluate decisions by their [outcomes]({{ 'outcomes/' | relative_url }}) instead of by the quality of the reasoning available at the time, they fall into **outcome bias**. The consequence is a distorted [feedback loop]({{ 'feedback-loop/' | relative_url }}): luck gets mistaken for skill, while bad luck is mistaken for poor judgement. Over time, this encourages risky habits that happened to succeed and discourages careful thinking that happened to fail. Classic psychological research first demonstrated this effect experimentally, and more recent replication studies show that it remains a robust feature of human judgement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3367280/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Outcome bias in decision evaluation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Outcome bias in decision evaluation - PubMed - NIHby J Baron · 1988 · Cited by 1449 — In 5 studies, undergraduate subjects were giv...</span></span></span>

Within a [feedback]({{ 'feedback/' | relative_url }}) system, the central question is therefore not merely, *"Did this work?"* but *"Given what was known beforehand, was this a sound decision?"* Separating process quality from outcome quality creates feedback that teaches rather than merely rewards or punishes.

## Why results can mislead reviews

Outcomes contain two ingredients: the quality of the decision and the role of chance. Reviews become misleading when they assume the outcome reflects only the first.

Suppose two investors analyse identical information and make equally well-supported decisions. One benefits from an unexpected market rally, while the other is hit by an unforeseen geopolitical shock. If their reasoning was equally sound, the difference in returns says little about who made the better decision. Yet observers—and often the decision-makers themselves—naturally treat the winner as more competent.

Jonathan Baron and John Hershey demonstrated this tendency in a series of influential experiments. Participants evaluated medical and financial decisions under uncertainty. Although the information available to the decision-maker remained identical, merely changing the eventual outcome caused participants to judge the quality of the original decision differently. Positive outcomes led to higher ratings of competence and reasoning, while negative outcomes led to harsher evaluations—even when participants explicitly recognised that outcomes should not matter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3367280/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Outcome bias in decision evaluation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Outcome bias in decision evaluation - PubMed - NIHby J Baron · 1988 · Cited by 1449 — In 5 studies, undergraduate subjects were giv...</span></span></span>

A large pre-registered replication published in 2023 found the same basic effect using modern methods and a substantially larger sample. Participants continued to judge identical decisions differently depending on whether the outcome was favourable or unfavourable, confirming that outcome bias remains a reliable feature of human evaluation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rips-irsp.com/articles/10.5334/irsp.751" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rips-irsp.com">[rips-irsp.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rips-irsp.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Outcomes Affect Evaluations of Decision Qualityby S Aiyer · 2023 · Cited by 4 — Research by Baron and Hershey (1988) demonstrated that ou...</span></span></span>

The practical danger is that noisy environments produce misleading lessons:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A reckless strategy that succeeds once appears brilliant.
* A cautious strategy that fails because of rare bad luck appears foolish.
* Organisations begin rewarding visible success rather than disciplined reasoning.
* Individuals become overconfident after lucky wins and excessively cautious after unlucky losses.

</div>

In environments such as investing, medicine, business strategy or public policy, where uncertainty is unavoidable, this distortion can accumulate into systematic errors.

## The four-cell process and outcome check

A useful review separates **decision quality** from **outcome quality**. Rather than treating success and failure as a single scale, it helps to classify decisions into four possibilities.

Decision processOutcomeInterpretationGood processGood outcomeDesired result, but success should not automatically validate every detail of the reasoning.Good processBad outcomeOften bad luck. The process may still deserve to be repeated in similar circumstances.Poor processGood outcomeLucky success. This is the most dangerous cell because it encourages repeating flawed reasoning.Poor processBad outcomeBoth the reasoning and the result require correction.

The most valuable learning often comes from the two mixed cases.

A **good process with a bad outcome** asks whether the uncertainty was recognised beforehand. If the risk was known and acceptable, the decision may still have been correct. For example, a surgeon choosing the treatment with the highest probability of success cannot guarantee survival in every individual case.

A **poor process with a good outcome** is more deceptive. Someone who ignored evidence, skipped analysis or relied on [intuition]({{ 'intuition/' | relative_url }}) may still succeed because chance happened to favour them. Without careful review, that success reinforces precisely the habits that should be changed.

The four-cell approach encourages reviewers to ask two separate questions:

1. Was the reasoning appropriate given the information available at the time?
2. How much of the outcome was influenced by uncertainty beyond the decision-maker's control?

Only after answering both questions should performance be evaluated.

## How outcome bias weakens learning

Outcome bias damages learning because it corrupts the feedback signal that experience provides.

Instead of identifying which thinking patterns consistently produce better decisions, people begin associating emotional reactions with isolated outcomes. A painful failure creates the illusion that the underlying reasoning was flawed. An exciting success creates the illusion that every judgement leading to it was insightful.

This produces several recurring problems:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* **False confidence.** Lucky successes increase confidence without increasing skill.
* **False self-doubt.** Sound reasoning abandoned after an unlucky outcome may never demonstrate its long-term value.
* **Inconsistent standards.** Identical decisions receive praise or criticism depending solely on events outside the decision-maker's control.
* **Poor experimentation.** Teams become reluctant to test sensible innovations if any unsuccessful trial is treated as evidence of incompetence.

</div>

The distortion is particularly severe in low-feedback environments where important decisions are rare and outcomes unfold slowly. A single visible success or failure may dominate memory despite containing little information about the underlying quality of judgement.

## How to reward reasoning without ignoring results

Avoiding outcome bias does not mean ignoring results. Outcomes remain essential because they reveal whether [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}) matched reality. The challenge is to use outcomes as evidence rather than as the sole measure of decision quality.

Several practices help preserve this distinction.

**Record reasoning before acting.** [Decision journals]({{ 'decision-journals/' | relative_url }}) capture assumptions, forecasts, confidence levels and expected risks before hindsight can reshape memory. Later reviews compare the original reasoning with what actually happened rather than relying on reconstructed recollections.

**Judge decisions using information available at the time.** Reviewers should deliberately ignore facts that emerged only after the decision. This recreates the original uncertainty and produces a fairer assessment.

**Look for repeated patterns rather than isolated wins or losses.** A single outcome is often dominated by randomness. Consistent performance across many decisions provides much stronger evidence of skill.

**Reward disciplined processes as well as successful outcomes.** Organisations that recognise careful analysis, explicit assumptions and thoughtful risk management reduce the temptation to chase lucky victories.

**Separate accountability from luck.** People remain responsible for the quality of their reasoning, preparation and risk management, but not for every random event that occurs afterwards.

These practices align incentives with long-term judgement rather than short-term fortune.

## The balance between process and results

Focusing on process alone would also be a mistake. A beautifully reasoned decision that repeatedly produces poor outcomes eventually signals that important assumptions are wrong. Outcomes contain valuable information; they simply cannot be interpreted in isolation.

The most reliable feedback loop therefore combines both perspectives. Process reviews ask whether the reasoning was sound when the decision was made. Outcome reviews ask whether repeated experience suggests the model itself needs revision. Neither question replaces the other.

By consistently distinguishing **skill from luck** and **reasoning from results**, decision-makers avoid rewarding fortunate [mistakes]({{ 'mistakes/' | relative_url }}) or punishing sensible risk-taking. That distinction is one of the foundations of better judgement in any uncertain environment.
