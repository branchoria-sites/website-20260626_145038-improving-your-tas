## Introduction

A decision review trigger is a pre-agreed rule that specifies *when* a decision must be revisited and *what kind of evidence* could justify changing course. Within the broader practice of asking “What would [change my mind]({{ 'change-mind/' | relative_url }})?”, review triggers turn an abstract commitment to [open-mindedness]({{ 'open-mindedness/' | relative_url }}) into an operational governance process. Rather than waiting until a project has clearly failed—or reacting to every temporary setback—they create predictable moments for reassessment based on evidence instead of emotion.

This matters because people and organisations often become trapped by previous investments of time, money, effort or reputation. Research on escalation of commitment shows that decision-makers frequently continue supporting a struggling course of action even after receiving negative [feedback]({{ 'feedback/' | relative_url }}), partly because abandoning it feels like admitting failure rather than making a better forward-looking decision.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5904751/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2Project Management Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A New Inaction-Effect Perspective on the Sunk-Cost Fallacyby G Feldman · 2018 · Cited by 66 — Escalation-of-commitment situations invo...</span></span></span>

## How review triggers reduce commitment traps

The central purpose of a review trigger is to separate **evaluating the original decision** from **evaluating today's best option**.

Without predefined triggers, reviews often happen only when someone becomes concerned enough to challenge the existing plan. By then, discussions can become personal. Team members may defend earlier decisions because they made them, because others expect consistency, or because substantial resources have already been invested.

A review trigger changes the conversation. Instead of asking:

> "Should we admit we were wrong?"

the agreed question becomes:

> "Has the agreed review condition been met?"

This shift reduces self-justification and makes changing course appear as following the process rather than abandoning a commitment. Organisational research has repeatedly found that external oversight and separating initial decisions from later review decisions can reduce escalation of commitment because they weaken personal ownership of the original choice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psychology.northwestern.edu/documents/faculty-publications/molden-%20hui_2011.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psychology.northwestern.edu">[Northwestern Psychology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psychology.northwestern.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psychology Promoting De-Escalation of Commitment: A Regulatory</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Northwestern PsychologyPromoting De-Escalation of Commitment: A Regulatory-...January 5, 2011 — by DC Molden · 2011 · Cited by 117 — Sol...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 5, 2011</span></span></span>

## Choosing meaningful metrics

Review triggers are only useful if they rely on measures that genuinely reflect whether the underlying [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}) remain true.

Good review metrics have three characteristics:

* They relate directly to the decision's expected [outcomes]({{ 'outcomes/' | relative_url }}).

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* They are observable rather than subjective.
* They can deteriorate before complete failure becomes obvious.

</div>

For example, a team launching a new software feature might agree in advance to review the rollout if:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* weekly active usage remains below an agreed target;
* customer support requests exceed a defined level;
* critical defects remain unresolved beyond a specified period; or
* infrastructure costs exceed expected limits without corresponding user growth.

</div>

The important feature is that these indicators were chosen **before** knowing whether the decision would succeed. That reduces the temptation to redefine success after disappointing results.

Poor review metrics often measure effort rather than progress. Statements such as "the team is working hard" or "we've already invested six months" describe commitment, not evidence that continuing is worthwhile.

## Setting thresholds and time windows

A useful trigger combines **what** will be measured with **how much change** is sufficient to require a review and **when** that judgement will be made.

Thresholds prevent constant second-guessing while avoiding indefinite persistence.

Examples include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* customer retention falls below 80% for two consecutive months;
* project costs exceed the approved budget by 15%;
* recruitment targets remain unmet after one quarter;
* a policy fails to achieve its minimum outcome by the first annual evaluation.

</div>

Time windows are equally important.

Reviewing too early may mistake normal variation for failure. Waiting too long increases the risk of additional [sunk costs]({{ 'sunk-costs/' | relative_url }}). Different decisions naturally require different review periods:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* operational experiments may justify weekly reviews;
* product launches may need monthly or quarterly reviews;
* strategic investments often require milestone-based reviews rather than continuous reconsideration.

</div>

Combining thresholds with minimum observation periods helps distinguish genuine trends from random fluctuations. Instead of reacting to one disappointing week, the organisation asks whether performance has crossed the agreed boundary over the agreed period.

## Assigning review actions and owners

A trigger should specify not only **when** a review occurs but also **who** conducts it and **what happens next**.

Without predefined ownership, review meetings are easily postponed or become exercises in defending existing plans.

A practical governance rule normally specifies:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the individual or committee responsible for initiating the review;
* the evidence to be gathered before discussion;
* who has authority to recommend continuation, modification or termination;
* whether additional approval is needed before significant new resources are committed.

</div>

Many organisations deliberately separate implementation from review. The people responsible for delivering a project possess valuable operational knowledge, but an independent reviewer or steering group may be less vulnerable to self-justification because their own reputation is less closely tied to the original decision. Research on de-escalation strategies consistently identifies independent oversight and divided responsibility as mechanisms that reduce commitment bias.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psychology.northwestern.edu/documents/faculty-publications/molden-%20hui_2011.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psychology.northwestern.edu">[Northwestern Psychology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psychology.northwestern.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psychology Promoting De-Escalation of Commitment: A Regulatory</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Northwestern PsychologyPromoting De-Escalation of Commitment: A Regulatory-...January 5, 2011 — by DC Molden · 2011 · Cited by 117 — Sol...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 5, 2011</span></span></span>

## Reviews should create options, not only binary choices

One misconception is that crossing a review threshold means a project automatically stops.

More often, the review creates a structured choice among several future actions, for example:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* continue unchanged because assumptions still hold;
* continue with revised scope;
* pause while key uncertainties are resolved;
* conduct another limited experiment;
* terminate the initiative and redirect resources.

</div>

This keeps the review focused on future expected value rather than on judging past decisions. The objective is not to prove that someone was wrong but to determine which option now offers the strongest evidence-supported path forward.

## Common design mistakes

Even well-intentioned review systems can fail if the triggers are poorly designed.

Frequent problems include:

* **Vague triggers.** "Review if things are not going well" leaves too much room for interpretation.
* **Moving thresholds.** Lowering standards after disappointing results removes the discipline that review triggers are intended to create.
* **Too many indicators.** Excessive metrics allow decision-makers to ignore the few that genuinely matter.
* **Ignoring opportunity costs.** Reviews should ask not only whether the current project can still succeed, but whether available alternatives now offer greater expected value.
* **Treating reviews as blame sessions.** If every review becomes an investigation into personal responsibility, people become less willing to surface negative information early.

Research on escalation of commitment suggests that reputational concerns can themselves encourage persistence. People may continue with weak decisions because consistency is interpreted as trustworthiness or reliability. ## Making "What would change my mind?" operational

The question "What would change my mind?" becomes substantially more powerful when paired with formal review triggers.

Instead of relying on memory or good intentions, the decision-maker specifies in advance:

* which assumptions are most important;
* which metrics indicate those assumptions remain valid;
* what thresholds require reconsideration;
* when reviews occur;
* who conducts them; and
* which actions are available [after review]({{ 'after-review/' | relative_url }}).

This transforms intellectual openness into a repeatable governance [practice]({{ 'practice/' | relative_url }}). Review triggers preserve consistency by preventing constant reversals, while also preventing consistency from becoming an excuse for remaining committed to decisions that no longer fit the evidence.
