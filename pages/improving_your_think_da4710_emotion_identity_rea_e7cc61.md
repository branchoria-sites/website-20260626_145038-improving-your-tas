## Introduction

Strong feelings do not simply “get in the way” of thinking; they often decide which facts feel relevant, which sources feel trustworthy, and which conclusions feel safe to consider. Under pressure, the problem is sharper: anger, fear, humiliation, loyalty and defensiveness can narrow attention just when careful judgement needs room. Identity makes this harder still. When a claim threatens someone’s group, values, reputation or self-image, evidence can be treated less like information and more like a social test. Research on motivated reasoning, [identity-protective]({{ 'identity/' | relative_url }}) cognition, affect, [stress]({{ 'stress/' | relative_url }}) and misinformation points to a practical lesson: better reasoning is not just about knowing logic, but about recognising when emotion has changed the task from “What is true?” to “What protects me, my side, or my story?”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fbaum.unc.edu/teaching/articles/Psych-Bulletin-1990-Kunda.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unc.edu">[Frank Baumgartner+2ndg.asc.upenn.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unc.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Frank Baumgartner The Case for Motivated Reasoning</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Frank BaumgartnerThe Case for Motivated ReasoningOctober 6, 2004 — by Z Kunda · 1990 · Cited by 13553 — In this article I explore the pos...</span></span></span>

## Why emotional pressure changes the question

Emotion can improve judgement when it draws attention to real stakes: fear can flag danger, [anger]({{ 'anger/' | relative_url }}) can register injustice, disgust can warn of contamination, and anxiety can make people check details. The mistake is to treat emotion as either pure wisdom or pure noise. A major review of emotion and decision-making argues that emotions are “potent, pervasive, predictable” influences on choices, sometimes helpful and sometimes harmful, depending on whether the feeling fits the actual problem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scispace.com/pdf/emotion-and-decision-making-25g5wm23rq.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scispace.com">[SciSpace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scispace.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sci Space Emotion and Decision Making</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Emotion and Decision MakingAbstract. The risk begins when a feeling becomes a shortcut for evidence. The “affect heuristic” describes how people use the positive or negative feeling attached to a person, policy, product or claim as a fast guide to its risk and benefit. If something feels good, its risks may seem smaller; if it feels threatening, its benefits may disappear from view. This is efficient in simple situations, but unreliable when the issue is complex, politicised or unfamiliar.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00970/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Frontiers The Affect Heuristic and Risk Perception – Stability Across</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Frontiers The Affect Heuristic and Risk Perception – Stability Across</span></span></span>

A useful example is public risk perception. People rarely evaluate a hazard by calmly listing [probabilities]({{ 'probabilities/' | relative_url }}), uncertainties and [trade-offs]({{ 'trade-offs/' | relative_url }}). They often start with a gut tag: safe, dangerous, corrupt, natural, artificial, elite, ordinary, familiar, alien. Once that tag is attached, later facts may be interpreted through it. The same evidence can feel reassuring to one person and manipulative to another, not because the evidence has changed, but because the emotional meaning of the topic has changed.

For analytical thinking, this means the first self-check is not “Am I emotional?” Everyone is. The better question is: “What is this emotion making obvious, and what is it making hard to see?”

## Emotional triggers that distort evidence

Emotional triggers matter because they do not merely add intensity; they change attention, memory and standards of proof. A person may demand impossibly strong evidence for a claim they dislike while accepting weak evidence for a claim that relieves anxiety or confirms blame. That is not always conscious dishonesty. Motivated reasoning often works through ordinary mental operations: searching memory, judging credibility, spotting flaws, and deciding when enough evidence is enough. Kunda’s classic review described motivation as shaping reasoning through a biased set of cognitive processes rather than through simple wishful thinking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fbaum.unc.edu/teaching/articles/Psych-Bulletin-1990-Kunda.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unc.edu">[Frank Baumgartner]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unc.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Frank Baumgartner The Case for Motivated Reasoning</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Frank BaumgartnerThe Case for Motivated ReasoningOctober 6, 2004 — by Z Kunda · 1990 · Cited by 13553 — In this article I explore the pos...</span></span></span>

Three triggers are especially important in reasoning under pressure.

**Threat** narrows the field. When people feel attacked, rushed or unsafe, they often prioritise immediate defence over open evaluation. Neuroscience reviews of stress find that even mild uncontrollable stress can impair prefrontal cortex functions involved in working memory, flexible thinking and top-down control. In everyday terms, the mind has less spare capacity for holding competing possibilities at once.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2907136/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCStress signalling pathways that impair prefrontal cortex</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by AFT Arnsten · 2009 · Cited by 5202 — The Review first describes how exposure to even mild uncontrollable stress can rapidly impair...</span></span></span>

**Anger** can increase certainty. Anger often carries the appraisal that someone is responsible and that action is justified. That can be useful when responsibility is clear, but it can also make blame feel like proof. In organisational decision-making, researchers warn that anger and other high-certainty emotions can push people towards action before they have thought enough, making cooling-off periods valuable for major decisions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://jenniferlerner.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/emotion-in-organizational-judgment.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jenniferlerner.com">[jenniferlerner.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jenniferlerner.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Emotion in organizational judgment and decision making</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Emotion in organizational judgment and decision making</span></span></span>

**Humiliation and status threat** make revision costly. If changing your mind means admitting that your group was wrong, your profession missed something, your public statement was premature, or your opponent had a point, the evidence is no longer just evidence. It becomes a demand for social or personal loss. This is where intellectual humility becomes hardest: not when a topic is abstract, but when the conclusion touches pride, belonging or reputation.

## Identity-protective thinking

Identity-protective thinking is motivated reasoning aimed at preserving a valued identity. The identity might be political, religious, professional, national, moral, class-based, scientific, sceptical, activist, entrepreneurial or personal. The core pattern is the same: [claims]({{ 'claims/' | relative_url }}) aligned with the identity receive easier passage; claims that threaten it face heavier inspection. Dan Kahan’s work on identity-protective cognition has been especially influential in showing how people can use reasoning ability not to escape bias, but to defend identity-consistent conclusions more skilfully.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ndg.asc.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/08/Ideology-motivated-reasoning.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ndg.asc.upenn.edu">[ndg.asc.upenn.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ndg.asc.upenn.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ideology motivated reasoning</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ideology, motivated reasoning, and cognitive reflectionby DM Kahan · 2013 · Cited by 2114 — When in- dividuals display identity-protectiv...</span></span></span>

This is why “just give people more facts” often disappoints. On identity-loaded questions, people may not be short of information. They may be defending a social position. Kahan’s research on ideology, motivated reasoning and cognitive reflection challenged the simple idea that polarisation is mainly caused by people relying too little on analytical thought. In some contexts, more cognitively reflective or numerate people were better at reaching identity-congenial interpretations of evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ndg.asc.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/08/Ideology-motivated-reasoning.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ndg.asc.upenn.edu">[ndg.asc.upenn.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ndg.asc.upenn.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ideology motivated reasoning</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ideology, motivated reasoning, and cognitive reflectionby DM Kahan · 2013 · Cited by 2114 — When in- dividuals display identity-protectiv...</span></span></span>

This does not mean intelligence makes people irrational. It means reasoning has more than one possible goal. One goal is accuracy: forming the belief best supported by evidence. Another is identity protection: maintaining standing with people whose respect, trust or shared worldview matters. From the inside, identity-protective reasoning can feel responsible: “I am not being biased; I am refusing to be fooled by hostile people.” The danger is that the same scepticism is not applied evenly.

A practical sign of identity-protective thinking is asymmetry. Ask:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Would the same evidence impress me if it supported the other side?
* Would I accept this source if it criticised my group?
* Am I treating a single bad argument from opponents as proof that their whole case is false?
* Am I more interested in whether a claim is useful to my side than whether it is accurate?
* Does changing my mind feel like betrayal rather than correction?

</div>

These questions are uncomfortable because they move the issue from abstract reasoning to social risk. That discomfort is often the point at which better thinking begins.

## Pressure turns reasoning into self-defence

Under pressure, people have less time, less working memory and less emotional tolerance for ambiguity. Time pressure can make the first plausible explanation feel good enough. Social pressure adds another layer: people may reason not only for themselves, but for an imagined audience. “How will this sound?” can quietly replace “Is this true?”

Stress research helps explain the mechanism. The prefrontal cortex supports planning, inhibition, working memory and flexible attention. Under stress, these systems can be disrupted, making habitual, emotionally salient or threat-focused responses more likely. This matters for analytical skill because many good reasoning moves are prefrontal-heavy: holding multiple hypotheses, delaying closure, considering base rates, separating evidence from interpretation, and noticing contradictions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2907136/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCStress signalling pathways that impair prefrontal cortex</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by AFT Arnsten · 2009 · Cited by 5202 — The Review first describes how exposure to even mild uncontrollable stress can rapidly impair...</span></span></span>

Pressure also changes what counts as a satisfying answer. In calm conditions, a person may tolerate “I do not know yet.” In a public argument, workplace crisis or family conflict, uncertainty can feel weak. A confident but poorly supported answer may become attractive because it restores control. This is one reason misinformation often uses urgency, outrage and moral accusation. It does not merely present a claim; it creates a state in which checking feels like delay, and delay feels like complicity. Reviews of misinformation psychology identify emotion, identity, repetition and source cues as major factors in belief and sharing, while prebunking research has focused partly on warning people about emotional manipulation before they encounter it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s44159-021-00006-y" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nature.com.</span></span></span>

The skill is not to become emotionally blank. It is to recognise the moment when pressure has changed the decision environment. A claim heard while calm, private and unhurried is not processed the same way as a claim heard while angry, watched and expected to respond immediately.

## When smarter thinking makes bias stronger

A common myth is that motivated reasoning is mainly a problem for ignorant or unintelligent people. The evidence is more awkward. Reasoning skill can reduce error when the person is genuinely trying to be accurate, but it can also supply better arguments for a preferred conclusion when identity is at stake. This is sometimes called “myside bias”: evaluating arguments partly by whether they support one’s existing side.

The important distinction is between **ability** and **direction**. Analytical ability gives a person more tools. It does not guarantee that the tools are aimed at truth. A highly skilled reasoner can notice flaws in hostile evidence, generate alternative explanations, find expert allies, reinterpret statistics, and frame uncertainty strategically. These are valuable skills when used fairly; they become defensive weapons when used only in one direction.

Recent philosophical work has also complicated the simple picture. Some scholars argue that not every case of identity-protective reasoning should be dismissed as sheer epistemic vice, because identities can sometimes preserve local knowledge, protect people from hostile institutions, or help communities resist misleading authority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/episteme/article/identityprotective-reasoning-an-epistemic-and-political-defense/5C62B4A3FC3F564ADE86BA5B7C5EF2BE" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

That caveat matters. The goal is not to shame people for caring about identity. Identity can carry legitimate experience, moral memory and social knowledge. The problem is unchecked identity protection: when group loyalty decides in advance which evidence may count. Good thinking therefore requires a double standard check, not an identity purge.

## Cooling-off checks

Cooling-off is not the same as avoidance. The aim is to create enough distance for accuracy goals to come back online. Evidence on psychological distancing suggests that taking a distanced perspective can reduce emotional intensity and help people regulate the influence of incidental fear, anger or distress on judgement. Meta-analytic work has found that psychological distance can attenuate emotional experience, though effects vary by emotion type and context.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8267060/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

A good cooling-off check is short, concrete and usable at the moment of pressure. It should interrupt the emotional momentum without pretending the feeling is irrelevant.

**Name the trigger.** Before answering, write one sentence: “The part of this that feels threatening is…” This separates the evidence from the identity alarm. If the sentence is hard to write, the threat may be doing more work than expected.

**Switch from verdict to question.** Replace “This is obviously wrong” with “What would I need to know for this to be wrong or partly right?” The wording matters because it moves the mind from defence to investigation.

**Use a same-standard test.** Ask whether the evidence would meet your standard if it came from your own side, and whether your preferred evidence would survive if offered by an opponent. This is one of the fastest ways to expose motivated asymmetry.

**Delay irreversible responses.** For major decisions, public posts, angry emails, disciplinary actions, resignations, purchases or accusations, a waiting period can prevent the emotional state from becoming the author of the decision. Organisational judgement research recommends cooling-off periods for major decisions because high-intensity emotions can create premature confidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://jenniferlerner.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/emotion-in-organizational-judgment.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jenniferlerner.com">[jenniferlerner.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jenniferlerner.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Emotion in organizational judgment and decision making</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Emotion in organizational judgment and decision making</span></span></span>

**Invite a role-based challenge.** Ask someone to argue the best version of the alternative explanation, not to “play devil’s advocate” lazily. The useful version is specific: “What is the strongest evidence that I am underweighting?”

**Pre-commit before the trigger.** Decide in advance what evidence would change your mind, what sources you would treat as credible, and which decisions require a pause. Pre-commitment works because it is made before the identity threat arrives.

## A practical example: reading a hostile article

Imagine reading an article that criticises a community, profession or political cause you identify with. The first reaction may be irritation: the author is biased, the examples are cherry-picked, the tone is unfair. Those objections may be correct. But they may also be serving as an exit ramp from evidence.

A disciplined reading would separate four questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Tone:** Is the article fair, sneering, careful or manipulative?
2. **Claim:** What exactly is it saying happened or is true?
3. **Evidence:** What documents, data, examples or expert sources support that claim?
4. **Scope:** Even if some examples are valid, how widely do they apply?

</div>

This separation matters because emotional reasoning often collapses the four. A hostile tone is treated as proof that the evidence is false. A flawed example is treated as proof that the whole pattern is invented. A disliked messenger is treated as proof that no claim needs checking. Better reasoning does not require trusting hostile sources; it requires not letting hostility alone decide the evidence question.

The same applies in reverse. A warm, identity-affirming article can be wrong. A familiar source can omit inconvenient facts. A claim that makes your side look wise can still need verification. Emotional comfort is not evidence quality.

## How to build pressure-resistant judgement

The most reliable protection is not a heroic act of willpower during crisis, but a reasoning environment designed before crisis. People think better when good habits are made easier than defensive habits.

One useful routine is a **two-pass judgement**. The first pass records the immediate impression: what feels true, what feels threatening, what conclusion seems likely. The second pass asks for [disconfirming evidence]({{ 'disconfirming-evidence/' | relative_url }}), alternative explanations and source checks. This respects intuition without letting it finish the job.

Another is a **red-team norm**. In teams, families or organisations, disagreement should not be reserved for enemies. If only outsiders challenge a group’s view, challenge will feel like attack. If insiders regularly test assumptions, correction becomes part of loyalty rather than betrayal.

A third is **accuracy prompting**. Research on misinformation sharing finds that simple prompts directing attention to accuracy can reduce the sharing of false or misleading content. The implication is modest but useful: many people do not always share misinformation because they deeply believe it; sometimes attention is captured by identity, novelty or emotion, and a small shift back to accuracy improves behaviour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9051116/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCAccuracy prompts are a replicable and generalizable</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PMCAccuracy prompts are a replicable and generalizable</span></span></span>

Prebunking can also help. Instead of waiting to debunk every false claim after it spreads, prebunking teaches people to recognise manipulation techniques such as emotional language, scapegoating or false dilemmas. Large-scale research has found that psychological inoculation can improve resilience against misinformation, although it should be treated as one tool rather than a cure for deeper mistrust and polarisation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.abo6254" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.org">[Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on science.org.</span></span></span>

## The core skill: protect accuracy before identity takes over

Emotion and identity are not enemies of reason. They tell people what matters, where danger may lie, and which communities they belong to. But under pressure, they can quietly rewrite the reasoning task. Evidence becomes a threat, certainty becomes relief, and loyalty becomes a standard of truth.

Improving analytical skill therefore requires more than learning fallacy names or debating techniques. It requires noticing the emotional conditions under which one’s standards shift. The strongest thinkers are not those who never feel defensive; they are those who can detect defensiveness early enough to slow down, widen the frame, and give inconvenient evidence a fair hearing.

    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
