## Introduction

Critical thinking is often described as a set of skills: analysing arguments, evaluating evidence, recognising [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}), and drawing justified conclusions. Yet those skills only improve judgement when people are willing to use them, especially when doing so is uncomfortable or inconvenient. A person may know how to detect [weak evidence]({{ 'weak-evidence/' | relative_url }}) but still ignore it if it challenges a cherished belief, threatens their status, or requires admitting a mistake. Research on critical thinking has therefore distinguished between **ability** and **disposition**: the habits of mind that determine whether critical-thinking skills are actually put into practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED315423.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.eric.ed.gov">[ERIC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.eric.ed.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Facione, Peter A. This distinction matters because many real-world failures of judgement are not caused by an absence of reasoning ability. They arise when capable people stop asking difficult questions, become overconfident, search only for confirming evidence, or rush to conclusions. ## The difference between ability and willingness

Knowing how to think critically is not the same as choosing to think critically.

The expert consensus behind the Delphi Report describes critical thinking as both a collection of cognitive skills and a corresponding "critical spirit" or disposition. In [practice]({{ 'practice/' | relative_url }}), these two components reinforce one another. Skills provide the tools for analysing information, while dispositions determine whether those tools are used carefully, consistently, and honestly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED315423.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.eric.ed.gov">[ERIC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.eric.ed.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Facione, Peter A. TITLE. Critical Thinking: A Statement of Expert Consensus for Purposes of Educational Assessment and. Instruction. Rese...</span></span></span>

Consider two people reading a controversial health claim. Both understand how to assess evidence. One immediately accepts information that supports existing beliefs without checking sources. The other deliberately looks for contrary evidence, considers alternative explanations, and is prepared to revise an opinion if stronger evidence appears. Their reasoning ability may be similar, but their dispositions lead to very different [outcomes]({{ 'outcomes/' | relative_url }}).

This explains why educational researchers increasingly treat critical thinking as partly motivational. The question is not only *Can this person evaluate evidence?* but also *Will they do so when it is inconvenient, emotionally uncomfortable, or socially costly?* Studies of critical-thinking disposition repeatedly find that willingness to engage with evidence predicts whether reasoning skills are consistently applied in academic, professional, and clinical settings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/252896581_The_Disposition_Toward_Critical_Thinking_Its_Character_Measurement_and_Relationship_to_Critical_Thinking_Skill" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate+2PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) The Disposition Toward Critical Thinking24 Dec 2025 — This essay presents a researchbased expert consensus definition o...</span></span></span>

## Seven dispositions that support better judgement

The Delphi project and subsequent work on the California Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory identified seven broad habits that characterise people who regularly use critical-thinking skills well. These are not fixed personality traits but patterns of behaviour that can be encouraged through education and [deliberate practice]({{ 'deliberate-practice/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7799093/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Critical thinking disposition as a measure of competent...by NC Facione · 1994 · Cited by 1288 — Delphi Report&#x27;s consensus definit...</span></span></span>

* **Truth-seeking.** A commitment to following evidence wherever it leads rather than defending existing opinions. Truth-seeking requires asking difficult questions, examining contrary evidence, and preferring accuracy over being right.
* **Open-mindedness.** A willingness to consider alternative viewpoints fairly, including arguments that conflict with one's own beliefs. Open-mindedness does not require accepting every idea equally; it requires evaluating competing ideas by their evidence.
* **Analyticity.** Looking for reasons, recognising potential weaknesses in arguments, and anticipating possible problems before reaching conclusions instead of relying on intuition alone.
* **Systematicity.** Approaching problems in an organised and methodical way. Rather than jumping between ideas, systematic thinkers gather information, compare alternatives, and work through evidence step by step.
* **Critical-thinking self-confidence.** Confidence in one's ability to reason carefully without confusing confidence with certainty. This encourages independent judgement while remaining open to correction.
* **Inquisitiveness.** Intellectual curiosity that motivates learning beyond immediate practical necessity. Curious people actively seek understanding instead of stopping once a minimally acceptable answer appears.
* **Cognitive maturity.** Recognition that complex problems rarely have perfectly simple answers. Mature thinkers tolerate uncertainty, understand that reasonable disagreement is possible, and proportion conclusions to the available evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.healio.com/doi/pdf/10.3928/0148-4834-19941001-05" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.healio.com">[journals.healio.com+2PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.healio.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Critical Thinking Disposition as a Measureby NC Facione · 1994 · Cited by 1288 — Seven CT dispositional subscales were created: inquisiti...</span></span></span>

These dispositions complement one another. Open-mindedness without analyticity may lead to entertaining every claim equally. Analyticity without truth-seeking may become sophisticated rationalisation. Systematicity without inquisitiveness can become rigid procedure. Reliable judgement emerges from their interaction rather than from any single habit.

## Why uncomfortable evidence is the real test

Many reasoning tasks are straightforward when evidence supports existing beliefs. Dispositions become most important when evidence points elsewhere.

Truth-seeking often requires admitting previous [mistakes]({{ 'mistakes/' | relative_url }}). Open-mindedness may require giving serious attention to ideas associated with rival groups or unpopular viewpoints. Cognitive maturity demands accepting that some questions remain uncertain even after careful investigation.

These moments expose the difference between genuine critical thinking and motivated reasoning. Motivated reasoning occurs when people unconsciously use their intelligence to defend preferred conclusions instead of evaluating evidence impartially. Research in psychology consistently shows that higher reasoning ability alone does not eliminate this tendency. In some situations, more knowledgeable people become better at constructing arguments for conclusions they already favour rather than becoming more objective. Strong critical-thinking dispositions help interrupt that process by redirecting attention towards evidence instead of identity or emotion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://insightassessment.com/iaresource/critical-thinking-what-it-is-and-why-it-counts/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insightassessment.com">[Insight Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insightassessment.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Insight AssessmentCritical Thinking: What It Is and Why It CountsHow do you define &quot;critical thinking&quot;? In professional environments, this matters because many costly errors develop gradually rather than through obvious mistakes. Teams that encourage questioning assumptions, inviting criticism, and revisiting decisions are often more reliable than equally skilled teams that discourage dissent.

## Negative habits that make sharp reasoning unreliable

High intelligence can coexist with poor judgement when unhelpful thinking habits dominate. Several dispositions consistently undermine the practical value of reasoning skills.


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Confirmation seeking.** Looking mainly for evidence that supports existing beliefs while ignoring conflicting information.
* **Overconfidence.** Assuming initial conclusions are probably correct without checking competing explanations.
* **Defensive reasoning.** Treating disagreement as a personal attack rather than an opportunity to improve understanding.
* **Premature closure.** Ending investigation once an acceptable explanation appears instead of asking whether better evidence exists.
* **Intellectual laziness.** Relying on shortcuts, slogans, or authority when careful evaluation would be possible.
* **Status protection.** Refusing to revise beliefs because changing one's mind feels like losing credibility.
* **Selective scepticism.** Demanding extremely high standards of evidence only for claims one dislikes while accepting weaker evidence for preferred conclusions.

</div>

These habits reduce the reliability of otherwise capable thinkers because they distort when and how reasoning skills are applied. They also reinforce one another: overconfidence encourages premature closure, which reduces curiosity and weakens systematic investigation.

## Building dispositions through everyday practice

Unlike isolated reasoning techniques, dispositions develop through repeated behaviour across many situations.

Educational research suggests that critical-thinking habits strengthen when learners repeatedly justify conclusions with evidence, compare competing explanations, reflect on mistakes, and receive [feedback]({{ 'feedback/' | relative_url }}) on their reasoning rather than simply their answers. Regular exposure to respectful disagreement also helps normalise revising beliefs without treating revision as failure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://insightassessment.com/iaresource/critical-thinking-what-it-is-and-why-it-counts/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insightassessment.com">[Insight Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insightassessment.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Insight AssessmentCritical Thinking: What It Is and Why It CountsHow do you define &quot;critical thinking&quot;? How do you use it in your day to...</span></span></span>

Individuals can encourage these habits by adopting simple routines:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Ask, "What evidence would change my mind?"
* Deliberately seek one credible argument against an initial conclusion.
* Separate confidence from certainty by estimating how strong the evidence actually is.
* Explain conclusions step by step rather than relying on intuition alone.
* Treat changing one's mind as evidence of learning rather than inconsistency.
* Review important decisions afterwards to identify which assumptions proved correct and which did not.

</div>

These practices reinforce dispositions because they reward the process of careful judgement instead of merely rewarding correct outcomes.

## Why thinking habits matter more than isolated skills

Critical-thinking skills provide the machinery of good judgement, but dispositions determine whether that machinery is switched on when it matters most. The person who habitually seeks truth, welcomes legitimate criticism, works systematically, and accepts uncertainty is more likely to use reasoning well under pressure than someone with equal technical ability but weaker intellectual habits.

For that reason, improving critical thinking is not simply a matter of learning analytical techniques. It also requires cultivating habits that keep those techniques active in the face of bias, emotion, social pressure, and uncertainty. Purposeful judgement depends on both knowing **how** to think and consistently choosing to think that way.
