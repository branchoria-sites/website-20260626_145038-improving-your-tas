## Introduction

After-action reviews (AARs) improve team judgement when they are designed as learning conversations rather than performance evaluations. Instead of asking who made the mistake, they compare what the team expected with what actually happened, examine why the two differed, and identify specific changes for the next similar situation. This structure helps teams avoid hindsight bias, capture useful knowledge before it is forgotten, and steadily improve future decisions rather than merely explaining past [outcomes]({{ 'outcomes/' | relative_url }}). Originally developed in the military and now widely used in healthcare, emergency management and business, AARs work best when they are short, evidence-based and focused on improving future judgement rather than assigning blame.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://executiveeducation.wharton.upenn.edu/thought-leadership/wharton-at-work/2012/04/after-action-reviews/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: executiveeducation.wharton.upenn.edu">[Wharton Executive Education+2cebma.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">executiveeducation.wharton.upenn.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wharton Executive Education After Action Review</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wharton Executive EducationAfter Action Review - AAR Process - Nano Tools for LeadersThe After Action Review (AAR) was developed by the U...</span></span></span>

## The four questions that keep reviews focused

The most effective after-action reviews are built around four simple questions. Their value lies not in their simplicity but in the discipline they impose on the discussion.

1. **What did we expect to happen?**

Starting with expectations forces the team to recover its original reasoning before hindsight reshapes memories. Good reviews compare today's knowledge with yesterday's [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}) rather than judging past decisions using information that only became available later.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://chiefexecutive.net/the-us-armys-secret-to-building-a-leader-driven-learning-culture-after-action-reviews/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: chiefexecutive.net">[2. **What actually happened?**]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">chiefexecutive.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">· What actually happened? · Why did it happen? · Most importantly, how do we improve our next operation?Read more...</span></span></span>

This stage establishes a shared factual record. Teams separate observable events from interpretations, reducing arguments over recollection and preventing premature conclusions.
3. **Why were there differences?**

This is the analytical core of the review. The aim is not merely to [explain]({{ 'explain/' | relative_url }}) failure but to identify which assumptions proved wrong, which signals were missed, which uncertainties were underestimated, and which decisions were sound despite producing poor outcomes because of chance.
4. **What should we sustain or change next time?**

Reviews should finish by identifying concrete behavioural or procedural adjustments. Successful practices deserve as much attention as failures because understanding why something worked makes it more likely to be repeated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cebma.org/assets/Uploads/Salem-Schatz-Guide-to-the-After-Action-Review.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cebma.org">[cebma.org+2Wharton Executive Education]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cebma.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Guide to the After Action ReviewOne team member reported, “without an AAR you eep learning your lessons again the hard way!” An AAR is ce...</span></span></span>

Notice that none of these questions asks, "Who caused the problem?" Responsibility still matters, but the review's purpose is improving collective judgement. Questions centred on reasoning and evidence produce far richer learning than questions centred on individual fault.

## Why blame ruins feedback

Many organisations hold meetings after important projects yet continue making similar [mistakes]({{ 'mistakes/' | relative_url }}). The difference is often not whether they review events but how they review them.

When participants expect criticism or embarrassment, they naturally defend themselves. They explain away mistakes, minimise uncertainty, or avoid mentioning weak signals they ignored. The discussion becomes a negotiation over reputation rather than an investigation into judgement.

Research on psychological safety—the shared belief that people can speak candidly without fear of humiliation—shows why this matters. Teams with greater psychological safety are more willing to report mistakes, ask questions and challenge assumptions, behaviours that support organisational learning rather than conceal problems. Psychological safety does not eliminate accountability; instead, it enables honest reporting so accountability can be based on accurate information rather than incomplete stories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2307/2666999" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[Sage Journals+2Harvard Business School]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It introduces the construct of team psychological safety—a shared...</span></span></span>

Blame also creates another subtle problem: it encourages outcome-based thinking.

A successful project can contain poor decisions that happened to work because circumstances were favourable. Equally, a well-reasoned decision can produce an unfavourable outcome because of uncertainty or bad luck. Teams that evaluate only results often reinforce bad judgement while criticising good reasoning that encountered unpredictable events. Effective AARs therefore examine the quality of the decision process alongside the outcome itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hbr.org/2011/04/strategies-for-learning-from-failure" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hbr.org">[Harvard Business Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hbr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by Amy C. ## How teams turn lessons into routines

Many reviews produce pages of observations that are rarely used again. Teams improve judgement only when lessons become part of future decisions.

Useful AARs therefore produce a small number of practical changes rather than a long catalogue of recommendations. These often include:

* updating decision [checklists]({{ 'checklists/' | relative_url }});

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* modifying planning assumptions;
* changing escalation thresholds;
* improving communication protocols;
* recording new warning indicators to monitor;
* clarifying roles during future incidents.

</div>

The changes should be specific enough that another team could apply them without attending the original meeting. Vague conclusions such as "communicate better" or "plan more carefully" rarely change behaviour. By contrast, "include operations staff in supplier risk meetings" or "require written probability estimates before approval" creates observable habits that strengthen judgement over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://alnap.hacdn.io/media/documents/pnadf360.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: alnap.hacdn.io">[alnap.hacdn.io+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">alnap.hacdn.io</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">After-Action Review Technical GuidanceThe meeting should follow an agenda, using the four guiding questions to set up the “meat” of the d...</span></span></span>

Another useful [practice]({{ 'practice/' | relative_url }}) is assigning ownership for each lesson. Without a named individual or team responsible for implementing a change, even accurate insights are easily forgotten once normal work resumes.

## Reviews should examine successes as well as failures

One common misconception is that after-action reviews exist only for things that went wrong.

In fact, successful operations often contain valuable information about sound judgement. Reviewing success helps distinguish repeatable practices from fortunate accidents. It can reveal effective coordination, accurate forecasting, timely information sharing or good contingency planning that might otherwise remain invisible because the outcome appeared routine.

The military tradition that popularised AARs deliberately applies them after successful exercises as well as unsuccessful ones, recognising that sustained excellence depends on understanding why success occurred, not merely celebrating it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://executiveeducation.wharton.upenn.edu/thought-leadership/wharton-at-work/2012/04/after-action-reviews/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: executiveeducation.wharton.upenn.edu">[Wharton Executive Education+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">executiveeducation.wharton.upenn.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wharton Executive Education After Action Review</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wharton Executive EducationAfter Action Review - AAR Process - Nano Tools for LeadersThe After Action Review (AAR) was developed by the U...</span></span></span>

## Common failure modes

Even organisations committed to learning can undermine after-action reviews through poor design.

Frequent problems include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Waiting too long.** Memories become reconstructed and important details disappear.
* **Allowing senior voices to dominate.** Junior participants often observed different evidence but may not volunteer it.
* **Collecting opinions before facts.** Speculation spreads quickly if the timeline is unclear.
* **Trying to solve every organisational problem.** Reviews become unfocused and produce little action.
* **Recording lessons without follow-through.** Knowledge that never changes future practice has little value.

</div>

Experienced facilitators therefore keep discussions tightly focused on the specific event, encourage contributions from everyone involved, and distinguish observed facts from interpretations before discussing improvements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pinnacle-leaders.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/Leaders_Guide_to_AAR.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pinnacle-leaders.com">[pinnacle-leaders.com+2alnap.hacdn.io]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pinnacle-leaders.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Leaders Guide to AAR</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Leader&#x27;s Guide to After-Action Reviews (AAR)by CA Center-Training · 2013 · Cited by 5 — An AAR is a professional discussion of a trai...</span></span></span>

## A practical review format for improving judgement

For most project teams, an effective review can often be completed in under an hour if it follows a disciplined sequence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Reconstruct the original objectives and assumptions.
2. Build a shared timeline using observable events.
3. Identify where expectations and reality diverged.
4. Examine the reasoning behind key decisions rather than only their outcomes.
5. Separate controllable causes from unavoidable uncertainty.
6. Agree on a limited number of concrete changes.
7. Assign responsibility for implementing each change before ending the meeting.

</div>

This format keeps attention on learning rather than retrospective justification. Over repeated projects, the accumulation of these small, documented adjustments creates a stronger decision-making process, allowing teams to recognise recurring patterns, calibrate their judgement more accurately and avoid repeating preventable mistakes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://executiveeducation.wharton.upenn.edu/thought-leadership/wharton-at-work/2021/07/after-action-reviews-simple-tool/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: executiveeducation.wharton.upenn.edu">[Wharton Executive Education+2cebma.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">executiveeducation.wharton.upenn.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wharton Executive Education After-Action Reviews: A Simple Yet Powerful Tool</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wharton Executive EducationAfter-Action Reviews: A Simple Yet Powerful Tool - WhartonThe process itself is an active discussion centered...</span></span></span>
