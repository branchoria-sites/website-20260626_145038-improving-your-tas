## Introduction

Good decision-making is not only about protecting yourself. A choice that feels low-risk from your own perspective may expose other people to costs, inconvenience, danger, or long-term commitments they did not choose. Within the broader [practice]({{ 'practice/' | relative_url }}) of matching analysis to [decision stakes]({{ 'decision-stakes/' | relative_url }}), one of the strongest signals that a decision deserves greater care is that its consequences will be shared.


<img src="{{ "/assets/images/improving_your_think_da4710_decision_stakes_7c2e2a_shared_risk_decision_6ce5c9-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Shared Risk illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/improving_your_think_da4710_decision_stakes_7c2e2a_shared_risk_decision_6ce5c9-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/improving_your_think_da4710_decision_stakes_7c2e2a_shared_risk_decision_6ce5c9-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">
A simple shift in perspective changes the quality of thinking: instead of asking only, “What happens if I am wrong?”, ask, “Who else pays if I am wrong?” This question often reveals hidden risks, identifies people who should be consulted before acting, and encourages safeguards that reduce harm without unnecessarily slowing every decision.

## Who Pays If This Goes Wrong?

Many poor decisions arise because the decision-maker experiences different incentives from the people who bear the consequences. Economists sometimes describe this as a principal–agent problem, but the practical idea is straightforward: the person choosing is not always the person carrying the risk.

Common examples include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A manager adopting a new workflow that increases employees' workload.
* A software engineer deploying a change that customers cannot easily undo.
* A parent making a financial commitment that affects the entire household.
* A project leader accepting an ambitious deadline that colleagues must meet.
* A local authority approving development that permanently changes a neighbourhood.

</div>

These decisions differ from purely personal choices because the downside is distributed across other people. That makes the decision effectively higher stakes, even if it feels routine to the person making it.

The UK Health and Safety Executive (HSE) places this idea at the centre of practical risk assessment by requiring organisations to identify not only hazards but also **who might be harmed and how**. The first step is therefore not merely identifying a problem but identifying the people exposed to its consequences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hse.gov.uk/risk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hse.gov.uk">[HSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hse.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">risk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HSEManaging risks and risk assessment at work: Overview24 Mar 2025 — As an employer, you must make a &#x27;suitable and sufficient assessment&#x27;...</span></span></span>

## Affected Parties Are a Signal That More Thinking Is Needed

Not every decision involving others requires lengthy deliberation. The important question is whether other people become involuntary participants in the risk.

Useful questions include:

* Who gains the benefit?
* Who bears the cost if things fail?
* Who cannot easily opt out?
* Who lacks information that I already possess?
* Who will have to repair the damage if my [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}) prove wrong?

These questions often reveal risks that ordinary cost-benefit thinking misses.

For example, selecting a new internal software tool may appear inexpensive because the purchase price is low. However, if hundreds of employees must learn new systems, experience temporary productivity losses, or migrate years of work, the true cost is distributed rather than visible on a budget sheet.

Similarly, a physician choosing among treatments, a school changing safeguarding procedures, or a company altering privacy settings all make decisions whose consequences fall partly on people with less control over the outcome.

The broader the circle of affected people, the stronger the argument for slowing down and [testing]({{ 'testing/' | relative_url }}) assumptions before acting.

Notice that none of these steps require certainty. They simply make hidden consequences visible before action.

## Consultation Without Creating Gridlock

A common mistake is assuming that involving affected people means every decision must become a committee decision. Effective consultation is not unanimous agreement; it is improving information before commitment.

Consultation is most valuable when other people possess information that the decision-maker lacks, when they understand operational realities, or when they will experience the consequences directly.

A practical approach is to scale consultation with the stakes.

[**Low shared risk**](#endnote-5 "
<details class="endnote-snippet"><summary>Source snippet</summary><p>Risk assessments must be shared with all staff and other people that may be affected by the risk.Read more&quot;)...</p></details>

* Inform people.
* Invite brief [feedback]({{ 'feedback/' | relative_url }}).
* Decide quickly.

[**Moderate shared risk**](#endnote-5 "
<details class="endnote-snippet"><summary>Source snippet</summary><p>Risk assessments must be shared with all staff and other people that may be affected by the risk.Read more&quot;)...</p></details>

* Consult those directly affected.
* Test assumptions.
* Adjust obvious problems before implementation.

[**High shared risk**](#endnote-5 "
<details class="endnote-snippet"><summary>Source snippet</summary><p>Risk assessments must be shared with all staff and other people that may be affected by the risk.Read more&quot;)...</p></details>

* Seek perspectives from multiple affected groups.
* Document important assumptions.
* Explore alternatives.
* Create monitoring and review points before irreversible commitment.

Health and safety guidance similarly distinguishes consultation from surrendering responsibility. Decision-makers remain accountable, but consulting affected people often produces better risk identification because those doing the work frequently understand practical hazards that senior leaders overlook. ## Practical Ways to Reduce Shared Risk

When a decision affects others, several techniques reduce potential harm without imposing excessive bureaucracy.

### Start with reversible trials

If possible, introduce a pilot rather than a permanent change.

Examples include:

* running a new policy in one department;
* testing software with a small user group;
* introducing temporary scheduling changes before permanent adoption.

Reversible experiments generate information while limiting the number of people exposed if assumptions prove wrong.

### Separate confidence from evidence

Decision-makers often mistake [familiarity]({{ 'familiarity/' | relative_url }}) for safety.

A routine decision may deserve fresh scrutiny if:

* circumstances have changed;
* new groups are affected;
* the scale is much larger than before;
* previous success depended on conditions that no longer exist.

### Look for silent stakeholders

People affected most are not always those represented in meetings.

Consider:

* future employees;
* customers;
* contractors;
* neighbours;
* vulnerable users;
* people with limited bargaining power.

Good governance asks not only who is speaking, but also whose interests are absent.

### Ask who performs the recovery

When failures occur, someone usually pays for correction.

If the answer is "someone else", the original decision deserves additional scrutiny because the costs have been shifted rather than eliminated.

## Common Failure Modes

Several recurring thinking errors increase shared risk.

**Assuming consent from silence.** People may remain quiet because they lack authority, not because they agree.

**Overweighting personal benefit.** Decision-makers naturally notice advantages they receive more readily than costs imposed on others.

**Treating routine decisions as harmless.** Repeated small decisions can accumulate into substantial burdens for staff, customers or communities.

**Ignoring unequal vulnerability.** The same mistake affects different people differently. A minor inconvenience for one person may create major hardship for another.

**Confusing speed with effectiveness.** Faster decisions are valuable only when they do not create larger downstream costs through avoidable failures and rework.

## A Simple Shared-Risk Check

Before committing to a decision that affects other people, ask:

1. Who besides me experiences the downside?
2. Can those people reasonably avoid the consequences?
3. Have I consulted the people with the best information about practical risks?
4. Can I reduce exposure through a pilot, phased rollout, or review point?
5. If the decision fails, who performs the repair and who pays the cost?

These questions rarely require extensive analysis. Instead, they act as a proportional safeguard, ensuring that decisions involving shared consequences receive more care than decisions affecting only the decision-maker. By making affected parties visible early, they improve both judgement and fairness while avoiding unnecessary delay.
