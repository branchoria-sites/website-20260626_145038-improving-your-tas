## Introduction

Good judgement often begins with a question that feels almost too simple: **how common is this outcome before considering the details of this particular case?** Base rates are the background frequencies of events in a relevant comparison group. Ignoring them leads people to match stories rather than estimate [probabilities]({{ 'probabilities/' | relative_url }}). A candidate "looks like" a successful executive, a patient "sounds like" they have a rare disease, or a new project "feels different" from past failures. These impressions may contain useful information, but they should usually be adjustments to the starting probability, not replacements for it.

Research on judgement and decision-making consistently shows that people often overweight vivid, case-specific information and underweight statistical prevalence, particularly when a compelling narrative is available. Yet the same body of research also shows that the problem is more nuanced than early laboratory experiments suggested: people can use base rates effectively when information is presented clearly, when they have relevant experience, or when decisions unfold over repeated real-world situations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9038831/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2Branden Fitelson]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCExperts use base rates in real-world sequential decisions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by D Link · 2021 · Cited by 15 — We argue here that while naïve subjects demonstrate base-rate neglect in laboratory conditions, exper...</span></span></span>

## The trap of "this looks like X"

The classic mistake is to confuse resemblance with probability. If someone matches our mental image of a profession, illness or personality type, we instinctively treat that resemblance as strong evidence. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky demonstrated that people frequently judge likelihood by representativeness: they ask whether a case resembles a stereotype rather than how frequently the outcome actually occurs. The result is base-rate neglect—giving too little weight to prior probabilities when making predictions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/209410239_On_the_psychology_of_prediction" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">209410239 On the psychology of prediction</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">On the psychology of prediction27 Sept 2025 — Considers that intuitive predictions follow a judgmental heuristic-representati...</span></span></span>

This shortcut is appealing because stories are concrete while base rates are abstract. A detailed personal description feels informative, whereas "2% of applicants succeed" or "1 in 1,000 people has this disease" can seem impersonal or irrelevant. Research suggests that people often classify specific information as more relevant than general statistical information, even when the statistics are objectively more informative for estimating probability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/base-rate-fallacy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thedecisionlab.com">[The Decision Lab]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thedecisionlab.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Decision Lab Base Rate Fallacy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Decision LabBase Rate Fallacy - The Decision...Base Rate Fallacy is our tendency to give more weight to the event-specific informati...</span></span></span>

The consequence is not that individual details should be ignored. Instead, they should update an initial estimate derived from the appropriate comparison group. The sequence matters:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Start with the outside frequency.
2. Ask how diagnostic the case-specific evidence really is.
3. Adjust the probability accordingly.

</div>

Skipping the first step often produces overconfident judgements.

## Medical, hiring and forecasting examples

### Medical diagnosis

Medicine provides one of the clearest demonstrations of why base rates matter.

Imagine a disease affecting only 1% of the population. Even if a diagnostic test correctly identifies affected patients 99% of the time, false positives can outnumber true positives because healthy people vastly outnumber patients. Many people—including trained physicians in some studies—substantially overestimate the probability that a positive result means a patient actually has the disease. The prevalence of the disease must be combined with the test's accuracy before interpreting the result.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8075297/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tversky and Kahneman&#x27;s Cognitive Illusions: Who Can Solve...by G Bruckmaier · 2021 · Cited by 47 — The mathematical reason for the co...</span></span></span>

This explains why clinicians often distinguish between [screening]({{ 'screening/' | relative_url }}) tests used in low-risk populations and confirmatory tests used after other evidence has increased the prior probability. The same laboratory result can have very different meanings depending on how common the condition is in the population being tested.

### Hiring decisions

Recruitment often shifts rapidly from evidence to storytelling. A confident interviewee who resembles previous successful managers may feel like an obvious choice. However, interviews typically provide limited predictive information compared with structured assessments and historical hiring data.

Suppose only one in ten applicants for a demanding role ultimately performs exceptionally. An impressive interview should increase the estimated probability of success, but it rarely justifies acting as though excellence has suddenly become the most likely outcome. Beginning with historical success rates encourages more calibrated expectations and reduces the influence of charisma or stereotype.

The practical question is not, "Does this person remind me of a successful employee?" but, "Among applicants who looked similar in measurable ways, how many actually succeeded?"

### Forecasting projects

Forecasting research has repeatedly found that planners favour the "inside view": concentrating on the unique features of the current project while neglecting how comparable projects actually turned out.

The recommended alternative is an "outside view" or reference class forecast. Instead of asking why the current project will succeed, decision-makers identify a relevant class of similar completed projects, examine the distribution of [outcomes]({{ 'outcomes/' | relative_url }}), and then adjust only if there is strong evidence that the current case genuinely differs. This approach has become influential in project planning because it counteracts optimism driven by compelling project narratives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_class_forecasting" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Reference class forecasting</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reference class forecasting</span></span></span>

## Updating from a comparison group

Using base rates does not mean treating every individual as average. It means establishing a sensible starting point before incorporating additional evidence.

A practical sequence looks like this:

* **Choose the right comparison group.** The base rate must come from genuinely similar cases. The average success rate of all businesses is less informative than the success rate of businesses in the same industry, scale and market.
* **Estimate the prior probability.** Ask, "Before I knew anything special about this case, what would I expect?"
* **Evaluate the strength of the new evidence.** Does it genuinely distinguish this case from others, or is it merely vivid?
* **Update gradually.** Strong evidence should move the estimate substantially; [weak evidence]({{ 'weak-evidence/' | relative_url }}) should move it only slightly.

This process reflects the logic behind Bayesian reasoning, even when no formal calculations are performed. The important habit is to begin with frequencies rather than [anecdotes]({{ 'anecdotes/' | relative_url }}).

## Why experts sometimes do better—and sometimes do not

Early laboratory studies gave the impression that people almost automatically ignore base rates. Later work has complicated that picture.

Research examining expert decisions in real-world settings has found that experienced decision-makers can make effective use of base-rate information, particularly when decisions are repeated over time and [feedback]({{ 'feedback/' | relative_url }}) is available. For example, analyses of elite beach volleyball serving decisions suggest that players adapt their choices according to opponents' underlying success rates rather than relying solely on recent memorable plays. This indicates that repeated experience can encourage attention to statistical regularities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9038831/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCExperts use base rates in real-world sequential decisions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by D Link · 2021 · Cited by 15 — We argue here that while naïve subjects demonstrate base-rate neglect in laboratory conditions, exper...</span></span></span>

However, expertise is not a guarantee. Medical research has documented situations in which physicians still underweight disease prevalence, especially when presented with compelling case descriptions or when statistical information is communicated poorly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/physicians-neglect-base-rates-and-it-matters/48984E851538FA30B5DE4D6D6CC35CA3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentPhysicians neglect base rates, and it mattersby RM Hamm · 1996 · Cited by 23 — A recent study show...</span></span></span>

The lesson is not that experts always avoid base-rate neglect, but that good environments—with repeated feedback, meaningful comparison groups and well-presented statistical information—make correct probabilistic reasoning more likely.

## Practical habits that reduce story matching

The most effective defence is to build a routine that forces statistical thinking before narrative thinking.

Before making an important judgement, ask:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **What is the relevant base rate?**(#endnote-7 "Endnote 7")<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_rate_fallacy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Base rate fallacy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Base rate fallacy</span></span></span>
* **What comparison group best matches this case?**
* **How much new information does this specific evidence genuinely add?**
* **Would I reach the same conclusion if the story were less vivid but the statistics were identical?**

</div>

Another useful technique is to express probabilities as [natural frequencies]({{ 'test-results/' | relative_url }}) rather than percentages. Saying "about 10 out of every 1,000 similar people" is often easier to reason about than "1%". Research has shown that presenting statistical information in frequency formats can improve probabilistic reasoning and reduce common errors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gordonpennycook.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/pennycook-thompson_base-rate-neglect.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gordonpennycook.com">[Gordon Pennycook]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gordonpennycook.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">pennycook thompson base rate neglect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gordon PennycookBase-Rate Neglect &#124; Gordon Pennycookby G Pennycook · Cited by 66 — Kahneman&#x27;s (2003) dual-process account holds that base...</span></span></span>

The goal is not to suppress [intuition]({{ 'intuition/' | relative_url }}) but to anchor it. A compelling story may justify moving away from the average, yet good analytical thinking begins by knowing what the average actually is. Starting with the outside frequency provides a disciplined reference point, making it far less likely that an attractive narrative will overwhelm the evidence.
