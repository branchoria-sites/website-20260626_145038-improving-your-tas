## Introduction

One of the most useful answers to the question “What would [change my mind]({{ 'change-mind/' | relative_url }})?” is: “It would not necessarily make me abandon my view—it would make me less confident in it.” Good reasoning is rarely a choice between complete certainty and complete reversal. Instead, new evidence often justifies moving from, for example, 90% confidence to 75%, or from “very likely” to “more uncertain”. This kind of gradual updating makes beliefs more responsive to reality while avoiding the instability of changing positions with every new piece of information. Research in psychology, decision science, and Bayesian reasoning consistently suggests that treating confidence as something that can be adjusted in degrees leads to better calibrated judgement and reduces the risk of both dogmatism and overreaction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9966223/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2P.J. ## Confidence as a spectrum

People often describe beliefs as though they were switches: either true or false, accepted or rejected. In [practice]({{ 'practice/' | relative_url }}), most important decisions involve uncertainty. Whether assessing an investment, evaluating scientific evidence, or deciding between competing explanations, the more realistic question is not "Do I believe this?" but "How strongly do I believe it?"

Thinking in terms of confidence creates room for evidence to have proportional effects. A single contradictory study, one disappointing business result, or an unexpected observation may reduce confidence without eliminating the overall case. Likewise, one supportive finding may strengthen a view without proving it beyond doubt.

This idea is closely related to Bayesian thinking. In Bayesian reasoning, beliefs begin with a prior level of confidence and are updated as new evidence arrives, producing a revised—or posterior—level of confidence. The important practical lesson is not that everyone must perform probability calculations, but that evidence should shift beliefs by an amount roughly proportional to its strength and reliability rather than triggering automatic reversals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://astrowizici.st/teaching/data-analysis/fundamentals/lecture-03/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astrowizici.st">[astrowizici.st+2HEC Paris]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astrowizici.st</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bayesian Updating in Practice</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Andy Casey2 Mar 2026 — The Bayesian view describes how probable it is some event will take place, and we update our beliefs about the wor...</span></span></span>

A useful mental scale might look like this:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **95–99%:** Extraordinary confidence; only very strong contrary evidence would justify a major revision.
* **70–90%:** Strong confidence but genuine openness to adjustment.
* **50–70%:** Tentative judgement where additional evidence could move confidence substantially.
* **Below 50%:** The alternative explanation appears more plausible.

</div>

The exact numbers matter less than recognising that confidence has many possible positions between certainty and rejection.

## When partial updates are enough

Many decisions involve evidence that is informative without being decisive. Expecting every new fact either to confirm everything or overturn everything creates distorted thinking.

Partial updates are appropriate when:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The new evidence is credible but limited in scope.
* Different pieces of evidence point in different directions.
* Existing evidence remains substantial despite new objections.
* The new information changes the estimated likelihood rather than the underlying possibility.

</div>

Consider a product launch. A company expects strong customer adoption. After the first month, uptake is weaker than expected, but customer satisfaction among early users is excellent. The rational response is rarely "The launch will definitely fail" or "Nothing has changed." Instead, confidence in rapid growth may decrease while confidence in long-term potential remains relatively high.

Similarly, when evaluating a scientific claim, one failed replication should usually reduce confidence rather than eliminate belief, particularly if multiple earlier studies remain supportive. Conversely, one successful replication should increase confidence without implying that every remaining uncertainty has disappeared.

Updating confidence in this way prevents a common mistake: treating every new observation as if it deserved equal weight regardless of its quality or relevance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK571047/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[NCBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NCBIReviewing the evidence: heuristics and biases</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NIHby L Bojke · 2021 · Cited by 3 — This chapter reviewed evidence relating to the psychological biases of greatest relevance to S...</span></span></span>

## Avoiding all-or-nothing thinking

All-or-nothing thinking encourages unnecessary certainty. Once people feel committed to an extreme position, any concession can seem like admitting complete error. This creates psychological resistance to learning.

A confidence-based approach changes the incentives. Lowering confidence from 90% to 75% is not a failure. It is evidence that the belief is responding appropriately to new information.

This also makes discussions more productive. Instead of asking:

> "Were you wrong?"

people can ask:

> "How much should this evidence change your confidence?"

That question is usually easier to answer honestly because it allows proportionate responses instead of forcing binary choices.

Research on [actively open-minded]({{ 'open-mindedness/' | relative_url }}) thinking describes this willingness to consider contrary evidence and delay premature certainty as a characteristic associated with better reasoning and more accurate judgement. ## Why overconfidence makes updating harder

Confidence itself influences how people process new information.

Experimental neuroscience has shown that when individuals are highly confident in an initial judgement, they become more likely to process supporting information while giving less weight to contradictory evidence. High confidence therefore does more than reflect beliefs—it can actively make future belief revision less likely by changing how subsequent evidence is interpreted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-16278-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Confidence drives a neural confirmation biasby M Rollwage · 2020 · Cited by 269 — We show that holding high confidence in a decisio...</span></span></span>

This helps [explain]({{ 'explain/' | relative_url }}) why confidence should itself be treated as something worth monitoring.

Questions such as these can help:

* How certain was I before seeing this evidence?
* Is my confidence justified by the quality of the evidence, or simply by [familiarity]({{ 'familiarity/' | relative_url }})?
* Would someone who disagreed reasonably lower my confidence after seeing this information?

These questions separate confidence from commitment. They encourage calibration rather than stubbornness.

## Practical ways to update confidence

Confidence updating works best when it is deliberate rather than intuitive.

A simple process is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **State your current confidence.** Even rough estimates such as "about 80% confident" create a baseline.
2. **Evaluate the new evidence.** Consider its reliability, independence, and relevance rather than whether it supports your preferred conclusion.
3. **Adjust proportionally.** Strong evidence deserves larger shifts than weak or ambiguous evidence.
4. **Look for accumulation.** Several modest pieces of evidence may together justify a substantial update, even if none would do so individually.

</div>

This approach also reduces emotional attachment to particular conclusions. Instead of defending an [identity]({{ 'identity/' | relative_url }}) or a position, you are continually refining an estimate.

## The goal is calibration, not constant doubt

Updating confidence should not become permanent indecision. Good judgement aims for calibration: confidence that matches the available evidence as closely as possible.

Someone who never changes confidence is likely to ignore new information. Someone who dramatically changes position after every new observation is equally unlikely to reach consistently good conclusions. The most reliable thinkers tend to occupy the middle ground, making many small, evidence-based adjustments over time rather than waiting for dramatic reversals or reacting impulsively to isolated events.

Viewed this way, changing your mind does not always mean adopting the opposite conclusion. Often, the most rational response is simply becoming a little less certain—or a little more certain—than you were before.
