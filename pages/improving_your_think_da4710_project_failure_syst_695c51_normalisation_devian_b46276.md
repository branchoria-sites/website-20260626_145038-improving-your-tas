## Introduction

Normalisation of deviance describes a gradual shift in judgement in which practices that were originally recognised as unsafe, exceptional or outside agreed standards come to be treated as normal because they repeatedly appear to work. The danger is not that people suddenly become reckless. Rather, each successful outcome without an immediate failure encourages the belief that the original safety margin was overly cautious. Over time, the organisation stops seeing itself as taking unusual risks at all. This mechanism is especially important for improving analytical thinking because it explains why intelligent, conscientious teams can collectively drift into poor decisions without anyone deliberately choosing an obviously dangerous course. The concept, developed by sociologist Diane Vaughan through her study of the Challenger disaster, has since become a widely used explanation for failures in engineering, healthcare and project management.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/C/bo22781921.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: press.uchicago.edu">[University of Chicago Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">press.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chicago PressThe Challenger Launch DecisionShe reveals how and why NASA insiders, when repeatedly faced with evidence that...</span></span></span>

## How repeated survival teaches false confidence

The most important feature of normalisation of deviance is that success becomes misleading evidence.

Projects rarely move directly from safe [practice]({{ 'practice/' | relative_url }}) to serious failure. Instead, a small exception is made: a test is shortened, a review is skipped, a deadline is protected by accepting an unresolved issue, or a known defect is judged "acceptable for now". If nothing visibly goes wrong, the exception becomes easier to justify next time. Eventually the exception no longer feels like an exception.

This creates a dangerous [feedback loop]({{ 'feedback-loop/' | relative_url }}):


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A deviation from the intended process occurs.
* The project succeeds despite the deviation.
* The absence of immediate harm is interpreted as proof that the deviation is safe.
* The deviation becomes routine.
* Future decisions begin from the new baseline rather than the original standard.

</div>

The key analytical mistake is confusing **survival** with **evidence of safety**. A project may avoid disaster because of luck, hidden safety margins or favourable circumstances rather than because the shortcut was genuinely harmless. Every successful outcome increases confidence while providing very little information about how close the project came to failure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25742063/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed+2Cranfield University DSpace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">When Doing Wrong Feels So Right: Normalization of...by MR Price · 2018 · Cited by 116 — Normalization of deviance is a term first...</span></span></span>

## From shuttle safety to everyday project habits

The Challenger disaster remains the defining historical example because it illustrates this gradual drift rather than a single catastrophic mistake.

Engineers at NASA and contractor Morton Thiokol had observed erosion and damage to the solid rocket booster O-rings on previous shuttle flights. These observations did not initially fit the intended design [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}). However, because successive launches completed successfully, the damage gradually came to be regarded as an acceptable operating condition rather than evidence that the design required correction. Vaughan argued that decision-makers did not suddenly abandon safety standards. Instead, they slowly redefined what counted as acceptable evidence of risk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/C/bo22781921.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: press.uchicago.edu">[University of Chicago Press+2Columbia Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">press.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chicago PressThe Challenger Launch DecisionShe reveals how and why NASA insiders, when repeatedly faced with evidence that...</span></span></span>

This is precisely why the Challenger case remains valuable for ordinary projects. Most organisations never face life-or-death engineering decisions, but many experience comparable forms of drift:

* Temporary workarounds become permanent operating procedures.
* Schedule [pressure]({{ 'pressure/' | relative_url }}) justifies incomplete documentation.
* [Risk registers]({{ 'risk-registers/' | relative_url }}) accumulate unresolved items that no longer receive attention.
* Defects are repeatedly accepted because previous releases caused no visible problems.
* Governance meetings approve exceptions so frequently that they cease to function as exceptions.

None of these decisions appears dramatic in isolation. Their significance lies in the accumulated change to what the organisation considers normal. Research on project governance has argued that normalised deviance develops through a sequence of individually understandable choices that become institutionalised until the organisation loses sight of its original standards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dspace.lib.cranfield.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/be88cd44-9590-4be0-bc43-abf5964f5448/content" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dspace.lib.cranfield.ac.uk">[Cranfield University DSpace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dspace.lib.cranfield.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cranfield.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The lesson is not that every exception is wrong. Projects often require informed [trade-offs]({{ 'trade-offs/' | relative_url }}). The problem begins when repeated exceptions quietly redefine the baseline without deliberate review.

## Why ordinary decisions are especially vulnerable

Large disasters often attract attention because of spectacular technical failures, but normalisation of deviance usually grows through routine administrative decisions.

Several organisational pressures make this mechanism particularly attractive:

**Deadline pressure.** Teams under delivery pressure naturally ask whether a known issue is "good enough for now". Repeating this judgement without reassessing cumulative risk gradually lowers standards.

**[Familiarity]({{ 'familiarity/' | relative_url }}).** Hazards that are encountered repeatedly become psychologically less alarming. What once prompted escalation becomes another routine item on a meeting agenda.

**Performance incentives.** If managers are rewarded for maintaining delivery dates rather than exposing uncertainty, evidence supporting continuation receives more attention than evidence supporting delay.

**Incremental change.** Small adjustments rarely trigger the scrutiny that would accompany a major policy change. Because each individual step appears modest, no single decision feels like crossing an important boundary.

These pressures do not require dishonesty or incompetence. They arise from normal organisational behaviour, making them particularly difficult to recognise while a project is underway.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dspace.lib.cranfield.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/be88cd44-9590-4be0-bc43-abf5964f5448/content" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dspace.lib.cranfield.ac.uk">[Cranfield University DSpace+2IDEAS/RePEc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dspace.lib.cranfield.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cranfield.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

## Warning signs that exceptions have become routine

Analytically, the important question is not whether a project contains exceptions—it almost certainly does—but whether the organisation still recognises them as exceptions.

Warning signs include:

* Teams describing known deviations as "the way we always do it".
* Risk acceptance being justified primarily by previous successful [outcomes]({{ 'outcomes/' | relative_url }}).

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* Temporary fixes remaining in place for months without formal review.
* Safety or quality thresholds gradually changing without explicit approval.
* Repeated waivers becoming easier to obtain than resolving the underlying problem.
* Engineers or specialists expressing concern while management increasingly views those concerns as routine rather than unusual.

</div>

Another warning sign is a subtle change in language. Teams stop asking, "Does this meet the intended standard?" and instead ask, "Have we got away with this before?" The first question measures performance against design intent; the second measures it against recent experience.

## Preventing deviance drift in project decisions

The practical defence against normalisation of deviance is not to ban every exception but to prevent today's exception from silently becoming tomorrow's standard.

Useful practices include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **Review repeated waivers together.** A single approved exception may be reasonable; ten similar exceptions indicate that the underlying process or design needs attention.
* **Separate schedule decisions from technical judgement.** Technical experts should be able to describe risk without simultaneously defending delivery commitments.
* **Record the original rationale.** Comparing current practice with the original design assumptions helps reveal how far operational behaviour has drifted.
* **Treat successful near misses as learning opportunities.** A problem that caused no visible harm should still be analysed if it required unusual recovery or depended on fortunate circumstances.
* **Invite periodic external review.** Fresh reviewers are less likely to accept local assumptions that long-serving teams now regard as normal.

</div>

These practices shift attention away from whether the project survived and towards whether its decisions remain consistent with the standards the organisation intended to uphold.

## Why this mechanism matters for better thinking

Normalisation of deviance is fundamentally a reasoning error embedded within organisations. It replaces evidence about safe operating limits with evidence about recent survival. The distinction is subtle but crucial.

For anyone trying to improve analytical thinking, the lasting lesson from Challenger is not simply "listen to experts" or "take safety seriously". It is to recognise that repeated success can sometimes reduce rather than improve judgement. The most dangerous project decisions are often those that no longer feel like decisions at all because accumulated exceptions have quietly become the accepted way of working.
