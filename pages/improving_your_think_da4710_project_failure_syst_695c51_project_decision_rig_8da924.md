## Introduction

Projects rarely recover because people simply work harder. They recover because someone has the recognised authority to change the plan when the original [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}) stop matching reality. That authority includes deciding whether deadlines move, scope is reduced, funding changes, unresolved conflicts are settled, or the project should even continue at all. Without clear decision rights, teams often recognise problems early but cannot act on them. They continue executing an increasingly unrealistic plan while waiting for approvals that never arrive or are repeatedly deferred.

From a systems perspective, decision rights are a governance mechanism rather than a management technique. They determine who is allowed to replace wishful thinking with operational reality. Organisations that define these rights clearly are generally better able to recover struggling projects because decisions happen before delays compound into crises.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://projectdelivery.gov.uk/teal-book/home/part-d-managing-programmes-and-projects/chapter-13-the-governance-and-management-of-programmes-and-projects/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: projectdelivery.gov.uk">[Government Project Delivery+2Project Management Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">projectdelivery.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ernment Project Delivery Chapter 13</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The governance and management of...A governance and management framework sets out the authority limits, decision-making roles and rules...</span></span></span>

## Deadline, scope and conflict authority

Most failing projects eventually confront three questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Can the deadline change?
* Can the promised scope be reduced?
* Who decides when stakeholders disagree?

</div>

These are governance questions rather than scheduling questions. A project manager may identify that the delivery date is impossible, but recovery cannot begin if nobody with sufficient authority is prepared to approve a revised schedule. Likewise, engineers may identify a smaller, achievable release, but if no sponsor can formally remove lower-priority features, the team continues chasing an unattainable objective.

Successful governance frameworks therefore distinguish between operational decisions, which remain with the project team, and strategic decisions, which belong to sponsors or governance boards. A well-designed framework specifies authority limits, escalation routes, accountabilities and the conditions under which plans must be reconsidered rather than defended.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://projectdelivery.gov.uk/teal-book/home/part-d-managing-programmes-and-projects/chapter-13-the-governance-and-management-of-programmes-and-projects/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: projectdelivery.gov.uk">[Government Project Delivery]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">projectdelivery.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ernment Project Delivery Chapter 13</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The governance and management of...A governance and management framework sets out the authority limits, decision-making roles and rules...</span></span></span>

The practical consequence is that recovery becomes faster because escalation leads directly to decisions instead of additional discussion.

## Why steering groups can become observers

Steering committees are intended to provide strategic direction, approve major changes and resolve issues beyond the authority of the delivery team. In [practice]({{ 'practice/' | relative_url }}), however, many drift into becoming reporting forums instead of decision forums.

Several patterns [explain]({{ 'explain/' | relative_url }}) this failure:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Meetings are dominated by status updates rather than decisions.
* Committee members lack the authority to commit their organisations.
* Difficult issues are repeatedly deferred until the next meeting.
* Members reward reassuring progress reports more than uncomfortable evidence.
* Escalations return to the project team without a clear ruling.

</div>

When this happens, governance adds delay instead of removing it. Teams continue working despite knowing that important assumptions have become false because nobody exercises the authority that governance was designed to provide.

Research on steering committees consistently identifies strategic decision-making, resource allocation, risk ownership and conflict resolution as their primary purpose. Studies of project governance likewise find that effective steering committees contribute to trust and project performance because participants know where difficult decisions will actually be made.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmi-nl.nl/blog/the-importance-of-steering-committees-to-project-managers-21957" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmi-nl.nl">[pmi-nl.nl+2emerald.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmi-nl.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the importance of steering committees to project managers 21957</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">They determine the project&#x27;s scope, objectives, and key performance indicators...Read more...</span></span></span>

The distinction is subtle but important. A steering committee that merely receives information is an observer. One that actively changes priorities, approves [trade-offs]({{ 'trade-offs/' | relative_url }}) and resolves conflicts is exercising governance.

## Decision rules that reward reality over reassurance

Projects rarely fail because warning signs are invisible. More often, warning signs fail to trigger meaningful decisions.

Recovery-oriented governance replaces vague judgement with explicit decision rules. Examples include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* **Schedule thresholds:** if completion confidence falls below an agreed level, executive review becomes mandatory.
* **Scope rules:** if critical milestones cannot be met, predefined lower-priority features are removed before adding more resources.
* **Risk thresholds:** unresolved high-impact risks automatically require sponsor intervention.
* **Dependency rules:** projects cannot pass governance gates while critical external dependencies remain unverified.
* **Evidence requirements:** progress is demonstrated through tested deliverables rather than presentation slides or percentage-complete estimates.

</div>

These rules reduce the influence of optimism because they define when authority must be exercised instead of allowing uncomfortable decisions to be postponed indefinitely.

Phase-gate governance follows this principle by requiring explicit "go", "pause", "redirect" or "stop" decisions before projects move into subsequent stages. The gate is valuable only if decision-makers are genuinely willing to reject or reshape unrealistic plans rather than treating every review as automatic approval.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/phase-gate-processes-promising-complex-547" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmi.org">[Project Management Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on pmi.org.</span></span></span>

## Why unclear authority keeps failing projects alive

Many organisations unintentionally distribute responsibility without distributing authority.

For example:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* The project manager is accountable for delivery but cannot change deadlines.
* Technical specialists identify unacceptable risks but cannot halt deployment.
* Sponsors own funding but rarely participate in operational trade-offs.
* Business departments demand additional features without accepting schedule consequences.

</div>

Everyone is responsible for success, yet no single person can authorise the changes needed to restore feasibility.

This creates a predictable behavioural pattern. Teams continue producing reports, updating plans and working harder because those actions remain within their authority. The decisions that would genuinely improve the situation—reducing scope, extending time, reallocating resources or cancelling work—remain outside anyone's practical control.

From a systems viewpoint, this is not primarily a motivation problem. It is a governance bottleneck.

## Recovery often depends on changing the governance, not just the plan

Major recovery efforts frequently begin with changes to decision structures rather than changes to technical work.

Recovery programmes commonly introduce:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* a single accountable executive sponsor;
* shorter escalation paths;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://pmexams.com/pmi-pmp/process/governance/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmexams.com">[pmexams.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmexams.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Establishing the Project Governance Structure13 Apr 2026 — Learn how to define decision rights, roles, and escalation paths (including sp...</span></span></span>
* authority delegated to a recovery leader;
* more frequent governance reviews focused on decisions rather than reporting;
* explicit ownership of cross-organisational conflicts.

</div>

The objective is not to centralise every decision but to ensure that decisions affecting feasibility are made quickly enough to influence [outcomes]({{ 'outcomes/' | relative_url }}).

Guidance on project recovery similarly recommends streamlining governance, clarifying escalation paths and empowering leaders to make time-sensitive trade-offs rather than allowing approval processes to become another source of delay.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://plprojects.co.uk/effective-strategies-for-project-recovery-how-to-get-project-back-on-track/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: plprojects.co.uk">[PL Projects]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">plprojects.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PL ProjectsEffective Strategies for Project Recovery: How to Get Your...June 9, 2025 — 9 Jun 2025 — Decision-Making Authority: Streamlin...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 9, 2025</span></span></span>

## Decision rights as protection against optimism bias

One reason governance matters is that organisations naturally resist acknowledging that plans have become unrealistic. The National Audit Office has repeatedly identified optimism bias, weak challenge and delayed intervention as recurring causes of poor project performance. Governance is intended to counter these tendencies by assigning people both the authority and the obligation to challenge assumptions before failure becomes irreversible. National Audit Office (NAO)+2National Audit Office (NAO)<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nao.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2013/12/10320-001-Over-optimism-in-government-projects.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nao.org.uk">[nao.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nao.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Decision- makers should be intolerant of optimism bias in the planning and delivery of...Read more...</span></span></span>

This changes the purpose of governance meetings. Instead of asking whether the project appears to be progressing, leaders ask whether the current plan remains credible and, if not, who has the authority to change it today.

## What good decision rights look like

Projects are more likely to recover when governance answers a small number of questions unambiguously:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* Who can approve a revised completion date?
* Who can remove or defer requirements?
* Who resolves conflicts between departments?
* Who authorises additional funding or resources?
* Who can pause or terminate the project if recovery is no longer justified?
* What evidence automatically triggers these decisions?

</div>

When these questions have clear answers before a crisis develops, warning signs become actionable rather than merely informative. Recovery is no longer dependent on persuasion, organisational politics or repeated requests for approval. Instead, the governance system itself creates a route from recognising reality to acting on it, making project recovery a matter of timely decision-making rather than prolonged hope.
