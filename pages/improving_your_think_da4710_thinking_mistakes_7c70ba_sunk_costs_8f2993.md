## Introduction

Sunk-cost thinking is the mistake of allowing past investments of time, money, effort or reputation to influence a decision that should depend only on expected future costs and benefits. Once those earlier investments cannot be recovered, they are economically irrelevant. Yet they often become psychologically dominant. This is why organisations continue funding failing projects, managers double down after disappointing results, and individuals remain committed to careers, businesses or relationships that no longer justify further investment. The danger is not simply wasting what has already been spent; it is allowing yesterday's losses to create tomorrow's losses as well. Research on the sunk-cost effect and escalation of commitment shows that this pattern is widespread across laboratory experiments, business decisions and public policy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/4812596_The_psychology_of_sunk_cost" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) The psychology of sunk costThe sunk cost effect is manifested in a greater tendency to continue an endeavor once an inv...</span></span></span>

## Why unrecoverable investments feel relevant

A sunk cost is any cost that cannot be recovered regardless of what happens next. Rationally, whether a project has already consumed £10,000 or £10 million should not affect the decision to continue if those costs are gone either way. The only relevant question is whether investing additional resources is expected to create more value than stopping.

People rarely experience the decision this way. Instead, abandoning a project often feels like making the previous investment "count for nothing". This creates an emotional [pressure]({{ 'pressure/' | relative_url }}) to continue, even when the objective evidence has turned negative. Arkes and Blumer's classic experiments demonstrated that people become more willing to continue an activity after investing more money, effort or time, even though those previous investments cannot be recovered. They argued that much of this behaviour reflects a desire to avoid appearing wasteful rather than careful evaluation of future [outcomes]({{ 'outcomes/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/4812596_The_psychology_of_sunk_cost" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) The psychology of sunk costThe sunk cost effect is manifested in a greater tendency to continue an endeavor once an inv...</span></span></span>

Several psychological forces reinforce this tendency:

* **Loss aversion.** Accepting a definite loss often feels worse than risking a larger future loss in the hope of recovery.
* **Need for consistency.** People prefer decisions that appear consistent with earlier choices rather than admitting that circumstances have changed.
* **Self-justification.** Leaders who approved the original project may feel that cancelling it implies they made a poor decision.
* **[Identity]({{ 'identity/' | relative_url }}) and reputation.** The more public the commitment, the harder it becomes to reverse course without feeling that credibility has been damaged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5904751/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A New Inaction-Effect Perspective on the Sunk-Cost Fallacyby G Feldman · 2018 · Cited by 66 — Escalation-of-commitment situations invo...</span></span></span>

The result is that the mind quietly substitutes the wrong question—"How can we avoid wasting what we've already spent?"—for the right one: "What is the best decision from today onwards?"

## Why negative feedback often increases commitment

The most surprising feature of escalation is that evidence against a project can strengthen commitment instead of weakening it.

Research on escalation of commitment examines situations where decision-makers receive negative performance information after making an initial commitment. Rather than treating disappointing results as evidence to reconsider, many participants invest even more resources in an attempt to recover earlier losses. Meta-analyses show that escalation becomes more likely when decision-makers feel personally responsible for the original decision, when sunk costs are high, and when they believe success remains just possible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5904751/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A New Inaction-Effect Perspective on the Sunk-Cost Fallacyby G Feldman · 2018 · Cited by 66 — Escalation-of-commitment situations invo...</span></span></span>

This creates a repeating cycle:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. A project begins with reasonable expectations.
2. Results disappoint.
3. More resources are committed to "protect" the original investment.
4. New disappointments appear.
5. The increased investment makes quitting feel even harder.

</div>

Each round raises the psychological cost of stopping while lowering the objective chance of success.

Importantly, this is not simply stubbornness. Decision-makers often reinterpret new evidence to support continued investment, convince themselves that recovery is just around the corner, or emphasise how close the project seems to completion. These reactions can feel rational from inside the decision even while producing systematically worse outcomes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5904751/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A New Inaction-Effect Perspective on the Sunk-Cost Fallacyby G Feldman · 2018 · Cited by 66 — Escalation-of-commitment situations invo...</span></span></span>

## Why organisations are especially vulnerable

Projects rarely survive on one person's judgement alone. Organisations create additional pressures that can keep failing initiatives alive.

Managers may worry that cancelling a project signals poor leadership or wastes previous budget allocations. Teams that have spent years developing specialist knowledge naturally become advocates for continuing. Political incentives can also reward avoiding visible failure today even if doing so creates larger costs later.

Large infrastructure and technology projects provide well-known illustrations. The supersonic Concorde programme became so closely associated with continued investment despite deteriorating economic prospects that the phrase "Concorde fallacy" entered discussions of decision-making. Other large public projects have similarly shown how reputational concerns, political commitments and previous spending can combine to delay termination long after warning signs emerge. These examples are complex and involve strategic, political and social considerations in addition to pure sunk-cost reasoning, but they illustrate how irrecoverable investments can distort future choices.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tesi.luiss.it/41510/1/265431_CAPRINO_CHIARA.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tesi.luiss.it">[LuissThesis]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tesi.luiss.it</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ThesisThe Sunk Cost Fallacy in Decision-Making: Psychological...These examples demonstrate how decisions influenced by sunk costs a...</span></span></span>

Modern project management research therefore treats escalation as more than an individual cognitive bias. ## Future-only questions for quitting or continuing

The most effective defence is to deliberately remove the past from the decision.

Useful questions include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--benefit" markdown="1">

* **If we had not already started, would we begin this project today?**
* **Ignoring what has already been spent, is the next pound, hour or employee likely to generate enough future benefit?**
* **What evidence would convince us to stop?**
* **If another team owned this project, what advice would we give them?**
* **What alternative use of these resources offers the highest expected return?**

</div>

These questions shift attention away from recovering historical investments and towards comparing realistic future opportunities.

Another valuable [practice]({{ 'practice/' | relative_url }}) is to define exit criteria before a project begins. Predetermined milestones, performance thresholds and review dates reduce the temptation to invent new reasons for continuing after disappointing results. Independent reviews by people who were not responsible for the original decision can also reduce self-justification pressures. ## A common misunderstanding

Avoiding sunk-cost thinking does **not** mean abandoning every difficult project after early setbacks.

Many worthwhile projects require persistence because temporary losses are expected. Research, product development and long-term infrastructure frequently experience periods where progress appears disappointing before eventual success.

The critical distinction is between **temporary evidence that a sound project remains worthwhile** and **continuing solely because previous investment feels too large to abandon**.

Future evidence should determine the decision. Past expenditure should not.

When discussions repeatedly return to phrases such as "We've already spent too much to quit now," "We have to justify all this effort," or "We're too far in to stop," the conversation has probably shifted from evaluating future value to defending past choices. Recognising that shift is often the first step towards better judgement.         exactMarket = localeMarketMap[normalized];
        break;
      }
      var languageOnly = normalized.split('-')[0];
      if (!languageMarket && localeMarketMap[languageOnly]) languageMarket = localeMarketMap[languageOnly];
    }

  }

    var matches = localeMatches();
    return matches.exact || matches.language || '';
  }
  function inferFromTimezone() {
    var tz = '';
    try { tz = String(Intl.DateTimeFormat().resolvedOptions().timeZone || ''); } catch (err) {}
    if (!tz) return '';
    for (var i = 0; i< timezoneRules.length; i += 1) {
      var rule = timezoneRules[i] || {};
      try {
        if (new RegExp(rule.pattern).test(tz)) return rule.market;
      } catch (err) {}
    }
    return '';
  }

    available = Array.isArray(available) ? available : [];
    options = options || {};

    try {
      var saved = window.localStorage && localStorage.getItem(storageKey);
      if (saved && available.indexOf(saved) >= 0) return saved;
    } catch (err) {}
    var timezoneMarket = inferFromTimezone();
    if (timezoneMarket && available.indexOf(timezoneMarket) >= 0) return timezoneMarket;
    var localeMarkets = localeMatches();
    if (localeMarkets.exact && available.indexOf(localeMarkets.exact) >= 0) return localeMarkets.exact;
    if (localeMarkets.language && available.indexOf(localeMarkets.language) >= 0) return localeMarkets.language;
    if (options.defaultMarket && available.indexOf(options.defaultMarket) >= 0) return options.defaultMarket;
    return available[0] || '';
  }

    normalize: normalize,
    inferMarket: inferMarket,
    inferFromLocale: inferFromLocale,
    inferFromTimezone: inferFromTimezone
  };
