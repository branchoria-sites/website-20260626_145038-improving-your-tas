## Introduction

A personal decision map is most useful not when it confirms what you already think, but when it reveals what you do not yet know. In argument mapping, a decision often appears stronger once its reasons are written down. Yet the real value comes from identifying unsupported [claims]({{ 'claims/' | relative_url }}), hidden [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}), and missing facts that must be checked before confidence is justified. A visible map can expose the gap between “I feel ready to decide” and “I have enough evidence to decide well”.

Research on argument mapping consistently shows that making reasoning explicit improves critical thinking because it forces claims and supporting evidence into a structure that can be examined rather than merely felt. At the same time, studies of confidence and decision-making show that people frequently become confident before gathering all the information that would improve judgement. The combination makes evidence gaps especially important: they are the places where additional checking could materially change a decision.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://web-archive.southampton.ac.uk/cogprints.org/3008/1/reasonpaper.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: web-archive.southampton.ac.uk">[University of Southampton Web Archive+2AJET]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">web-archive.southampton.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Southampton Web ArchiveArgument Maps Improve Critical Thinkingby CR Twardy · Cited by 356 — Computer-based argument mapping...</span></span></span>

## What Your Decision Map Says to Check Next

When people map a decision, they often discover that some reasons are supported by evidence while others are merely beliefs, impressions, or expectations. The map becomes a guide to future information gathering.

Consider a simple decision: “I should buy this house.” Supporting reasons might include affordability, location, investment potential, and quality of life. Once these reasons are placed on a map, each can be inspected separately. Some branches may already have evidence, such as mortgage calculations. Others may depend on assumptions, such as expected future property values or neighbourhood development plans.

The practical question becomes: which branch contains the largest uncertainty? Instead of gathering information randomly, the map highlights the evidence gaps most likely to affect the conclusion.

This mirrors a broader principle from evidence-based decision-making: high-quality decisions depend on evidence that is relevant, reliable, and appropriately reflects uncertainty. Missing information is not evenly distributed across a decision. Some gaps are trivial; others sit beneath a crucial supporting reason.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://qcmr-1.itrcweb.org/3-evidence-based-decisions/?print=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: qcmr-1.itrcweb.org">[QCMR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">qcmr-1.itrcweb.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">QCMR3 Evidence-Based DecisionsToday — Evidence fit for decision making is relevant, reliable, based on appropriate data and judgment, and...</span></span></span>

## Marking Unsupported Reasons

A useful decision map distinguishes between supported reasons and unsupported reasons.

Unsupported reasons are not necessarily wrong. They are simply claims for which adequate evidence has not yet been collected. Common examples include:

* Predictions about future [outcomes]({{ 'outcomes/' | relative_url }}).

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* Assumptions about costs or risks.
* Judgements based on limited experience.
* Generalisations from a small number of examples.
* Information heard second-hand but not verified.

</div>

One practical technique is to label each reason according to its evidence status:

StatusMeaningVerifiedSupported by direct evidence or reliable sourcesPartially supportedSome evidence exists but important questions remainUnverifiedNo meaningful evidence has been checkedContradictedAvailable evidence weakens the claim

This prevents all reasons from appearing equally strong simply because they occupy similar positions in the diagram.

Argument-mapping research emphasises that the process of linking reasons to evidence improves evaluation because it makes support relationships visible. A reason that looked persuasive in conversation can appear surprisingly weak once the supporting evidence is absent from the map. ### Hidden assumptions often create the largest gaps

Many evidence gaps are not missing facts but unstated assumptions.

For example:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* “This role will advance my career.”
* “The cheaper option will be good enough.”
* “The risk is unlikely to occur.”
* “I can adapt to the longer commute.”

</div>

Each statement may be functioning as a support for the decision without being explicitly recognised. Once written into the map, the assumption becomes a claim that can be investigated rather than silently accepted.

## Separating Confidence from Missing Evidence

One of the most valuable lessons from decision mapping is that confidence and evidence are not the same thing.

People often feel increasingly confident as they construct a coherent story around a decision. However, psychological research shows that confidence can become disconnected from accuracy. Studies of calibration—the relationship between confidence and correctness—find that better decisions emerge when confidence is aligned with the quality of available evidence rather than with the strength of a person's [intuition]({{ 'intuition/' | relative_url }}) or narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.emerald.com/md/article/61/8/2374/284010/Improving-decision-making-through-visual-knowledge" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: emerald.com">[Emerald Publishing+2Alexandria]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">emerald.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Improving decision making through visual knowledge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Emerald PublishingImproving decision making through visual knowledge calibration29 Mar 2023 — calibration helps to close the gap between...</span></span></span>

A decision map helps separate these two questions:

1. How confident am I?
2. What evidence supports that confidence?

If confidence is high but several critical branches remain unsupported, the map provides a warning signal. The appropriate response is not necessarily to delay the decision indefinitely. Instead, it is to identify which missing facts would most improve confidence calibration.

Research on information-seeking under uncertainty suggests that confidence can influence whether people search for additional information, yet people do not always seek available evidence even when uncertainty remains. Visualising evidence gaps makes those opportunities harder to ignore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A39d7574e-5e7c-4346-890a-6ebb6cc9a7a2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ora.ox.ac.uk">[Oxford University Research Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ora.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ford University Research ArchiveConfidence and information seeking in decision making under...by L Wei · 2021 · Cited by 1 — This thes...</span></span></span>

### Not every gap deserves equal attention

A common mistake is trying to eliminate all uncertainty.

Decision maps work better when they prioritise high-impact gaps. Ask:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Would resolving this uncertainty change my choice?
* Does this claim support a central branch or a peripheral one?
* Is reliable evidence reasonably available?
* What is the cost of being wrong?

</div>

A missing fact about a minor feature may not justify further investigation. A missing fact about a major financial obligation or legal risk probably does.

The goal is not perfect information. It is sufficient evidence for the importance and consequences of the decision.

Supporting reasons might include:<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.indeed.com/career-advice/starting-new-job/job-offer-considerations" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: indeed.com">[indeed.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">indeed.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">job offer considerations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">18 Important Considerations Before Accepting a Job Offer4 May 2026 — You should seriously assess a job offer before accepting it, w...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2026</span></span></span>


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--example" markdown="1">

* Higher salary.
* Better career progression.
* More interesting work.
* Flexible working arrangements.
* Strong company reputation.

</div>

At first glance, the map may look persuasive. The evidence-gap exercise begins by asking what supports each reason.

For the salary branch, the evidence check might reveal unanswered questions about bonuses, pension contributions, healthcare, annual leave, or commuting costs. Career progression may depend on promotion policies that have not been discussed. Flexible working arrangements may be based on verbal impressions rather than written terms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.indeed.com/career-advice/starting-new-job/job-offer-considerations" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: indeed.com">[Indeed+2Larson Maddox]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">indeed.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">job offer considerations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">18 Important Considerations Before Accepting a Job Offer4 May 2026 — You should seriously assess a job offer before accepting it, w...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2026</span></span></span>

A further branch might concern the legal status of the offer itself. In the UK, conditional and unconditional job offers carry different implications, and accepting an offer before understanding the conditions attached to it can introduce risks that were not represented in the original decision narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/job-offers-your-rights" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Job offers: your rights</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">September 26, 2011 — Rights of employees when accepting conditional and unconditional job offers and about breach of contract...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 26, 2011</span></span></span>

Once these gaps are visible, the map naturally generates a checklist of targeted questions:

* What exactly is included in the compensation package?
* What conditions remain before the offer becomes final?
* What are the notice and probation terms?
* How often are promotions awarded?
* What are the expected working hours in [practice]({{ 'practice/' | relative_url }})?

The decision map therefore becomes more than a diagram. It becomes an evidence-gathering plan.

## Turning Evidence Gaps into Better Decisions

The most productive decision maps are not those with the largest number of reasons. They are the ones that clearly distinguish established facts from assumptions, expectations, and unanswered questions.

A useful final review asks:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Which supporting reasons have the weakest evidence?
* Which assumptions have never been tested?
* Which missing facts could reverse the conclusion?
* Does my confidence match the actual quality of evidence?

</div>

By treating missing evidence as visible components of the map rather than as background uncertainty, decision-makers gain a clearer picture of what deserves investigation before commitment. The result is not certainty. It is a more disciplined connection between what is known, what is merely believed, and what still needs to be checked.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.emerald.com/md/article/61/8/2374/284010/Improving-decision-making-through-visual-knowledge" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: emerald.com">[Emerald Publishing+2Alexandria]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">emerald.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Improving decision making through visual knowledge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Emerald PublishingImproving decision making through visual knowledge calibration29 Mar 2023 — calibration helps to close the gap between...</span></span></span>
