## Introduction

The Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT) is designed to reveal whether people can resist a tempting [first answer]({{ 'first-answer/' | relative_url }}) and pause to check their reasoning. However, that goal becomes harder to interpret once the questions are widely known. If someone has already seen the classic [bat-and-ball]({{ 'bat-and-ball/' | relative_url }}), machines-and-widgets, or lily-pad problems, a high score may partly reflect memory rather than fresh reflection. The central issue is not cheating. It is measurement. A puzzle that once tested the ability to detect a first-answer trap may stop doing so after repeated exposure because the trap is no longer new. Research over the last decade has examined how familiarity affects CRT performance, whether memorised answers undermine the test, and how researchers can distinguish genuine reflective thinking from simple recall.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5018679/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCA limitation of the Cognitive Reflection Test: familiarity</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by S Stieger · 2016 · Cited by 134 — To solve the CRT items, one has to ignore the first intention of the System 1 processes and switc...</span></span></span>

## How Repeated Exposure Changes CRT Scores

The original CRT became one of the most widely used reasoning tasks in psychology and behavioural science. Its popularity created an unusual problem: many participants began encountering the same questions repeatedly in studies, classrooms, books, websites, and social media discussions. Researchers increasingly worried that participants might remember answers rather than solve the problems afresh.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6909056/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCUpon Repeated Reflection: Consequences of Frequent</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by JK Woike · 2019 · Cited by 24 — Repeated Exposure to the Cognitive Reflection Test. Evidence suggests that familiarity can raise scores. Studies examining self-reported prior exposure found that people who had previously seen CRT items tended to perform better than those encountering them for the first time. One analysis reported a meaningful performance advantage among familiar participants, raising concerns about whether the test was still measuring the same underlying tendency in experienced samples.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://peerj.com/articles/2395/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: peerj.com">[PeerJ]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">peerj.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">exposure] had... higher CRT scores (Cohen&#x27;s d = 0.41)&quot; (https... The effect is easiest to understand through the classic bat-and-ball question. The first time a person sees it, the intuitive answer often feels correct. After learning that the answer is actually five cents, the puzzle may never function in quite the same way again. The next encounter can become a memory retrieval task rather than a reflection task. Instead of recognising and correcting a tempting [intuition]({{ 'intuition/' | relative_url }}), the participant may simply remember a previously learned solution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sjdm.org/~baron/journal/15/151029/jdm151029.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sjdm.org">[Society for Judgment and Decision Making]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sjdm.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Although there are reasons to suspect that prior exposure to the CRT undermines the validity of the test, to our knowledge this...Read more...</span></span></span>

Yet the evidence is more nuanced than a simple claim that familiarity destroys the test. Large-scale analyses of repeated test-takers have found surprisingly small average gains from repeated exposure alone. In one study involving more than 14,000 participants, each additional exposure produced only a tiny increase in score on average. The improvement appeared to come mainly from people who continued to engage thoughtfully with the questions rather than merely seeing them again.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/judgment-and-decision-making/article/noneffects-of-repeated-exposure-to-the-cognitive-reflection-test/E0A484DEF534324055E71C251EEA1E96" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe non-effects of repeated exposure to the Cognitive...by A Meyer · 2018 · Cited by 96 — We esti...</span></span></span>

This creates an important distinction: familiarity can inflate scores, but repeated exposure does not automatically transform everyone into a high performer.

## Why Memorisation Is Not Transferable Reflection

A common misunderstanding is that learning the correct CRT answers demonstrates an improvement in reflective thinking. In reality, knowing the answer to a specific puzzle and developing a general habit of checking first impressions are different achievements.

Imagine two people who both answer the bat-and-ball problem correctly. One has never seen it before and notices that the intuitive answer creates an arithmetic contradiction. The other remembers reading the solution years earlier. Their scores are identical, but the mental processes behind those scores are very different.

This distinction matters because the broader goal of cognitive reflection is transfer. Reflective thinking is valuable when it helps someone recognise new situations in which an appealing answer might be wrong. Memorising three famous puzzles does not necessarily improve performance on unfamiliar judgement problems, probability questions, statistical [claims]({{ 'claims/' | relative_url }}), or real-world decisions. The person may have learned a fact rather than a thinking habit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sjdm.org/~baron/journal/15/151029/jdm151029.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sjdm.org">[Society for Judgment and Decision Making]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sjdm.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Although there are reasons to suspect that prior exposure to the CRT undermines the validity of the test, to our knowledge this...Read more...</span></span></span>

Researchers therefore distinguish between:

* **Item knowledge**, where a person remembers a specific answer.
* **Reflective disposition**, where a person habitually questions intuitive responses.
* **Reasoning skill**, where a person can work through unfamiliar problems successfully.

This concern became significant enough that researchers began developing alternative versions of the CRT. ## The Debate: Does Familiarity Ruin the CRT?

The research literature contains two positions that are often presented as contradictory but are actually addressing different questions.

One line of research argues that familiarity threatens validity because prior exposure increases scores. If familiar participants systematically outperform unfamiliar participants, then raw scores may no longer provide a clean measure of spontaneous reflection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://peerj.com/articles/2395/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: peerj.com">[PeerJ+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">peerj.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">exposure] had... higher CRT scores (Cohen&#x27;s d = 0.41)&quot; (https... A limitation of the Cognitive Reflection Test: familiarity. Research...</span></span></span>

Another line of research argues that the CRT remains surprisingly useful even when participants have seen it before. Studies examining correlations between CRT scores and other psychological traits found that many of the relationships associated with the test remained stable despite prior exposure. In other words, familiar participants often still differed from less reflective participants in predictable ways.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28849403/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The cognitive reflection test is robust to multiple exposuresby M Bialek · 2018 · Cited by 234 — The cognitive reflection test (CRT...</span></span></span>

These findings can coexist. Familiarity may reduce the purity of the score while leaving much of the test's predictive value intact. A remembered answer can inflate performance on a specific item, yet individuals who score well may still differ systematically from those who score poorly on broader measures of analytical thinking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28849403/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The cognitive reflection test is robust to multiple exposuresby M Bialek · 2018 · Cited by 234 — The cognitive reflection test (CRT...</span></span></span>

For readers interested in improving their thinking, the practical lesson is straightforward: getting the right answer to a familiar CRT puzzle should not be treated as evidence that [first-answer traps]({{ 'first-answers/' | relative_url }}) have been mastered in general.

## Designing Checks for Unfamiliar Traps

Because familiarity is now a recognised limitation, researchers increasingly use strategies that reduce the influence of memorisation.

One approach is to create alternative CRT versions using different surface details and different correct answers. If participants cannot rely on memory, they must engage with the underlying reasoning process again. Several expanded and revised CRT forms were developed partly for this purpose.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/297046436_Investigating_an_alternate_form_of_the_cognitive_reflection_test" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CRT-2 questions appear to rely less on numeracy than the original CRT but appear to measure closely related constructs in...Read more...</span></span></span>

Another approach is to examine reasoning across multiple unfamiliar tasks rather than relying on a single well-known puzzle. For personal development, a useful self-test is not whether you know the answer to the bat-and-ball question. A better question is whether you notice the feeling that accompanies many first-answer traps: the sense that an answer arrived quickly and therefore seems trustworthy. Reflection begins when that feeling triggers a verification step rather than immediate acceptance.

The most valuable skill is therefore not remembering famous CRT solutions. It is learning to recognise when a problem deserves a second look, especially when the problem is unfamiliar and no memorised answer is available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13546783.2022.2075035" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Taylor &amp; Francis OnlineElaborative feedback and instruction improve cognitive...by DP Calvillo · 2023 · Cited by 14 — The cognitive refl...</span></span></span>
