## Introduction

[Active learning]({{ 'active-learning/' | relative_url }}) becomes genuinely valuable at work when it rehearses the decisions employees will later have to make under real constraints. Reading procedures, attending briefings and completing compliance modules can increase [familiarity]({{ 'familiarity/' | relative_url }}), but they do not prove that people can recognise a situation, weigh competing evidence and act appropriately when time is limited, information is incomplete or consequences are significant. The strongest workplace learning therefore shifts from information delivery to structured decision rehearsal: practising the judgement itself rather than merely reviewing the rules. Research from organisational psychology, naturalistic decision-making and simulation-based learning consistently shows that realistic practice, combined with structured reflection, improves the transfer of knowledge into performance more effectively than passive instruction alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6020850/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Naturalistic Decision Making in After-Action Review Meetingsby R Reiter-Palmon · 2014 · Cited by 71 — The purpose of this study was to...</span></span></span>

## Why briefings do not prove usable skill

Many organisations still equate training with exposure to information. Employees attend presentations, read policy documents or complete online modules, then are assumed to be prepared. The weakness is that recognising information is different from applying it in an ambiguous situation.

Real workplace decisions rarely resemble textbook examples. A project manager must decide whether a schedule slip justifies escalation. A customer-service adviser must distinguish between a difficult client and a genuine safeguarding concern. A cybersecurity analyst must decide whether unusual network activity represents a harmless anomaly or the beginning of an attack. None of these judgements depends solely on remembering facts. They require recognising patterns, weighing uncertainty and anticipating consequences.

Research on naturalistic decision-making emerged precisely because laboratory decision models often failed to explain how experienced professionals actually work. Studies of firefighters, military commanders, emergency clinicians and other experts found that they frequently identify workable options by recognising familiar patterns and mentally simulating likely [outcomes]({{ 'outcomes/' | relative_url }}) instead of comparing long lists of alternatives. Such expertise develops through repeated exposure to realistic cases rather than repeated exposure to written information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/235188278_Investigations_of_Naturalistic_Decision_Making_and_the_Recognition-Primed_Decision_Model" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate+2Sage Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Investigations of Naturalistic Decision Making and the...March 1, 1996 — 25 Nov 2015 — This monograph reviews 3 years of res...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1, 1996</span></span></span>

This helps explain why workplace learning often disappoints. Employees may pass a knowledge assessment immediately after training yet struggle months later because they never rehearsed recognising when the knowledge should be applied.

## Why realistic rehearsal changes judgement

Decision rehearsal works because it strengthens several abilities simultaneously.

* **Pattern recognition:** learners begin noticing cues that distinguish routine situations from exceptional ones.
* **Mental simulation:** they practise imagining how different actions might unfold before committing to one.
* **Calibration:** they discover where confidence exceeds competence because scenarios expose [mistakes]({{ 'mistakes/' | relative_url }}) safely.
* **Shared judgement:** teams develop common expectations about what good decisions look like under pressure.

Simulation-based professional education repeatedly finds that learners benefit from making realistic decisions, receiving immediate feedback and repeating challenging situations until sound judgement becomes more automatic. The value lies not simply in "learning by doing" but in practising the same cognitive work required during real operations. Importantly, the rehearsal should preserve uncertainty. If every exercise has one obvious correct answer, participants learn to identify the trainer's preferred response rather than improving real judgement.

## Premortems, incident reviews and case classification

### Premortems: rehearsing failure before it happens

One of the simplest ways to rehearse judgement is the premortem, developed by Gary Klein. Instead of asking, "What could go wrong?", participants imagine that a project has already failed and independently write down the reasons why.

This subtle change matters because it reduces social pressure to appear optimistic and encourages people to voice concerns they might otherwise suppress. Klein linked the technique to research on "prospective hindsight", which found that imagining an event has already occurred improves people's ability to identify plausible causes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hbr.org">[Harvard Business Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hbr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">He is the author</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Harvard Business ReviewPerforming a Project PremortemSeptember 1, 2007 — Gary Klein (gary@decisionmaking.com) is the chief scientist of K...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 1, 2007</span></span></span>

Premortems do more than generate risk lists. They rehearse an important workplace judgement: identifying weak signals before commitment makes changing course politically difficult.

### Incident reviews that improve future decisions

Post-incident reviews often become exercises in assigning blame or documenting compliance. Effective reviews instead reconstruct the decisions that participants faced using only the information available at the time.

The goal is not to ask why people failed to [predict]({{ 'predict/' | relative_url }}) an outcome that is now obvious, but how the situation appeared before the result was known. This distinction reduces hindsight bias and produces lessons that transfer to future situations.

Research on After Action Reviews (AARs) shows that structured discussion of what happened, why it happened and what should change can improve future decision-making, particularly when participants openly discuss reasoning rather than merely listing procedural errors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6020850/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Naturalistic Decision Making in After-Action Review Meetingsby R Reiter-Palmon · 2014 · Cited by 71 — The purpose of this study was to...</span></span></span>

### Case classification builds better recognition

Another overlooked form of rehearsal involves comparing multiple cases instead of studying each individually.

For example, fraud investigators might classify previous incidents by behavioural warning signs rather than by financial loss. Engineers may group equipment failures according to underlying mechanisms instead of component type. HR teams might sort employee relations cases by patterns of evidence rather than legal category.

Classification forces learners to identify diagnostic features that separate superficially similar situations. This mirrors real work, where the first challenge is often recognising which kind of problem is actually present.

## Designing practice around real workplace decisions

Effective workplace rehearsal begins by identifying the decisions that genuinely determine organisational performance rather than the information employees are expected to remember.

A useful design process typically follows this sequence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Identify high-value decisions.** Focus on recurring judgements with meaningful consequences, such as approving expenditure, diagnosing faults, responding to complaints or escalating risks.
2. **Collect authentic cases.** Use real incidents, anonymised where necessary, instead of invented examples whenever possible.
3. **Preserve ambiguity.** Include incomplete information, conflicting evidence and realistic time constraints.
4. **Require explicit reasoning.** Ask participants to explain not only what they chose but why.
5. **Provide expert comparison.** Feedback should compare reasoning processes, not simply reveal the correct answer.
6. **Repeat with variation.** Similar decisions presented in different contexts strengthen transferable judgement rather than memorised responses.

</div>

This design reflects [deliberate practice]({{ 'deliberate-practice/' | relative_url }}) principles: repeated performance of clearly defined tasks with immediate feedback and opportunities for correction rather than passive review alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/168641528/1-s2.0-S1876139924000768-main.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk">[Northumbria University Research Portal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Northumbria University Research PortalAn Exploration of the Use of Deliberate Practice in Team-...by A Platt · 2024 · Cited by 3 — An in...</span></span></span>

## Historical shift from instruction to judgement

Traditional industrial training frequently reflected manufacturing logic: standardise procedures, document processes and teach employees to follow them consistently.

As work became more knowledge-intensive, organisations increasingly recognised that procedures alone cannot anticipate every situation. Aviation, healthcare, emergency response and military organisations were among the first to formalise simulation, scenario exercises and structured debriefing because many critical failures stemmed from judgement rather than lack of procedural knowledge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1555343418773915" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[Sage Journals+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sage JournalsNaturalistic Decision Making and the Practice of Health Care16 Aug 2018 — A recent essay in this journal identified health c...</span></span></span>

Today, similar ideas have spread into project management, software engineering, finance and human resources. Evidence-based management emphasises combining research evidence, organisational data, professional expertise and stakeholder perspectives instead of relying on habit or fashionable management practices. Decision rehearsal complements this approach by giving teams repeated opportunities to practise using evidence before important choices arise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cipd.org/uk/knowledge/factsheets/evidence-based-practice-factsheet/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cipd.org">[CIPD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cipd.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Evidence-based practice for effective decision-making</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Evidence-based practice for effective decision-makingMay 19, 2025 — Evidence-based practice is an approach for improving decision-mak...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 19, 2025</span></span></span>

## Common mistakes when rehearsing workplace decisions

Organisations often undermine decision rehearsal by treating it as another presentation rather than a practice session.

Common problems include:

* **Overly tidy scenarios** where the correct answer becomes obvious within minutes.
* **Exercises without feedback**, leaving participants unsure why one judgement was stronger than another.
* **Punishing mistakes**, which encourages defensive thinking instead of experimentation.
* **Measuring [recall]({{ 'recall/' | relative_url }}) instead of reasoning**, rewarding memorisation rather than judgement quality.
* **Separating training from operational reality**, so scenarios fail to resemble the decisions employees actually face.

A rehearsal succeeds when participants leave with better judgement, not merely higher confidence.

## What good workplace rehearsal ultimately develops

The most valuable outcome is not faster answers but better calibrated thinking. Employees become more skilled at recognising when a familiar solution fits, when assumptions should be questioned and when additional evidence is needed before acting.

Within an active-learning approach, workplace rehearsal therefore serves a distinctive purpose. It converts abstract knowledge into practical judgement by repeatedly placing learners in situations that resemble the choices they will eventually encounter. Briefings may introduce concepts, but carefully designed decision practice develops the ability to use those concepts when uncertainty, time pressure and organisational consequences make good thinking matter most.
