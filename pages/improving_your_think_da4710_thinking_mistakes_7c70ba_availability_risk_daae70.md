## Introduction

When people judge risk, they rarely calculate [probabilities]({{ 'probabilities/' | relative_url }}) from scratch. Instead, they often ask themselves a simpler question: *How easily can I think of an example?* If a dramatic event comes to mind immediately, it feels common. If ordinary events leave little impression, they feel rare or unimportant. This mental shortcut, known as the **availability heuristic**, is often useful because frequent events are genuinely easier to remember. However, it also produces predictable errors when memorable stories replace actual frequencies or base rates. The result is that rare but vivid dangers can appear more threatening than common but less newsworthy risks, affecting decisions in everyday life, work, travel and public policy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://people.umass.edu/biep540w/pdf/Tversky%20availability.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: people.umass.edu">[UMass People]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">people.umass.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tversky availability</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UMass PeopleAvailability: A heuristic for judging frequency and probabilityby A Tversky · Cited by 17494 — Availability is an ecologicall...</span></span></span>

## Why memorable stories feel more convincing than statistics

The availability heuristic was first described by psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman as a way people estimate frequency and probability by relying on examples that come readily to mind. In many situations this shortcut is efficient because repeated experiences are easier to [recall]({{ 'recall/' | relative_url }}). Problems arise when ease of recall is driven not by frequency but by emotional intensity, novelty, media attention or personal experience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://people.umass.edu/biep540w/pdf/Tversky%20availability.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: people.umass.edu">[UMass People]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">people.umass.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tversky availability</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UMass PeopleAvailability: A heuristic for judging frequency and probabilityby A Tversky · Cited by 17494 — Availability is an ecologicall...</span></span></span>

A vivid story has several advantages over a table of numbers:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* It is easier to imagine.
* It often includes identifiable people rather than anonymous statistics.
* It evokes emotion, making the memory stronger.
* It is frequently repeated by news media and social networks.
* It fits naturally into conversations, allowing it to spread further.

</div>

Because memory [retrieval]({{ 'retrieval/' | relative_url }}) feels effortless, the brain mistakenly treats that ease as evidence that the event itself is common. In other words, the *accessibility* of an example is confused with its *probability*.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://people.umass.edu/biep540w/pdf/Tversky%20availability.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: people.umass.edu">[UMass People]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">people.umass.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tversky availability</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UMass PeopleAvailability: A heuristic for judging frequency and probabilityby A Tversky · Cited by 17494 — Availability is an ecologicall...</span></span></span>

### Why boring denominators disappear

Risk is always a comparison between a numerator and a denominator. Hearing that ten people experienced an unusual side effect sounds alarming until it is compared with ten million people who did not.

Unfortunately, denominators are rarely memorable. Human memory stores stories more effectively than proportions. A single dramatic account of an aircraft emergency can overshadow millions of uneventful flights, while thousands of safe daily journeys by car rarely become memorable because nothing unusual happened.

This imbalance means that people often remember the exceptional event but forget the population from which it came. The result is systematic neglect of base rates—the ordinary frequencies needed to judge risk accurately.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://people.umass.edu/biep540w/pdf/Tversky%20availability.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: people.umass.edu">[UMass People]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">people.umass.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tversky availability</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UMass PeopleAvailability: A heuristic for judging frequency and probabilityby A Tversky · Cited by 17494 — Availability is an ecologicall...</span></span></span>

## How vivid examples distort risk in everyday decisions

### At work

Managers may overestimate the chance of a cyberattack after a highly publicised breach while underinvesting in more common operational failures such as routine human error, poor documentation or supplier disruptions.

Recruiters may also remember one spectacular hiring mistake and become excessively cautious about candidates who resemble that case, despite broader hiring evidence suggesting otherwise.

### During travel

Many people report greater fear of flying than driving after seeing extensive television coverage of an aviation accident. Commercial aircraft accidents receive intense international attention because they are unusual and dramatic, whereas road collisions occur individually, receive less coverage and therefore feel less psychologically available despite their far greater frequency.

The issue is not that aircraft accidents are unimportant. Rather, the amount of attention they receive is a poor guide to their statistical likelihood.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3563507/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Perception of riskby P Slovic · 1987 · Cited by 15680 — This research aims to aid risk analysis and policy-making by providing a ba...</span></span></span>

### In politics and public debate

Political campaigns frequently rely on memorable individual cases rather than representative evidence. One shocking crime, one striking immigration story or one exceptional welfare fraud case can dominate public discussion, even if the broader data show these cases are uncommon.

Individual stories are emotionally compelling and important in their own right, but they do not automatically represent the underlying pattern. Sound policy requires asking whether the memorable case is typical or exceptional.

## Why media coverage amplifies the effect

News organisations naturally prioritise events that are unusual, emotionally engaging and visually striking. Those are also precisely the events that become easiest to remember.

This creates a gap between the world people experience directly and the world they remember. Repeated exposure to reports of terrorism, violent crime, shark attacks or rare diseases can increase perceived prevalence even when objective risks remain very low. Meanwhile, chronic hazards such as cardiovascular disease, air pollution or everyday traffic collisions often receive less dramatic coverage despite causing far more harm overall.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3563507/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed+2Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Perception of riskby P Slovic · 1987 · Cited by 15680 — This research aims to aid risk analysis and policy-making by providing a ba...</span></span></span>

## Why personal experience can mislead

Personal experience often feels like the strongest possible evidence because it is concrete rather than abstract. Yet an individual's experience is an extremely small sample.

Someone who has experienced one burglary may conclude crime is rapidly increasing. Someone who has never encountered flooding may underestimate flood risk despite living in a vulnerable area. Neither judgement necessarily reflects the wider pattern.

The same applies in organisations. Teams often generalise from one recent success or failure without asking whether it represents a long-term trend or merely random variation.

This is one reason organisations increasingly rely on dashboards, incident databases and long-run performance measures rather than retrospective storytelling alone.

## How to collect ordinary comparison cases

The most effective defence is not trying to ignore vivid stories. Instead, place them alongside ordinary comparison cases.

Useful habits include:

* **Ask for the denominator.** Out of how many total cases did this event occur?
* **Look for the base rate first.** What usually happens before examining exceptional cases?
* **Collect several examples, not one.** Five representative cases are usually more informative than the single most memorable one.
* **Search deliberately for uneventful [outcomes]({{ 'outcomes/' | relative_url }}).** Safe flights, successful software deployments or ordinary medical recoveries rarely become headlines but often represent the overwhelming majority.
* **Compare with similar risks.** Instead of asking whether an event sounds frightening, ask whether it is more or less likely than comparable hazards.
* **Separate emotional impact from frequency.** A tragic event deserves empathy without automatically becoming evidence that it is common.

These practices do not eliminate the availability heuristic, but they reduce the chance that one exceptional example will crowd out a representative sample. ## Remember: memorable is not the same as likely

The availability heuristic is powerful because memory evolved to highlight events that are surprising, emotional or important for survival. Those priorities are valuable, but they are not the same as estimating probability.

Whenever a single striking example immediately comes to mind, it is worth asking one additional question: **Is this event easy to remember because it happens often, or because it is unusually memorable?**

That small distinction shifts attention from [anecdotes]({{ 'anecdotes/' | relative_url }}) to evidence, from isolated stories to representative comparisons, and from intuitive impressions to more reliable judgements about risk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://people.umass.edu/biep540w/pdf/Tversky%20availability.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: people.umass.edu">[UMass People+2PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">people.umass.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tversky availability</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UMass PeopleAvailability: A heuristic for judging frequency and probabilityby A Tversky · Cited by 17494 — Availability is an ecologicall...</span></span></span>
