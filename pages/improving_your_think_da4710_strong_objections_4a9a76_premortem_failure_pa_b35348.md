## Introduction

A pre-mortem is a structured decision-making exercise that asks a team to imagine that a plan has already failed and then [explain]({{ 'explain/' | relative_url }}), in detail, why it failed. Rather than asking, “What might go wrong?”, participants assume failure is a fact and work backwards to identify its causes. This small shift in perspective often makes it easier to notice overlooked risks, challenge optimistic [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}), and raise concerns that might otherwise remain unspoken. The technique is especially valuable before launching products, policies, major projects, or strategic decisions, when changing course is still relatively inexpensive. Rather than replacing ordinary risk analysis, a pre-mortem strengthens it by exposing hidden failure paths before they become real.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Decide which risks deserve preventive action, additional evidence or revised assumptions before proceeding. Harvard Business Review+2strategyunitwm.nhs.uk">[Harvard Business Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Decide which risks deserve preventive action, additional evidence or revised assumptions before proceeding. ## Why imagine failure before anything has gone wrong?

Many planning discussions suffer from a predictable imbalance. Teams devote most of their energy to explaining why a plan will succeed, while treating doubts as obstacles to overcome rather than evidence to investigate. Optimism, commitment to previous work, and social pressure can all make criticism feel disloyal, particularly when senior people have already expressed enthusiasm.

A pre-mortem changes the social rules. Instead of asking participants to attack the plan directly, it tells them that the failure has already happened. Their task is no longer to oppose colleagues but to explain an established outcome. Gary Klein, who developed the method, argued that this legitimises concerns that knowledgeable people often hesitate to voice during conventional planning meetings. Daniel Kahneman has similarly described the technique as valuable because it gives doubt an accepted place in the decision process rather than forcing it to compete with group optimism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Decide which risks deserve preventive action, additional evidence or revised assumptions before proceeding. Harvard Business Review+2strategyunitwm.nhs.uk">[Harvard Business Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Decide which risks deserve preventive action, additional evidence or revised assumptions before proceeding. Harvard Business Review+2strategyunitwm.nhs.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hbr.org.</span></span></span>

The psychological mechanism is often described as **prospective hindsight**: mentally placing yourself in a future where the outcome is already known, then reasoning backwards to discover its causes. Earlier research on prospective hindsight found that this perspective improves people's ability to generate plausible explanations for future events compared with ordinary forecasting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-mortem" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

## Turn confidence into a failure story

A useful pre-mortem is deliberately concrete. Participants should imagine that the launch, investment, policy or project failed spectacularly six or twelve months later. They then write down specific reasons for that failure before discussion begins.

The initial period of independent writing is important. If discussion starts immediately, early speakers often shape everyone else's thinking. Individual reflection allows quieter participants and technical specialists to identify risks that dominant voices might overlook.

The resulting list should describe mechanisms rather than vague anxieties. Compare these examples:

* **Weak:** "Customers might not like it."
* **Better:** "Early customers misunderstand the pricing, producing negative reviews that damage trust before customer support has prepared standard responses."

The second explanation points directly to something that can be investigated or tested.

## Collect risks people avoid saying aloud

One of the greatest strengths of a pre-mortem is not simply generating more risks but uncovering the kinds of concerns people often suppress.

These commonly include:

* Assumptions that everyone privately doubts but believes others accept.
* Political concerns about challenging senior leaders.
* Operational weaknesses that appear embarrassing.
* Dependencies on suppliers, regulators or partners that receive too little attention.
* Overly optimistic schedules created by external pressure rather than realistic estimates.

Research and practitioner experience repeatedly suggest that teams frequently possess scattered fragments of warning information before failures occur. The difficulty is often not ignorance but reluctance to assemble those fragments into an explicit challenge to the preferred plan. A pre-mortem temporarily changes the incentive structure by making criticism part of the assigned task rather than an act of opposition. [Harvard Business Review+2CIPD](https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem)

This depends on psychological safety—the shared expectation that people can raise concerns without punishment or ridicule. Without that environment, participants may still censor themselves even when asked to imagine failure. The exercise therefore works best when leaders visibly welcome uncomfortable observations instead of immediately defending the existing plan. [CIPD](https://www.cipd.org/globalassets/media/knowledge/knowledge-hub/evidence-reviews/2024-pdfs/8542-psych-safety-trust-practice-summary.pdf)

## Convert failure paths into tests

The value of a pre-mortem lies in what happens afterwards. Producing a long list of possible disasters is useful only if those scenarios become practical changes.

For each identified failure path, ask:

* Which assumption would have to be true for this failure to occur?
* Can we test that assumption before launch?
* Can we reduce the likelihood of this failure?
* Can we reduce its consequences if it happens anyway?
* What evidence would convince us to delay or redesign the plan?

## Common mistakes that weaken pre-mortems

Although simple, the technique is often applied poorly.

A frequent mistake is treating the exercise as a routine brainstorming session. If participants merely list generic risks such as "budget problems" or "poor communication", they gain little beyond an ordinary project review.

Another mistake is allowing managers to rebut each concern immediately. Once people see objections being defended against rather than investigated, they quickly stop offering their strongest reservations.

Teams also weaken the exercise by attempting to eliminate every conceivable risk. A pre-mortem is not intended to produce exhaustive catalogues of unlikely scenarios. Its purpose is to identify the few realistic failure mechanisms that deserve immediate attention before commitment.

Finally, organisations sometimes conduct a pre-mortem because governance procedures require it, then proceed without modifying the plan. The exercise only improves decisions if identified risks alter priorities, testing, sequencing or launch criteria.

## Where pre-mortems are most valuable

Pre-mortems are particularly useful when:

* Decisions are expensive to reverse.
* Teams are highly confident or enthusiastic.
* Senior leaders have publicly endorsed the proposal.
* Projects involve substantial uncertainty rather than routine execution.
* Launches depend upon several assumptions succeeding simultaneously.

They are less valuable for highly routine activities where risks are already well understood and standard operating procedures capture previous experience. In those situations, conventional [checklists]({{ 'checklists/' | relative_url }}) may provide greater benefit than speculative failure analysis.

## How pre-mortems complement other decision tools

A pre-mortem should not replace forecasting, risk registers or post-project reviews. Each answers a different question.

* **Forecasting** estimates what is likely to happen.
* **Risk registers** catalogue known threats and assign owners.
* **Pre-mortems** generate hidden failure mechanisms before commitment.
* **Post-mortems** explain failures after events have occurred.

Used together, these methods create a learning cycle. Forecasts establish expectations, pre-mortems challenge assumptions before action, implementation tests the revised plan, and post-mortems improve future judgement by comparing expectations with reality.

Within the broader discipline of improving analytical thinking, the distinctive contribution of the pre-mortem is that it creates an organised moment when strong objections are expected rather than resisted. By imagining that failure has already happened, teams often discover weaknesses while they are still inexpensive—and politically possible—to fix.
