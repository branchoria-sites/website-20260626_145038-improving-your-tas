## Introduction

The Good Judgment Project showed that better judgement is not mainly about having exceptional intelligence or privileged information. Instead, it comes from making explicit probabilistic forecasts, updating them as evidence changes, and learning systematically from [feedback]({{ 'feedback/' | relative_url }}). Originally developed as part of a large forecasting tournament for the US intelligence community, the project demonstrated that ordinary people could substantially improve their predictive accuracy through training, disciplined thinking, teamwork, and careful measurement. These lessons translate surprisingly well to everyday decisions, from planning projects and assessing business risks to judging whether a news story will develop as expected. Rather than trying to [predict]({{ 'predict/' | relative_url }}) everything perfectly, the project teaches people how to become better calibrated: more confident when the evidence is strong, less certain when it is weak, and quicker to revise beliefs when reality changes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://web.stanford.edu/~knutson/jdm/mellers15.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: web.stanford.edu">[Stanford University+2iarpa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">web.stanford.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Community sponsored a series of forecasting tournaments designed to explore the best... ## Why forecasting tournaments matter

The Good Judgment Project emerged from the Intelligence Advanced Research Projects Activity (IARPA) Aggregative Contingent Estimation (ACE) programme, which asked competing research teams to improve geopolitical forecasting. Participants answered hundreds of clearly defined questions such as whether a country would hold an election by a particular date or whether a political agreement would be reached within a specified period. Every forecast received a numerical probability and was later scored against reality.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.iarpa.gov/research-programs/ace" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iarpa.gov">[iarpa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iarpa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ACEThe goal of the ACE Program is to dramatically enhance the accuracy, precision, and timeliness of intelligence forecasts for a broad r...</span></span></span>

This structure matters because it removes many of the excuses that normally protect poor judgement. Forecasts must be:

* specific enough to be resolved;
* probabilistic rather than vague;
* made before [outcomes]({{ 'outcomes/' | relative_url }}) are known;
* scored objectively after the event.

Unlike ordinary opinions, forecasts cannot quietly evolve in memory after the fact. The continual cycle of prediction, outcome and feedback allows genuine improvement.

Perhaps the project's most important finding was that forecasting ability varied far more than many researchers expected. Some participants consistently outperformed both experts and other volunteers over multiple years. These "superforecasters" were not simply lucky. Their advantage persisted across different questions and forecasting seasons, suggesting that better judgement reflected repeatable habits rather than isolated successes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://web.stanford.edu/~knutson/jdm/mellers15.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: web.stanford.edu">[Stanford University+2Wharton Faculty Platform]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">web.stanford.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Community sponsored a series of forecasting tournaments designed to explore the best... ## Training, updating, teamwork, and aggregation

The Good Judgment Project identified several practical behaviours that repeatedly distinguished stronger forecasters. None require participation in a forecasting tournament.

### Think in probabilities, not certainties

Everyday conversations encourage words such as "probably", "unlikely" or "almost certain". Superforecasters instead attached explicit numerical [probabilities]({{ 'probabilities/' | relative_url }}) to [claims]({{ 'claims/' | relative_url }}).

Doing so forces clearer thinking. Saying there is a 65% chance that a supplier will deliver on time communicates much more information than simply saying the delivery is "likely". It also makes later evaluation possible.

Repeated use of numerical probabilities gradually improves calibration because forecasters discover whether their 70% predictions really occur about seven times out of ten.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://web.stanford.edu/~knutson/jdm/mellers15.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: web.stanford.edu">[Stanford University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">web.stanford.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Community sponsored a series of forecasting tournaments designed to explore the best... ### Update beliefs frequently

One of the strongest findings from the project was that successful forecasters revised their estimates more often than weaker participants.

Updating did not mean constantly changing one's mind for trivial reasons. Instead, it meant responding proportionately to genuinely new evidence. Rather than treating an initial prediction as something to defend, superforecasters viewed it as a temporary estimate that should improve over time.

This contrasts with a common psychological tendency to anchor on first impressions and adjust too little afterwards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://web.stanford.edu/~knutson/jdm/mellers15.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: web.stanford.edu">[Stanford University+2Good Judgment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">web.stanford.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Community sponsored a series of forecasting tournaments designed to explore the best... ### Break difficult questions into smaller ones

Large uncertain questions become easier when divided into manageable components.

Instead of asking:

> "Will this new product succeed?"

a forecaster might separately estimate:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* whether development finishes on schedule;
* whether regulators approve it;
* whether competitors launch alternatives;
* whether customer demand reaches a specified level.

</div>

Thinking through each component often produces better overall judgement than relying on [intuition]({{ 'intuition/' | relative_url }}) about the whole problem. This "outside view plus inside view" style of reasoning became a recurring theme in the project's training.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.crforum.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/2016_Schoemaker_Tetlock_Superforecasting-HBR-May-2016.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: crforum.co.uk">[Corporate Research Forum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">crforum.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Advanced Research Projects Activity tournament.Read more</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">rporate Research ForumSuperforecasting: How to Upgrade Your Company&#x27;s...by PJH Schoemaker · 2016 · Cited by 71 — The Good Judgment Pro...</span></span></span>

### Use base rates before unique stories

Superforecasters typically began with historical frequencies before considering special circumstances.

Suppose someone wants to estimate whether a software project will finish on time. A useful first question is not "How motivated is this team?" but "How often do comparable software projects finish on schedule?"

Starting with similar past cases helps prevent compelling narratives from overwhelming statistical reality. Individual details still matter, but they are interpreted relative to an evidence-based baseline rather than replacing it altogether.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://goodjudgment.com/philip-tetlocks-10-commandments-of-superforecasting/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: goodjudgment.com">[Good Judgment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">goodjudgment.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Good JudgmentTen Commandments for Aspiring SuperforecastersThese commandments describe behaviors that have been “experimentally demonstra...</span></span></span>

### Learn collaboratively without seeking consensus

An interesting finding from the Good Judgment Project was that carefully designed teamwork improved forecasting.

The most successful teams did not simply vote or compromise. Members challenged one another's assumptions, shared evidence and independently revised their probabilities. Diversity of perspective proved valuable when combined with respectful disagreement and independent judgement.

Aggregation algorithms then combined many individual forecasts, usually producing predictions that outperformed most single forecasters. This illustrates a refined version of the "wisdom of crowds": crowds become especially effective when individuals think independently and update from shared information rather than merely copying one another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://web.stanford.edu/~knutson/jdm/mellers15.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: web.stanford.edu">[Stanford University+2Wharton Faculty Platform]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">web.stanford.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Community sponsored a series of forecasting tournaments designed to explore the best... ## What individuals can borrow without a tournament

Most people do not need sophisticated software or hundreds of forecasting questions to benefit from these ideas. The habits can be incorporated into an ordinary prediction journal.

Useful questions include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Will this project finish before the deadline?
* Will inflation fall below a stated figure by year-end?
* Will I complete this course within six months?
* Will this customer renew their contract?

</div>

Each prediction should include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* a precise resolution criterion;
* a numerical probability;
* key assumptions;
* the date for review.

</div>

After the outcome becomes known, compare the prediction with reality rather than with memory.

Over time, patterns emerge. Some people discover they consistently assign probabilities that are too high. Others realise they update too slowly when new evidence arrives. These recurring errors are far more useful than isolated [mistakes]({{ 'mistakes/' | relative_url }}) because they reveal systematic biases in judgement.

## The mindset behind superforecasting

Research associated with the Good Judgment Project suggests that successful forecasters shared several psychological habits.

They tended to be intellectually curious rather than ideologically rigid. They were comfortable expressing uncertainty instead of pretending certainty. They actively searched for evidence that could prove them wrong. They separated their identity from individual predictions, making it easier to admit error without treating it as personal failure.

This combination has sometimes been described as holding strong opinions lightly: working hard to reach the best current estimate while remaining willing to revise it whenever better evidence appears.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://goodjudgment.com/philip-tetlocks-10-commandments-of-superforecasting/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: goodjudgment.com">[Good Judgment+2Esoteric Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">goodjudgment.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Good JudgmentTen Commandments for Aspiring SuperforecastersThese commandments describe behaviors that have been “experimentally demonstra...</span></span></span>

## Limits and misunderstandings

The Good Judgment Project should not be interpreted as showing that anyone can reliably predict complex events far into the future.

Several important limitations deserve attention.

First, the project focused largely on questions that were clearly defined, objectively resolvable and typically answered within months or a few years. Many everyday uncertainties are less sharply defined.

Second, forecasting skill improves decision quality but does not eliminate randomness. Even excellent forecasters make many incorrect predictions because uncertainty is inherent rather than merely a consequence of poor reasoning.

Third, some later research has questioned how easily superforecasting methods transfer to environments with limited data, smaller participant pools or rapidly changing conditions. Structured forecasting still helps, but gains may be smaller than those observed in the original large-scale tournaments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7333631/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2Stanford University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The superforecasting hypothesis is challenged under real-life scarcity</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Superforecasting reality check: Evidence from a small pool of...by I Katsagounos · 2020 · Cited by 23 — The study contributes to the...</span></span></span>

Finally, forecasting is only one part of good decision-making. A well-calibrated prediction does not determine what someone ought to do. Decisions also involve values, costs, risk tolerance and practical constraints.

## The enduring lesson for everyday thinkers

The lasting contribution of the Good Judgment Project is not that a small group of "superforecasters" possess extraordinary intuition. It is that many elements of better judgement can be learned.

For someone already keeping a prediction journal, the project offers a practical framework:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* assign explicit probabilities instead of vague language;
* define questions so outcomes are objectively resolvable;
* begin with historical base rates before unique narratives;
* revise forecasts whenever meaningful evidence appears;
* review outcomes using proper scoring rather than memory;
* discuss forecasts with others while maintaining independent judgement.

</div>

Together, these practices create a continuous learning loop.       var rule = timezoneRules[i] || {};
      try {
        if (new RegExp(rule.pattern).test(tz)) return rule.market;
      } catch (err) {}
