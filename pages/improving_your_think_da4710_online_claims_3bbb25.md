## Introduction

A strong thinker does not ask first, “Do I agree with this?” but “What does the evidence actually show?” That question is especially important for news headlines, social posts, viral screenshots, short videos and confident online [claims]({{ 'claims/' | relative_url }}), because the most shareable version of a story is often not the best-evidenced version. False or misleading information can spread quickly online; a major study of Twitter found that false news travelled farther, faster and more broadly than true news, partly because it tended to feel more novel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://news.mit.edu/2018/study-twitter-false-news-travels-faster-true-stories-0308" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.mit.edu">[MIT News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.mit.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">study twitter false news travels faster true stories 0308</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MIT NewsStudy: On Twitter, false news travels faster than true stories8 Mar 2018 — A new study by three MIT scholars has found that false...</span></span></span>

Evidence quality is not the same as source prestige, tone, popularity or visual polish. A claim is stronger when it can be traced to an identifiable original source, checked against independent evidence, placed in its proper context and corrected if new information changes the picture. This page focuses on a practical skill: learning to separate what a headline or post says from what the underlying evidence can actually support.

## Why Confident Claims Deserve a Slower Read

Online claims often arrive already packaged with certainty: a dramatic headline, a cropped chart, a short clip, a screenshot of a “document”, or a post that says “the media will not show you this”. The problem is not simply that some claims are false. It is that the surface cues people use under time [pressure]({{ 'pressure/' | relative_url }}) are often weak signals of truth. A professional-looking website can be misleading; a rough phone video can be authentic; a real image can be attached to the wrong date, place or event.

Research on digital evaluation helps [explain]({{ 'explain/' | relative_url }}) why “just read the page carefully” is not enough. In a study comparing professional fact-checkers, historians and Stanford undergraduates, fact-checkers were better at evaluating unfamiliar online sources because they left the original site quickly and looked across the web for outside evidence. The researchers called this “[lateral reading]({{ 'lateral-reading/' | relative_url }})”: checking what other reliable sources say about the source or claim, rather than staying trapped inside one polished page.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/016146811912101102" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[Sage Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sagepub.com.</span></span></span>

That matters because many ordinary readers do the opposite. They read vertically: they inspect the page’s design, scroll through its “About” section, notice official-sounding language and decide whether it feels credible. Those signals can help, but they are easy to imitate. Better thinkers treat a webpage as one piece of evidence, not as the whole courtroom.

## Source Checks: Who Is Making the Claim, and How Would They Know?

The first evidence-quality question is not “Is this source good or bad?” It is more precise: “Is this source in a position to know this particular thing?” A newspaper may be reliable on reported events but weak on a technical medical claim. A government department may be authoritative for official statistics but self-interested when defending its own performance. A social media account may have genuine footage but no reliable knowledge of what happened before or after the clip.

Useful source checks include:

* **[Identity]({{ 'identity/' | relative_url }}):** Is the author, organisation or account clearly identifiable?
* **Access:** Did they witness the event, obtain documents, analyse data, or simply repeat another post?
* **Method:** Do they explain how they know?

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **Independence:** Can the claim be confirmed outside the source’s own network?
* **Track record:** Have reputable outlets, researchers or fact-checkers previously found the source reliable or unreliable?
* **Correction behaviour:** Does the source correct mistakes visibly?

</div>

The SIFT method, developed by Mike Caulfield and widely used in digital literacy teaching, captures this habit in four moves: stop, investigate the source, find better coverage, and trace claims back to their original context. Its value is that it begins with restraint. Before arguing with a claim, sharing it, or dismissing it, the reader pauses long enough to ask what kind of evidence would settle the question.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://guides.lib.uchicago.edu/c.php?g=1241077&amp;p=9082322" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: guides.lib.uchicago.edu">[University of Chicago Library Guides]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">guides.lib.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Chicago Library Guides The SIFT Method</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chicago Library Guides The SIFT Method</span></span></span>

A source check should not become a lazy shortcut. “This outlet is usually good” is not proof that this article is right. “This account is biased” is not proof that this clip is fake. Evidence quality depends on the relationship between the claim and the support offered for it.

## Evidence Versus Headline: What Is Actually Being Proved?

Headlines compress. Social posts exaggerate. Video captions interpret. A claim can be misleading even when every visible element contains a piece of truth. The practical skill is to translate the headline into a testable claim and then compare it with the body of evidence.

For example, a headline might say that a study “proves” a food causes disease. The actual paper may show only a correlation, a small effect, a result in animals, or a finding limited to a particular group. A post might say “Video shows riots today”, while the video is real but from a different city and year. Reuters Fact Check regularly documents this kind of mismatch: authentic footage or real images are often recirculated with a false location, date or explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/fact-check/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on reuters.com.</span></span></span>

Misleading headlines matter because they shape memory and interpretation. A study on subtle misinformation in news headlines found that misleading headlines affected readers’ memory, reasoning, behavioural intentions and impressions, even when the article itself contained more nuanced information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25347407/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

This is why the headline should be treated as a claim about the evidence, not as evidence itself. The reader’s job is to ask:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* Does the article support the headline, or merely gesture towards it?
* Are the strongest words in the headline repeated in the evidence?
* Are uncertainty, caveats or limits hidden lower down?
* Does the claim depend on one anecdote, one expert, one study, or one unnamed source?
* Would the conclusion still hold if the headline were rewritten in plainer, narrower terms?

</div>

A careful reader often ends up with a smaller but truer version of the claim. “This proves corruption” may become “this raises a question about a contract award”. “Scientists say X is dangerous” may become “one early study found a possible association that needs replication”. That narrowing is not pedantry. It is the difference between reacting to a story and understanding it.

## What Good Evidence Looks Like in News and Online Claims

High-quality evidence has several features. It is traceable, relevant, proportionate, independently checkable and honestly limited. The strongest evidence does not merely sound authoritative; it lets a reader see how the conclusion was reached.

A claim about public spending is stronger when it links to budget documents, official statistics or audited accounts. A claim about a court case is stronger when it refers to filings, judgments or named legal records rather than anonymous summaries. A claim about a scientific issue is stronger when it points to peer-reviewed research, systematic reviews, or expert bodies that explain the state of the evidence. A claim about a fast-moving event is stronger when it distinguishes confirmed facts from early reports.

Good evidence also matches the scale of the claim. A single eyewitness may support “someone saw smoke near the station”; it cannot by itself support “the attack was carried out by X group”. A before-and-after photo may show visible damage; it does not automatically prove who caused it. A survey can describe what its sample said; it cannot safely stand for a whole population unless the sampling method supports that inference.

Professional fact-checking organisations make this logic visible by publishing claim-specific verdicts, methods and corrections policies. The International Fact-Checking Network’s Code of Principles stresses nonpartisanship, source transparency, transparent methodology and open corrections for fact-checking organisations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ifcncodeofprinciples.poynter.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ifcncodeofprinciples.poynter.org">[IFCN Code of Principles]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ifcncodeofprinciples.poynter.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on poynter.org.</span></span></span> Full Fact likewise describes fact-checking as a process of selecting checkable claims, examining evidence and correcting the public record when claims are wrong or misleading.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fullfact.org/how-we-fact-check/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fullfact.org">[Full Fact]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fullfact.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on fullfact.org.</span></span></span>

The lesson for ordinary readers is not that every person must become a professional investigator. It is that a claim deserves more trust when the route from evidence to conclusion is visible.

## Viral Images and Videos Need Their Own Evidence Test

Images feel direct. Video feels even more direct. That is exactly why visual claims are so powerful. But a visual item can mislead in several ways: it can be fabricated, edited, staged, cropped, captioned falsely, taken from another event, or presented without the context that would change its meaning.

Verification specialists therefore treat images and videos as sources that need checking, not as automatic proof. The House of Commons Library’s 2026 guide to verifying images and videos recommends reverse image search tools to see whether the material has appeared online before.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-10816/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commonslibrary.parliament.uk">[House of Commons Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commonslibrary.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">House of Commons Library How to verify images and videos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">House of Commons Library How to verify images and videos</span></span></span> Bellingcat’s investigative guides similarly emphasise reverse image search, geolocation, metadata where available, maps, landmarks and other external clues.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bellingcat.com/resources/how-tos/2019/12/26/guide-to-using-reverse-image-search-for-investigations/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bellingcat.com">[bellingcat]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bellingcat.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Guide To Using Reverse Image Search For Investigations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Guide To Using Reverse Image Search For Investigations</span></span></span>

A simple visual-evidence routine is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Find the earliest appearance.** Has the image or video been posted before?
2. **Check the caption.** Does the caption match the original location, date and event?
3. **Look for stable clues.** Road signs, weather, shadows, architecture, uniforms and language can help confirm or challenge the claimed context.
4. **Compare with independent reporting.** Are reputable outlets, local authorities, journalists or investigators reporting the same event?
5. **Separate authenticity from interpretation.** A real video can still be used to support a false explanation.

</div>

This distinction is increasingly important as AI-generated images and videos improve. But the older problem remains just as common: real media attached to the wrong claim. Reuters has debunked AI-generated images falsely presented as aircraft landing at a burning Beirut airport, while AP has documented fabricated and misrepresented images spreading after high-profile incidents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/fact-check/images-aircraft-landings-into-flaming-beirut-airport-are-ai-generated-2024-10-29/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Images of aircraft landings into flaming Beirut airport are AI-generated</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Images of aircraft landings into flaming Beirut airport are AI-generated</span></span></span>

## What Would Verify It?

One of the best [thinking habits]({{ 'thinking-habits/' | relative_url }}) is to ask, “What would I expect to see if this claim were true?” This turns vague scepticism into a practical test.

For a claim that a public figure said something, verification might require the full speech, transcript, uncut interview, official recording or multiple independent reporters who heard it. For a claim about crime or migration numbers, it might require official statistics, clear definitions and comparison with previous periods. For a claim about a scientific finding, it might require the study itself, expert interpretation and whether other studies point the same way. For a claim about a breaking event, it might require time-stamped footage, named witnesses, official statements and corroboration from more than one news organisation.

The point is not to demand impossible certainty before believing anything. It is to match confidence to evidence. A reader can say:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* “This is confirmed.”<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://apnews.com/article/1cd447e8963456af2be9a24d660ed1d6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[apnews.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>
* “This is plausible but not yet confirmed.”
* “This is a real document, but the interpretation is disputed.”
* “This video is authentic, but the caption is false.”
* “This claim may be true, but the source has not shown enough evidence.”
* “There is not enough public evidence to know.”

</div>

Those middle categories are where better thinking often lives. They prevent the false choice between instant belief and blanket cynicism.

## How Online Sharing Weakens Evidence Judgement

Social platforms reward speed, emotion and identity. Evidence checking rewards patience, context and proportion. That mismatch helps explain why low-quality claims travel well.

A Nature Human Behaviour study on sharing without clicking found that the spread of political content on social media, including misinformation, was driven in part by superficial processing of headlines and blurbs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39562798/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span> This fits the everyday experience of seeing people argue over an article they have not read, a screenshot without a link, or a claim whose original source has disappeared by the time it goes viral.

News consumption patterns make this problem more consequential. Ofcom’s 2024 UK news consumption report found that online sources were used by 71% of UK adults for news, roughly level with television at 70%, and that 52% of UK over-16s used social media as a news source.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/research-and-data/tv-radio-and-on-demand-research/tv-research/news/news-consumption-2024/news-consumption-in-the-uk-2024-report.pdf?v=379621" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ofcom.org.uk">[www.ofcom.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ofcom.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">news consumption in the uk 2024 report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">news consumption in the uk 2024 report</span></span></span> The Reuters Institute’s 2025 Digital News Report describes a news environment shaped by political uncertainty, platform change, fragmentation and pressure on trust.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2025/dnr-executive-summary" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk">[reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">dnr executive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">dnr executive</span></span></span>

The practical implication is simple: the reader is often not seeing “the news” in a neutral order. They are seeing selected fragments promoted by algorithms, friends, influencers, campaigns, outrage, humour and habit. Evidence quality therefore has to be checked at the claim level, not assumed from the feed.

## Common Evidence Traps

Many misleading claims do not require a completely fake story. They rely on ordinary evidence traps.

**Anecdote as proof.** A single vivid case can reveal that something happened, but not how often it happens, whether it is increasing, or what caused it.

**Real data, wrong denominator.** A number can sound alarming until the relevant population, time period or comparison group is added.

**Correlation as causation.** Two things moving together does not prove one caused the other.

**Expertise drift.** A genuine expert in one field may be quoted as though they are equally authoritative in another.

**Anonymous sourcing without support.** Anonymous sources can be legitimate in journalism, but the reader should look for whether the outlet explains why anonymity was granted and whether the claim is corroborated.

**Screenshots without originals.** A screenshot is easy to crop, fabricate or detach from context. The original document, post or archive matters.

**True quote, missing context.** A short clip or sentence may omit the question, qualification, joke, correction or surrounding discussion.

**Old material as new.** Real images from previous events are frequently recycled during crises because they look emotionally relevant.

These traps are not exotic. They are the everyday mechanics of weak evidence. A better thinker learns to recognise them before deciding how much confidence a claim deserves.

## When Fact-Checks Help, and When They Are Not Enough

Fact-checks are valuable because they slow a claim down and expose the evidence trail. Reuters, AP, Full Fact and other organisations often show the original claim, the relevant documents or media, the verification steps and the corrected interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/ap-fact-check" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News+2Full Fact]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on apnews.com.</span></span></span>

But fact-checks are not magic. They arrive after many people have already seen the claim. They may not reach the same audience. Some readers distrust the checking organisation. Some claims are too vague to check cleanly. Others concern emerging events where evidence is still incomplete. Research on fact-checking and misinformation also shows that trust in corrective labels can vary by topic and by users’ political preferences, although the presence of a fact-checking label may still discourage sharing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.00866" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

This is why evidence quality should be a reader habit, not only an institutional service. Fact-checkers can help correct the record, but the individual still needs a pre-sharing threshold: “Have I seen enough to pass this on?”

## A Practical Evidence Ladder for Everyday Claims

Not every claim deserves the same amount of work. A useful evidence ladder helps match effort to stakes.

**[Low-stakes]({{ 'low-stakes/' | relative_url }}) claim:** a harmless curiosity, joke, entertainment rumour or minor lifestyle claim. A quick source check may be enough.

**Medium-stakes claim:** a claim that could shape someone’s opinion of a person, group, product, policy or institution. Look for the original source and at least one independent account.

**High-stakes claim:** health advice, legal allegations, violence, elections, public safety, financial decisions, identity claims about suspects or victims, or anything likely to inflame hostility. Do not rely on a single post, screenshot or clip. Seek primary documents, reputable reporting, expert context and time for confirmation.

**Breaking-news claim:** treat early information as provisional. Ask whether the claim is confirmed, attributed, independently corroborated, and still current. Early reports often change because witnesses are mistaken, officials revise details, and media organisations update cautiously.

The stronger the potential consequence, the higher the evidence threshold should be. This is not about distrusting everything. It is about refusing to let urgency or emotion set the standard of proof.

## How to Read a Claim Like a Better Thinker

A compact routine can improve judgement without turning every article into a research project.

First, identify the exact claim. Strip away outrage, humour and insinuation until the claim can be tested. “They are hiding this” is not a checkable claim; “this policy caused X increase between these dates” is.

Second, locate the best available source. That may be an official dataset, court record, research paper, full video, original post, local reporting, or a specialist fact-check. The House of Commons Library describes fact-checking as following a claim back to its original source or comparing it with other sources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-10820/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commonslibrary.parliament.uk">[House of Commons Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commonslibrary.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">House of Commons Library How to check facts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">House of Commons Library How to check facts</span></span></span>

Third, compare the evidence with the wording. Does the evidence prove the claim, partly support it, weaken it, or fail to address it?

Fourth, ask what is missing. Missing context is not always deception, but it is often where the real judgement lies: sample size, date range, definitions, comparison group, uncertainty, omitted footage, or alternative explanations.

Finally, decide how confident to be. A mature answer may be “not enough evidence yet”. In public reasoning, that is often better than a fast wrong answer.

## The Real Skill: Calibrated Trust

Evidence quality is not a call to become cynical. Cynicism says nothing can be trusted. Good judgement says trust should be earned in degrees.

A reliable claim is one where the source is identifiable, the evidence is relevant, the method is visible, the context is not misleading, and independent checks point in the same direction. A weak claim may still turn out to be true, but it has not yet earned strong belief. A polished claim may still fail if its evidence is thin. A messy early report may deserve attention while remaining provisional.

This habit strengthens analytical thinking because it keeps confidence connected to reasons. Instead of asking whether a claim feels true, sounds clever, flatters your side or angers your opponents, the better question is: “What would I have to see to know?”

    var languages = [];

    var languageMarket = '';

      else if (navigator.language) languages = [navigator.language];

      if (!normalized) continue;

        break;
      }

      if (!languageMarket && localeMarketMap[languageOnly]) languageMarket = localeMarketMap[languageOnly];

        if (new RegExp(rule.pattern).test(tz)) return rule.market;
      } catch (err) {}
    }
    return '';
  }

    if (localeMarkets.language && available.indexOf(localeMarkets.language) >= 0) return localeMarkets.language;
    if (options.defaultMarket && available.indexOf(options.defaultMarket) >= 0) return options.defaultMarket;
    return available[0] || '';
