## Introduction

A decision journal is one of the simplest tools for improving judgement because it captures what you genuinely believed *before* reality revealed the answer. Instead of relying on memory—which is surprisingly easy to rewrite after the fact—you create a dated record of your reasoning, expectations and confidence. When the outcome arrives, you compare reality with your original thinking rather than with a reconstructed version of it.


<img src="{{ "/assets/images/improving_your_think_da4710_feedback_loops_judgm_3e40b7_decision_journals_44d9d9-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Decision Journals illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/improving_your_think_da4710_feedback_loops_judgm_3e40b7_decision_journals_44d9d9-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/improving_your_think_da4710_feedback_loops_judgm_3e40b7_decision_journals_44d9d9-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">
This matters because hindsight bias, often described as the "I knew it all along" effect, makes past events appear far more predictable than they really were. Once people know the outcome, they tend to remember having expected it, even when their original beliefs were quite different. A decision journal interrupts that process by freezing your thinking before hindsight can edit it. Research on judgement and forecasting consistently shows that learning improves when predictions are recorded in advance and later evaluated against what was actually written rather than what is remembered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39913490/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed+2Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fifty years of hindsight bias research-Reflection on...by B Fischhoff · 2025 · Cited by 13 — Fischhoff (1975) introduced tasks for...</span></span></span>

## What to Record Before the Outcome

The value of a decision journal depends less on length than on specificity. A one-page entry written before an important decision is often enough.

Record the following:

* **The decision itself.** State exactly what choice you are making.
* **The date and context.** Include the information available at the time. Future reviews should not benefit from information you did not yet possess.
* **Your reasoning.** [Explain]({{ 'explain/' | relative_url }}) why you think this is the best option. Focus on cause-and-effect logic rather than conclusions alone.
* **Alternative options.** Briefly note the strongest competing choice and why you rejected it.
* **Key [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}).** List the facts you believe to be true and the uncertainties that matter most.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--checklist" markdown="1">

* **Expected outcomes.** Describe what success and failure would look like in measurable terms.
* **Confidence level.** Assign a probability where practical—for example, "70% chance the project finishes before October."
* **Signals that would change your mind.** Identify evidence that would justify revising the decision before results arrive.

</div>

These elements make later review far more informative than simply asking whether the decision "worked". A good process can produce a poor outcome because of bad luck, and a weak process can occasionally succeed through favourable circumstances. Recording assumptions separately helps distinguish these cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-0-306-47630" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer+2Stanford University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Knowing what has happened can degrade these evaluations, reducing forecasters&#x27; ability to learn...Read more...</span></span></span>

## How Decision Journals Expose Hindsight Bias

Hindsight bias is not simply forgetting details. Research suggests that outcome knowledge actively changes how people reconstruct their earlier beliefs, making events seem more foreseeable and inevitable than they originally appeared. Baruch Fischhoff's pioneering experiments demonstrated that once participants learned an outcome, they systematically overestimated how predictable it had been beforehand. Decades of subsequent research have confirmed the robustness of this effect across many settings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39913490/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed+2Carlson School of Management]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fifty years of hindsight bias research-Reflection on...by B Fischhoff · 2025 · Cited by 13 — Fischhoff (1975) introduced tasks for...</span></span></span>

A decision journal blocks three common hindsight excuses.

**"I knew this would happen."**<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/223213727_I_Knew_It_Would_Happen_Remembered_Probabilities_of_Once-Future_Things" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[researchgate.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">I Knew It Would Happen: Remembered Probabilities of...6 Jun 2026 — In short, events are viewed as more predictable in hindsight than the...</span></span></span>

The journal contains your actual forecast. If you assigned only a 40% probability to an event, the written record prevents memory from quietly turning it into an 80% prediction.

**"The evidence was obvious."**

It rarely was. The journal preserves what information was genuinely available at the time, reminding you which facts were uncertain or missing.

**"The decision was obviously wrong."**

After failure, people often judge the process solely by the outcome. A journal allows evaluation of whether the reasoning was sound given the evidence then available, rather than judging yesterday's decision using today's knowledge.

This distinction is especially valuable in uncertain environments such as investing, business strategy, hiring, product development and public policy, where [outcomes]({{ 'outcomes/' | relative_url }}) are influenced by chance as well as judgement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/258127985_The_Consequences_of_the_Hindsight_Bias_in_Medical_Decision_Making" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate+2Academy of Management Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) The Consequences of the Hindsight Bias in Medical...The hindsight bias manifests in the tendency to exaggerate the ext...</span></span></span>

## Turning Entries into Better Next Decisions

A decision journal becomes useful only when it is reviewed.

Rather than asking, "Was I right?", ask questions such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* Which assumptions proved correct?
* Which assumptions failed?
* What information did I underestimate?
* Did I express appropriate confidence?
* Was I surprised for the right reasons or because I ignored warning signs?
* Would I make the same decision again knowing only what I knew then?

</div>

This shifts attention from defending past decisions to improving future judgement.

Over time, recurring patterns usually emerge. Some people consistently underestimate project delays. Others are routinely overconfident when evaluating familiar topics but overly cautious in unfamiliar ones. These patterns are difficult to detect from memory alone because memory selectively highlights dramatic successes and failures while smoothing away the everyday forecasting errors that reveal genuine habits.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/10/1/92953/200113/Overprecision-in-the-Survey-of-Professional" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: online.ucpress.edu">[University of California Press+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">online.ucpress.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Overprecision in the Survey of Professional</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of California PressOverprecision in the Survey of Professional Forecasters28 Feb 2024 — We find forecasts are overly precise...</span></span></span>

## Process Quality Versus Outcome Quality

One of the biggest advantages of decision journals is separating decision quality from outcome quality.

Imagine two managers approving similar projects.

The first performs careful analysis, identifies key risks, estimates only a moderate chance of success and proceeds. The project later fails because of an unexpected regulatory change.

The second ignores obvious warning signs, performs little analysis and approves a project that succeeds because a competitor unexpectedly exits the market.

Looking only at outcomes suggests the second manager made the better decision. Looking at their journals suggests the opposite.

This distinction prevents organisations from rewarding lucky processes or punishing thoughtful ones. It also encourages better calibration—matching confidence to evidence instead of treating every successful decision as proof of expertise. ## Combining Decision Journals with Pre-Mortems

Decision journals become even stronger when paired with a pre-mortem.

A pre-mortem asks participants to imagine that a decision has already failed and then explain why. Developed by Gary Klein, this exercise encourages consideration of overlooked risks before commitment becomes psychologically difficult. The resulting concerns can be written directly into the journal as explicit assumptions or warning signals to monitor later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theuncertaintyproject.org/tools/pre-mortem" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theuncertaintyproject.org">[theuncertaintyproject.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theuncertaintyproject.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pre-MortemDeveloped by Gary Klein, the pre-mortem analysis is a tool intended to increase the probability of a project&#x27;s success. Together, the two techniques create complementary safeguards:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* The pre-mortem broadens thinking before commitment.
* The decision journal preserves that thinking for later evaluation.
* The review compares both predictions and imagined failure modes with what actually happened.

</div>

This makes it harder to claim afterwards that the risks were always obvious.

## Common Mistakes That Reduce Their Value

Decision journals often fail because they become diaries rather than decision records.

Typical [mistakes]({{ 'mistakes/' | relative_url }}) include:

* Recording only conclusions instead of reasoning.
* Writing entries after the outcome is partly known.
* Using vague language such as "fairly likely" instead of [probabilities]({{ 'probabilities/' | relative_url }}) or concrete expectations.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* Reviewing only failed decisions instead of successful ones.
* Editing old entries instead of preserving the original version.
* Focusing on blame instead of identifying improvements to the decision process.

</div>

The goal is not to create perfect predictions. It is to create an honest historical record against which judgement can be measured.

## A Simple Template

A practical entry can fit comfortably on one page.


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Decision:** What am I choosing?
2. **Date:** What information is available today?
3. **Objective:** What am I trying to achieve?
4. **Alternatives considered:** What else could I do?
5. **Key assumptions:** What must be true for this decision to succeed?
6. **Forecast:** What outcomes do I expect, and with what probabilities?
7. **Confidence:** How certain am I overall?
8. **Review date:** When will I evaluate this decision?
9. **Success criteria:** What evidence will show whether the decision performed as expected?

</div>

The simplicity is deliberate. The journal should be quick enough to use before any decision significant enough that hindsight could later rewrite its story. By preserving the original reasoning, it creates an objective [feedback loop]({{ 'feedback-loop/' | relative_url }}) that exposes recurring biases, improves calibration and makes future decisions more evidence-based rather than memory-based.
