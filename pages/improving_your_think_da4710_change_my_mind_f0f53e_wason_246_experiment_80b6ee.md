## Introduction

Peter Wason's famous 2-4-6 experiment remains one of the clearest demonstrations of why people often fail to test their own beliefs effectively. Rather than deliberately looking for evidence that could prove their initial idea wrong, most participants searched for examples that confirmed it. The lesson reaches far beyond laboratory psychology. In everyday decisions—whether choosing investments, evaluating a medical concern, hiring someone, or deciding whether a relationship is working—the same tendency can leave people increasingly confident in incorrect conclusions because they ask the wrong questions. Wason's experiment shows that better thinking depends not only on gathering more evidence, but on actively seeking evidence that could change your mind.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/wason-qjep1960.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bear.warrington.ufl.edu">[bear.warrington.ufl.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bear.warrington.ufl.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">wason qjep1960</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">On the failure to eliminate hypotheses in a conceptual taskby PC Wason · Cited by 3767 — This investigation examines the extent to which...</span></span></span>

## How the experiment worked

In the original 1960 study, participants were shown the number sequence **2, 4, 6** and told that it followed a rule known only to the experimenter. Their task was to discover that rule by proposing additional triples of numbers. After each proposal, the experimenter simply replied whether it conformed to the rule or not. Participants could test as many examples as they wished before stating their answer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/wason-qjep1960.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bear.warrington.ufl.edu">[bear.warrington.ufl.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bear.warrington.ufl.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">wason qjep1960</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">On the failure to eliminate hypotheses in a conceptual taskby PC Wason · Cited by 3767 — This investigation examines the extent to which...</span></span></span>

Many participants quickly formed a specific hypothesis such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* even numbers
* numbers increasing by two
* regularly spaced ascending sequences

</div>

They then generated more examples such as **8, 10, 12** or **20, 22, 24**. Because these examples were accepted, participants became increasingly convinced their hypothesis was correct.

The actual rule, however, was far broader:

> **Any three numbers in ascending order.**

Sequences such as **3, 5, 100**, **1, 2, 9**, or **14, 15, 16** also satisfied the rule. A sequence like **5, 4, 3** or **7, 7, 8** would have been rejected.

The striking feature was not that participants guessed incorrectly. It was that relatively few deliberately tested examples that might have disproved their own theory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/wason-qjep1960.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bear.warrington.ufl.edu">[bear.warrington.ufl.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bear.warrington.ufl.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">wason qjep1960</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">On the failure to eliminate hypotheses in a conceptual taskby PC Wason · Cited by 3767 — This investigation examines the extent to which...</span></span></span>

## Why confirming tests mislead

The experiment illustrates an important distinction between **confirming** a favourite explanation and **challenging** it.

Suppose someone believes the rule is "even numbers increasing by two."

[Testing]({{ 'testing/' | relative_url }}) **12, 14, 16** feels informative because it succeeds. Yet success reveals very little. That sequence would fit both the narrow hypothesis ("increase by two") and the broader true rule ("ascending numbers"). It cannot distinguish between the competing explanations.

A much stronger test would be something like:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* **3, 5, 7**
* **1, 4, 9**
* **2, 3, 100**

</div>

If any of these were accepted, the original hypothesis would immediately need revision.

The key insight is that evidence supporting multiple explanations is usually [weak evidence]({{ 'weak-evidence/' | relative_url }}). Progress comes from finding observations that separate one explanation from another. Wason described this contrast as the difference between seeking only confirming cases and attempting to eliminate competing hypotheses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/wason-qjep1960.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bear.warrington.ufl.edu">[bear.warrington.ufl.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bear.warrington.ufl.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">wason qjep1960</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">On the failure to eliminate hypotheses in a conceptual taskby PC Wason · Cited by 3767 — This investigation examines the extent to which...</span></span></span>

Later researchers have argued that the phenomenon is more nuanced than a simple desire to confirm beliefs. Some suggest people naturally generate "positive tests" because they appear informative or are easier to imagine. Even so, the practical recommendation remains largely unchanged: deliberately search for cases your preferred explanation predicts should fail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7838739_Generation_of_Hypotheses_in_Wason%27s_2-4-6_Task_an_Information_Theory_Approach" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Generation of Hypotheses in Wason&#x27;s 2–4–6 TaskThere are currently two competing interpretations of hypothesis testing in Waso...</span></span></span>

## Applying the lesson to real choices

The value of the 2-4-6 experiment lies in recognising the same pattern in ordinary decisions.

### Career decisions

Imagine believing:

> "This new job is clearly the right move."

A confirming approach asks:

* What makes this company attractive?
* Why am I excited?

A Wason-style test asks:<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/324150467_Confirmation_Bias_and_the_2-4-6_Game_How_do_we_Test_our_Perspectives" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[researchgate.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">324150467 Confirmation Bias and the 2 4 6 Game How do we Test our Perspectives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Confirmation Bias and the 2-4-6 Game: How do we Test...This class activity builds on Peter Cathcart Wason&#x27;s (1960) 2-4-6 Hypothesis Rule...</span></span></span>


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* What evidence would show this role is a poor fit?
* What information would make me decline the offer?
* Which current employees might tell a different story?

</div>

Those questions are more likely to uncover hidden risks.

### Personal relationships

Someone who believes a relationship is healthy may notice affectionate moments while overlooking recurring patterns of avoidance or unresolved conflict.

A better question becomes:

* What behaviour would convince me that my current view is mistaken?

Rather than collecting pleasant examples, the focus shifts to identifying evidence that genuinely discriminates between alternative interpretations.

### Financial decisions

An investor convinced that a company is undervalued can easily accumulate confirming news articles and optimistic analyst reports.

A stronger approach is to ask:

* Which financial results would invalidate my investment thesis?
* What would competitors need to achieve to prove me wrong?
* Which [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}) about future growth are most vulnerable?

This changes investing from defending a position to testing one.

### Everyday planning

Even routine choices benefit from disconfirming tests.

Instead of asking:

> "Does my plan seem reasonable?"

ask:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* What would cause this plan to fail?
* What assumptions have I never checked?
* What result would make me change course immediately?

</div>

These questions are practical versions of "What would [change my mind]({{ 'change-mind/' | relative_url }})?"

## What later research adds

Wason's experiment became one of the most influential studies in the psychology of reasoning and helped launch decades of research into hypothesis testing and confirmation bias. Researchers have refined, criticised and extended its interpretation rather than simply accepting the original explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17470218.2014.914547" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Taylor &amp; Francis OnlineReasoning, biases and dual processes: The lasting impact...by JSBT Evans · 2016 · Cited by 82 — Wason (1960) publ...</span></span></span>

## A simple habit for better decisions

The 2-4-6 experiment can be translated into a straightforward thinking routine whenever an important judgement is at stake.

1. State your current belief clearly.
2. Ask what evidence would support that belief—but do not stop there.
3. Deliberately search for the observation that would most strongly contradict it.
4. Compare which explanation survives both confirming and [disconfirming evidence]({{ 'disconfirming-evidence/' | relative_url }}).

The goal is not to become sceptical about everything. It is to make confidence depend on surviving serious tests rather than collecting agreeable examples. That is why the experiment continues to be closely associated with the practical question, **"What would change my mind?"** It encourages people to replace the comforting search for agreement with the more reliable search for evidence that genuinely distinguishes between competing explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/wason-qjep1960.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bear.warrington.ufl.edu">[bear.warrington.ufl.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bear.warrington.ufl.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">wason qjep1960</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">On the failure to eliminate hypotheses in a conceptual taskby PC Wason · Cited by 3767 — This investigation examines the extent to which...</span></span></span>
