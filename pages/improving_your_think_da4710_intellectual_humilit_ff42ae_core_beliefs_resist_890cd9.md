## Introduction

Most people can be open-minded about many everyday opinions while remaining surprisingly resistant to changing a small number of deeply held beliefs. The difference is not simply how much evidence supports a belief. It is often about what the belief represents. When a claim becomes connected to personal identity, moral values, trusted communities or a sense of purpose, revising it can feel less like correcting an error and more like losing part of oneself. Intellectual humility is especially valuable in these situations because it separates the question "Is this belief true?" from "What does changing my mind say about me?" ## Peripheral Beliefs Versus Identity-Linked Beliefs

Not all beliefs occupy the same place in a person's mental world.

Peripheral beliefs tend to concern matters with little emotional or social importance. You might easily revise an opinion about the quickest route to work, the best way to prepare a meal or the release date of a film after seeing reliable evidence. Little else changes when those beliefs change.

Identity-linked beliefs are different. They are woven into answers to questions such as:

* Who am I?
* Which people are "my" people?<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/dspace/handle/10161/23967" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dukespace.lib.duke.edu">[dukespace.lib.duke.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dukespace.lib.duke.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Four studies examined intellectual humility-the degree to which people recognize that their beliefs might be wrong. * Which authorities do I trust?
* What kind of moral person do I believe myself to be?

</div>

Changing these beliefs may imply admitting that a trusted group was mistaken, acknowledging that a long-held cause was flawed or accepting that previous decisions rested on false [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}). Because the personal costs appear much larger, evidence alone often has less influence than people expect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2973067" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: papers.ssrn.com">[SSRN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">papers.ssrn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Misconceptions, Misinformation, and the Logic of Identity-...by DM Kahan · 2017 · Cited by 593 — The incidence and impact of misconc...</span></span></span>

Importantly, this does not mean identity-linked beliefs are necessarily false. Many central commitments are well supported by evidence. The point is that beliefs connected to identity tend to be processed differently from beliefs that carry few personal consequences.

## Why Central Commitments Update More Slowly

Resistance to updating is usually not caused by a single psychological bias. Several mechanisms often reinforce one another.

### Identity protection changes how evidence feels

Research on identity-protective cognition argues that people often evaluate information in ways that preserve membership within valued groups. Rather than consciously deciding to ignore evidence, they may find supportive evidence more convincing, scrutinise contradictory evidence more aggressively or trust sources associated with their own community more readily.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2973067" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: papers.ssrn.com">[SSRN+2informalscience.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">papers.ssrn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Misconceptions, Misinformation, and the Logic of Identity-...by DM Kahan · 2017 · Cited by 593 — The incidence and impact of misconc...</span></span></span>

This helps [explain]({{ 'explain/' | relative_url }}) why intelligent, well-informed people sometimes become more polarised rather than converging on shared conclusions.

### Moral commitments create additional resistance

Beliefs tied to moral convictions often become difficult to revise because changing them appears to involve compromising one's character rather than merely updating factual knowledge.

For example, if someone interprets a disagreement as a challenge to whether they are compassionate, responsible or loyal, the discussion quickly becomes about identity instead of evidence. Defensive reasoning becomes more likely because protecting self-respect feels more urgent than evaluating competing [claims]({{ 'claims/' | relative_url }}).

### Social relationships raise the stakes

Beliefs rarely exist in isolation. They are often reinforced by families, professional communities, religious organisations, political groups or friendship networks.

Changing one's mind can therefore involve practical social costs:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* disappointing respected mentors
* creating conflict within close relationships
* losing status within a community
* becoming uncertain about where one belongs

</div>

These costs can make even careful thinkers hesitate before reconsidering central assumptions.

### Confidence and identity are not the same

A common misunderstanding is that strong confidence necessarily prevents intellectual humility.

In reality, people may hold very confident beliefs while remaining able to specify what evidence would change their minds. The greater obstacle is often not confidence itself but identity fusion—the point at which disagreement feels like a personal attack rather than an opportunity to learn. Research on intellectual humility consistently distinguishes confidence from the willingness to acknowledge one's own fallibility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9244574/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2John Templeton Foundation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCPredictors and consequences of intellectual humility</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by T Porter · 2022 · Cited by 359 — Intellectual humility involves acknowledging the limitations of one&#x27;s knowledge and that one&#x27;s bel...</span></span></span>

## Why Better Evidence Alone Often Fails

Many discussions assume that disagreement exists because one side lacks information. Although this is sometimes true, it is often incomplete.

Studies of motivated reasoning suggest that people actively interpret information through existing beliefs and goals rather than simply absorbing facts. Evidence that supports existing commitments may receive relatively little scrutiny, while contradictory evidence is examined for flaws, alternative explanations or reasons to dismiss the source.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13546783.2023.2276975" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online+2Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Reasoning differently about information supporting</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Taylor &amp; Francis OnlineThe skeptical import of motivated reasoning: a closer look...by M van Doorn · 2024 · Cited by 16 — Central to man...</span></span></span>

This helps explain why simply providing additional facts frequently produces disappointing results when beliefs are closely tied to identity.

It also explains why debates sometimes become increasingly emotional despite increasing amounts of evidence: participants are not merely evaluating information but protecting different understandings of themselves and the groups they trust.

## How to Notice Defensiveness Before It Steers Reasoning

The most useful skill is not eliminating defensiveness entirely. It is recognising it early enough that it does not quietly take control of judgement.

Several signals commonly indicate that identity may be driving reasoning:

* You feel personally criticised before identifying exactly what factual claim is being challenged.
* You become much more interested in finding weaknesses in opposing evidence than in evaluating your own assumptions.
* You dismiss a source primarily because it belongs to an opposing group without first considering its actual evidence.
* You notice an unusually strong emotional reaction—[anger]({{ 'anger/' | relative_url }}), embarrassment or anxiety—before you have finished examining the argument.
* You find yourself thinking, "People like us don't believe that," rather than asking whether the claim itself is accurate.

None of these signs proves that your conclusion is wrong. They simply indicate that identity concerns may be influencing how evidence is being processed.

## Practising Intellectual Humility Without Losing Conviction

Recognising identity-linked beliefs does not require treating every opinion as equally credible or abandoning firmly supported conclusions.

Instead, intellectual humility encourages habits that reduce unnecessary defensiveness:<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://drcharlesmrusso.substack.com/p/intellectual-humility" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: drcharlesmrusso.substack.com">[drcharlesmrusso.substack.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">drcharlesmrusso.substack.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">intellectual humility</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Charles M. Russo - The Critical ThinkerIntellectual humility (IH) doesn&#x27;t mean weak thinking; it means robust thinking: recognizing that...</span></span></span>


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* Separate the evidence from the person. Critiquing a claim does not automatically require judging the character of the person making it.
* Ask what evidence would genuinely change your mind before entering a disagreement. If no conceivable evidence exists, the issue is probably functioning more as an identity commitment than an empirical belief.
* Distinguish loyalty from accuracy. Supporting a community does not require defending every claim associated with it.
* Notice emotional reactions as information rather than instructions. Feeling threatened may signal that identity is involved, not that the opposing evidence is necessarily false.
* Remember previous occasions when changing your mind improved your understanding. Viewing revision as learning rather than defeat reduces the psychological cost of updating.

</div>

## The Practical Lesson

The beliefs that resist revision most strongly are usually those that answer questions about identity, belonging and moral commitment rather than those supported by the greatest amount of evidence. Recognising this distinction helps explain why thoughtful people can remain remarkably flexible in some areas while becoming unexpectedly defensive in others.

Intellectual humility does not ask people to detach from every deeply held commitment. It asks them to recognise when protecting an identity has quietly become more important than evaluating evidence. That awareness makes it easier to revise beliefs when reality genuinely demands it while still holding well-supported convictions with justified confidence.
