## Introduction

When people disagree, they often think they are arguing about facts. In many cases, however, the real disagreement sits elsewhere: in the hidden assumption that connects those facts to a conclusion. Stephen Toulmin, the philosopher who developed the Toulmin model of argumentation in the 1950s, called this connecting assumption the **warrant**. The warrant is the reason the evidence is supposed to support the claim. Without it, facts and conclusions simply sit side by side.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/historical_perspectives_on_argumentation/toulmin_argument.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: owl.purdue.edu">[Purdue OWL]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">owl.purdue.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">OWLToulmin Argument</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Purdue OWLToulmin Argument - Purdue OWLThe Toulmin method is a style of argumentation that breaks arguments down into six component parts...</span></span></span>

For anyone trying to improve their thinking and analytical skills, warrants matter because they reveal the often-unspoken logic inside everyday arguments. Once the warrant becomes visible, it is easier to understand why reasonable people can look at the same evidence and reach different conclusions. The debate is often not about the evidence itself but about whether the bridge from evidence to claim is justified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/establishing_arguments/organizing_your_argument.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: owl.purdue.edu">[Purdue OWL]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">owl.purdue.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">OWLOrganizing Your Argument</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Purdue OWLOrganizing Your Argument - Purdue OWLWarrant (Bridge): In this section, you explain why or how your data supports the claim. ## The Hidden Bridge in Every Argument

### Claim, grounds, and warrant in plain language

Toulmin's model identifies three core elements present in ordinary arguments:<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JJ2XgVfpKXc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: youtube.com">[youtube.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">youtube.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Toulmin Model of Argumentation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Toulmin Method of Argumentation &#124; THUNK...</span></span></span>


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* **Claim**: what someone wants you to believe.
* **Grounds** (or data): the evidence offered in support.
* **Warrant**: the assumption or principle that explains why the evidence supports the claim.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/historical_perspectives_on_argumentation/toulmin_argument.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: owl.purdue.edu">[Purdue OWL+2mhcc.pressbooks.pub]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">owl.purdue.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">OWLToulmin Argument</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Purdue OWLToulmin Argument - Purdue OWLThe Toulmin method is a style of argumentation that breaks arguments down into six component parts...</span></span></span>

</div>

A simple example shows the difference:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Claim: "This restaurant is worth visiting."
* Grounds: "It is always busy on weekends."
* Warrant: "Restaurants that attract many customers are usually good."

</div>

The evidence alone does not force the conclusion. The conclusion becomes plausible only if the listener accepts the warrant. If someone instead believes that popularity often reflects marketing rather than quality, they may reject the conclusion while accepting the evidence.

This is why Toulmin described warrants as the mechanism that authorises movement from grounds to [claims]({{ 'claims/' | relative_url }}). They are the hidden rules of reasoning that make an argument seem sensible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://argumentcenterededucation.com/2019/07/17/our-adaptation-of-the-toulmin-model-of-argument/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: argumentcenterededucation.com">[Argument-Centered Education]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">argumentcenterededucation.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">our adaptation of the toulmin model of argument</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Argument-Centered EducationOur Adaptation of the Toulmin Model of ArgumentJul 17, 2019 — In The Uses of Argument, he says that a warrant...</span></span></span>

### Why warrants are often invisible

In everyday conversation, people rarely state their warrants directly. They assume listeners already share them. Writing guides based on Toulmin's work consistently note that warrants are frequently implicit rather than explicitly stated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://purdueglobalwriting.center/the-toulmin-model-of-argument/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: purdueglobalwriting.center">[purdueglobalwriting.center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">purdueglobalwriting.center</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Toulmin Model of Argument PodcastThe warrant is the assumption, or belief, the writer has in mind when forming an argument. Consider these examples:

GroundsClaimLikely warrant"She has twenty years of experience.""She should lead the project."Experience usually improves judgement and leadership."The policy was introduced last year, and costs increased.""The policy caused the increase."Events that occur before a change are likely responsible for it."Several friends recommend the film.""The film is probably good."Trusted recommendations are useful indicators of quality.

## How to Spot the Assumption Doing the Work

One of the most useful analytical habits is learning to uncover the warrant beneath an argument.

A practical technique is to ask:

**"Why does that evidence make this conclusion reasonable?"**

The answer usually exposes the warrant.

Imagine someone says:

> "Three employees resigned this month, so morale must be poor."

The grounds are clear: three resignations. The claim is that morale is poor. The hidden warrant is something like:

> "When multiple employees leave in a short period, low morale is usually a significant cause."

Now the discussion becomes more precise. Instead of arguing vaguely about the conclusion, people can examine the assumption itself. Perhaps resignations were caused by higher salaries elsewhere. Perhaps a restructuring programme was announced. The real issue is whether the warrant fits the situation.

This is one reason Toulmin's model remains influential in argument analysis. By separating claims, grounds, and warrants, it allows each part to be evaluated independently.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.blinn.edu/writing-centers/wide/toulmin-argument.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blinn.edu">[Blinn College]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blinn.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Blinn CollegeToulmin ArgumentIdentify each argument&#x27;s claims, data, and warrants. ### Where disagreements often really live

Many public and personal disagreements turn out to be warrant disputes.

For example:

* **Health discussions**: Two people may agree that someone improved after taking a supplement. One accepts the warrant that improvement indicates effectiveness; the other questions whether recovery, placebo effects, or other factors [explain]({{ 'explain/' | relative_url }}) the change.
* **Workplace debates**: Colleagues may agree that a team missed deadlines. They disagree about whether missed deadlines indicate poor management or unrealistic workload expectations.
* **News and politics**: People may accept the same facts but disagree over whether those facts demonstrate success, failure, fairness, or responsibility.

The evidence is not always the source of conflict. Often the conflict lies in different [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}) about what the evidence means.

## Testing Whether the Bridge Is Strong Enough

Recognising a warrant is only the first step. The next question is whether it is a good warrant.

### Ask whether the warrant is generally true

A strong warrant usually connects evidence and conclusion through a principle that works in many comparable situations.

For example:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* Grounds: "The bridge inspection found structural damage."
* Claim: "The bridge should be repaired."
* Warrant: "Structural damage creates safety risks."

</div>

This warrant is broadly reliable.

Compare it with:<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.blinn.edu/writing-centers/wide/toulmin-argument.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blinn.edu">[blinn.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blinn.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Blinn CollegeToulmin ArgumentIdentify each argument&#x27;s claims, data, and warrants. Look for qualifiers, rebuttals, and backing for the war...</span></span></span>


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Grounds: "The bridge inspection found structural damage."
* Claim: "The city council is incompetent."
* Warrant: "Infrastructure problems always result from incompetence."

</div>

That warrant is much more questionable because alternative explanations are easy to imagine.

### Look for exceptions

Toulmin emphasised that arguments should be tested against possible objections and exceptions. A warrant that collapses when a few obvious exceptions appear is usually weak.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://open.ocolearnok.org/englishcomposuokc/chapter/using-the-toulmin-system-to-build-an-argument/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: open.ocolearnok.org">[Open Oco]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">open.ocolearnok.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open OcoModels of Argumentation – First-Year English CompositionToulmin emphasizes first identifying all of the potential objections to t...</span></span></span>

Consider:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Grounds: "The company is profitable."
* Claim: "Employees must be happy."
* Warrant: "Profitable companies treat employees well."

</div>

Many exceptions immediately come to mind. Profitability and employee satisfaction are related in some circumstances but not reliably enough to support a confident conclusion.

### Check whether the warrant matches the strength of the claim

A common reasoning error occurs when a modest warrant is used to support a sweeping conclusion.

For example:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--benefit" markdown="1">

* Grounds: "Two customers complained."
* Claim: "The product is a failure."
* Warrant: "Customer complaints indicate product quality."

</div>

Even if the warrant has some truth, it does not justify such a broad claim. The evidence and warrant support only a much weaker conclusion, such as "the product may have issues worth investigating".
