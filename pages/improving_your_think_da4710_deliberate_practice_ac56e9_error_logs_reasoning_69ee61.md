## Introduction

An error log is a structured record of reasoning [mistakes]({{ 'mistakes/' | relative_url }}) that turns being wrong into usable [feedback]({{ 'feedback/' | relative_url }}). Instead of asking, “Did I get the answer right?”, it asks, “What kind of wrong was this, why did it happen, and what should I practise next?” That shift is important because reasoning rarely improves through repetition alone. Improvement depends on recognising recurring failure patterns, monitoring one's own thinking, and deliberately targeting weak points with new practice. Research on self-regulated learning consistently shows that planning, monitoring and evaluating one's own performance are central to developing higher-level thinking skills.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5408091/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Self-regulated learning (SRL) includes the cognitive, metacognitive, behavioral, motivational, and emotional/affective aspects of learnin...</span></span></span>

Within [deliberate practice]({{ 'deliberate-practice/' | relative_url }}), an error log is therefore not a diary of failures. It is a diagnostic tool. Its purpose is to convert isolated mistakes into categories that reveal stable habits, allowing each future exercise to address a specific reasoning weakness rather than hoping that experience alone will produce improvement.

## Turning mistakes into categories instead of shame

People often treat errors as evidence of ability. Deliberate practice treats them as evidence about process.

Suppose two people reach the same incorrect conclusion. One misunderstood the evidence, while the other ignored an alternative explanation because they became overconfident after recognising a familiar pattern. Although the outcome is identical, the training need is completely different. An effective error log separates these cases instead of recording only that the answer was "wrong".

The key principle is that every entry should identify the *reasoning failure*, not merely the incorrect conclusion. Useful questions include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* What assumption proved false?
* Which step first departed from good reasoning?
* What evidence should have changed my mind?
* Was the mistake due to missing knowledge or poor reasoning with available knowledge?
* Would I make this mistake again under similar conditions?

</div>

These questions encourage metacognitive regulation—the ability to monitor and evaluate one's own thinking—which educational research identifies as a core component of effective self-regulated learning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5408091/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Self-regulated learning (SRL) includes the cognitive, metacognitive, behavioral, motivational, and emotional/affective aspects of learnin...</span></span></span>

The emotional effect also matters. When errors become classified observations rather than personal failures, they become easier to inspect honestly. The goal is diagnosis, not self-judgement.

A useful reasoning error log contains relatively few categories that are broad enough to reveal patterns but specific enough to suggest a corrective exercise.

## Common reasoning-error patterns worth tracking

Over time, an error log becomes valuable because repeated entries expose habits that are difficult to notice in individual exercises.

Several recurring patterns deserve particular attention.

**Premature closure.** The learner accepts the first explanation that appears satisfactory instead of continuing to search for alternatives. This often produces confident but fragile conclusions.

**Evidence neglect.** Information that contradicts an initial judgement is overlooked, discounted or forgotten. The error frequently appears when someone forms an opinion before carefully examining available evidence.

**Confidence calibration errors.** The confidence assigned to a conclusion does not match its actual reliability. Recording confidence before receiving feedback makes these errors visible and allows calibration to improve over repeated practice.

**Strategy mismatch.** The learner applies an otherwise useful reasoning method to the wrong type of problem—for example, relying on [intuition]({{ 'intuition/' | relative_url }}) where systematic analysis is required, or over-analysing a task that depends on recognising a familiar pattern.

**Transfer failures.** A reasoning strategy works in one context but is not recognised as applicable elsewhere. Logging both successful and unsuccessful transfers helps identify missing generalisation rather than missing knowledge.

Rather than treating these as isolated mistakes, the log asks whether one pattern repeatedly dominates performance. That recurring pattern becomes the highest-priority target for deliberate practice.

## Recording confidence makes mistakes more informative

An unusually powerful addition to an error log is a confidence rating made *before* checking the answer.

This separates two very different situations:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* low confidence followed by an incorrect answer, suggesting recognised uncertainty;
* high confidence followed by an incorrect answer, revealing a misconception or serious calibration problem.

</div>

Research on the **hypercorrection effect** shows that high-confidence errors are often especially memorable once corrected because the conflict between expectation and reality captures attention. However, this benefit depends on receiving accurate corrective feedback rather than simply discovering that an answer was wrong.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypercorrection_%28psychology%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hypercorrection (psychology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hypercorrection (psychology</span></span></span>

For reasoning practice, the lesson is practical: do not record only whether you were correct. Record how certain you were before checking.

A simple confidence scale from 0–100% is often sufficient to reveal systematic [overconfidence]({{ 'overconfidence/' | relative_url }}) or excessive caution across many exercises.

## Designing the next practice task from the log

An error log only becomes deliberate practice if it changes what happens next.

Each category should lead directly to a targeted exercise rather than another general problem set.

For example:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--checklist" markdown="1">

* **Repeated unsupported assumptions** → complete exercises where every assumption must be written before solving.
* **Repeated failure to consider alternatives** → require three competing explanations before selecting one.
* **Frequent evidence neglect** → practise distinguishing observations from interpretations using short articles or case studies.
* **Persistent overconfidence** → estimate probabilities before revealing answers and review calibration weekly.
* **Frequent procedural slips** → introduce deliberate verification checklists rather than additional conceptual study.

</div>

This principle mirrors broader research on deliberate practice: feedback is useful only when it identifies the next performance target rather than merely evaluating past performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5408091/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Self-regulated learning (SRL) includes the cognitive, metacognitive, behavioral, motivational, and emotional/affective aspects of learnin...</span></span></span>

## Using AI without weakening the error log

Generative AI can provide rapid feedback, suggest alternative arguments or identify overlooked evidence. However, if it supplies [corrections]({{ 'corrections/' | relative_url }}) before the learner has analysed the mistake independently, it may reduce the metacognitive work that the error log is intended to strengthen.

Recent educational research has suggested that while AI can improve immediate task performance, excessive dependence may reduce active monitoring and evaluation of one's own reasoning if learners simply accept AI-generated corrections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/386649880_Beware_of_metacognitive_laziness_Effects_of_generative_artificial_intelligence_on_learning_motivation_processes_and_performance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative...December 10, 2024 — 10 Feb 2025 — We found that AI technologies su...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 10, 2024</span></span></span>

A better sequence is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Complete the reasoning task independently.
2. Predict confidence.
3. Identify and classify the likely error yourself.
4. Compare your diagnosis with external feedback or AI assistance.
5. Design the next targeted practice exercise.

</div>

In this workflow, AI supplements diagnosis rather than replacing it.

## A compact error log template

An effective reasoning error log can remain brief while still supporting meaningful improvement.

For each attempt, record:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **Task:** What reasoning problem was attempted?
* **Initial answer:** What conclusion did I reach?
* **Confidence:** How certain was I before checking?
* **Actual outcome:** What was correct?
* **Error category:** Which reasoning pattern best explains the mistake?
* **Root cause:** Why did that pattern occur?
* **Correct reasoning:** What should have happened instead?
* **Next deliberate practice:** What single exercise would reduce this specific error?

</div>

After several weeks, review the log for frequency rather than memorable failures. The most common category—not the most dramatic mistake—is usually the best guide to the next stage of deliberate practice.
