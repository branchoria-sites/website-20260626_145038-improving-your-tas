## Introduction

A common reason people fail on complex tasks is not that they lack ability, but that they continue using a strategy that has quietly stopped fitting the problem. During-task checks for strategy drift are short, deliberate moments of metacognitive monitoring that ask whether the current approach is still producing meaningful progress. Rather than waiting until the end of a task to discover that hours were spent unproductively, these checks create a [feedback loop]({{ 'feedback-loop/' | relative_url }}): monitor what is happening, compare it with the goal, and adjust if necessary. Research on [metacognition]({{ 'metacognition/' | relative_url }}) consistently shows that effective learners and problem-solvers monitor their progress and use those observations to regulate their behaviour rather than simply working harder with the same method.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sanlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/RBjork_Dunlosky_Kornell_2012.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sanlab.psych.ucla.edu">[Sanlab+2NSW Education]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sanlab.psych.ucla.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sanlab Self‐regulated Learning: Beliefs, Techniques, and Illusions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Self‐regulated Learning: Beliefs, Techniques, and IllusionsJanuary 3, 2013 — by N Kornell · Cited by 1 — As Nelson and Narens (1990...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 3, 2013</span></span></span>

Unlike broad reflection before or after a task, drift checks happen while the work is unfolding. Their purpose is not to interrupt concentration constantly, but to prevent persistence from turning into stubbornness.

## What strategy drift looks like

Strategy drift occurs when your actions no longer match the demands of the task, yet you continue because the current routine feels familiar. The danger is that [familiarity]({{ 'familiarity/' | relative_url }}) often creates a false sense of progress.

Drift commonly develops in subtle ways:

* continuing to gather information when enough evidence already exists to decide;
* repeatedly rereading difficult material instead of [testing]({{ 'testing/' | relative_url }}) whether it can be recalled or explained;

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* refining small details before the main argument or solution is established;
* applying a technique that worked earlier even though the problem has changed;
* spending increasing time without reducing uncertainty or producing useful output.

</div>

The defining feature is not effort but diminishing returns. Time continues to pass, yet each additional minute contributes less towards the objective.

Metacognitive research describes this as a breakdown between monitoring and control. Monitoring should detect that progress has slowed or stopped. Control should then alter the strategy. When monitoring is weak, people often mistake continued activity for genuine advancement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-the-learning-sciences/metacognition/B670B062FF21016A7CFEB4EACB81D72C" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment+2Sanlab]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment Metacognition (Chapter 4</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">more...</span></span></span>

## Signs that the current approach has stopped working

One useful principle is to judge a strategy by its outputs rather than by how busy it makes you feel.

Warning signs include:

* **The same obstacle keeps reappearing.** You repeatedly solve the wrong sub-problem or return to the same confusion.
* **Confidence rises without stronger evidence.** Material feels increasingly familiar, yet you cannot [explain]({{ 'explain/' | relative_url }}) it independently or solve a fresh example.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Progress becomes mechanical.** You continue because stopping would require deciding what to do next.
* **New information changes nothing.** Additional reading, calculations or notes fail to influence your understanding or decisions.
* **The goal becomes blurred.** You remember what you are doing but lose sight of why you are doing it.

</div>

These signals matter because people are often poor judges of learning when relying on feelings alone. Fluency—the sense that information is easy to process—can be mistaken for genuine understanding, even when later recall or transfer is weak. Research on self-regulated learning repeatedly identifies this gap between subjective confidence and actual performance as a major reason why learners persist with ineffective strategies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sanlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/RBjork_Dunlosky_Kornell_2012.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sanlab.psych.ucla.edu">[Sanlab+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sanlab.psych.ucla.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sanlab Self‐regulated Learning: Beliefs, Techniques, and Illusions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Self‐regulated Learning: Beliefs, Techniques, and IllusionsJanuary 3, 2013 — by N Kornell · Cited by 1 — As Nelson and Narens (1990...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 3, 2013</span></span></span>

## Questions that expose confusion, fluency and drift

Good drift checks rely on concrete questions rather than vague impressions. They should produce evidence that the current strategy is either working or failing.

### Detecting confusion

Ask questions that reveal whether you know where the difficulty lies.

Examples include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* What exactly is preventing progress?
* Can I identify the specific missing piece?
* Am I solving the intended problem or an easier substitute?

</div>

Being able to name the obstacle is itself useful information. A vague feeling of being stuck often becomes manageable once the obstacle is defined.

### Detecting false fluency

Instead of asking, "Does this make sense?", ask questions that require demonstration.

Examples include:

* Could I explain this without looking?
* Could I [predict]({{ 'predict/' | relative_url }}) the next step before seeing it?
* Could I solve a similar problem from scratch?

These questions replace familiarity with retrieval or application, both of which provide more reliable evidence of understanding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sanlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/RBjork_Dunlosky_Kornell_2012.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sanlab.psych.ucla.edu">[Sanlab+2jasonfinley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sanlab.psych.ucla.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sanlab Self‐regulated Learning: Beliefs, Techniques, and Illusions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Self‐regulated Learning: Beliefs, Techniques, and IllusionsJanuary 3, 2013 — by N Kornell · Cited by 1 — As Nelson and Narens (1990...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 3, 2013</span></span></span>

### Detecting strategy drift

Finally, ask whether the current method is still justified.

Useful prompts include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* If I were starting this task now, would I choose this approach?
* What evidence says this strategy is still productive?
* What has changed since I began?
* If another person watched my last twenty minutes, would they describe genuine progress or repetition?
* What result should I have produced by now if this method were working?

</div>

Notice that these questions focus on observable outcomes rather than motivation. They ask whether the strategy deserves to continue.

## How to switch strategies without random flailing

Recognising drift does not mean abandoning a task at the first sign of difficulty. Effective metacognition balances persistence with adaptation.

A practical sequence is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Pause briefly.** Stop long enough to evaluate rather than reacting impulsively.
2. **Identify the failure mode.** Is the problem missing knowledge, an unsuitable method, excessive detail, or a misunderstanding of the goal?
3. **Change one important variable.** For example:

</div>
move from passive reading to self-testing;
sketch a diagram instead of reasoning verbally;
generate an example before reading another explanation;
seek [disconfirming evidence]({{ 'disconfirming-evidence/' | relative_url }}) instead of supporting evidence;
break the task into smaller checkpoints.
4. **Set a short review point.** Decide in advance when to assess whether the new approach is producing measurable improvement.

Changing only one major feature makes it easier to determine whether the adjustment helped. Switching everything simultaneously makes success or failure difficult to interpret.

This reflects the metacognitive cycle described in educational research: plan, monitor, evaluate, then regulate behaviour based on the information obtained rather than habit alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/metacognition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk">[EEF+2EEF]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">EEFMetacognition and Self-Regulated LearningTeachers should support pupils to plan, monitor, and evaluate their learning through explicit...</span></span></span>

## A simple example of drift detection

Imagine analysing a research paper.

For forty minutes you have highlighted sentences and written detailed notes. You feel productive because the pages are becoming colourful.

A drift check asks different questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--example" markdown="1">

* Can I state the paper's central claim from memory?
* What evidence supports that claim?
* Could I explain why the authors preferred this method?
* Have my notes improved my understanding, or merely recorded the text?

</div>

If you cannot answer those questions, highlighting has become disconnected from the goal. A better strategy might be to stop annotating temporarily and write a short summary from memory, then compare it with the paper to locate genuine gaps.

The task has not changed, but the strategy has.

## Building drift checks into demanding work

Strategy checks are most effective when they occur at natural transition points rather than every few minutes.

Examples include:

* after completing a section of reading;
* after solving one problem before attempting the next;
* every fixed period during extended analytical work;
* whenever progress feels unusually effortless or unusually frustrating;
* immediately before committing to a major decision.

These checkpoints reduce the risk that hours of work become locked into an increasingly ineffective approach.

Research on metacognition emphasises that monitoring alone is insufficient. The value comes from linking monitoring to control—using the information gathered during the task to change actions while there is still time for those changes to improve the outcome.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-the-learning-sciences/metacognition/B670B062FF21016A7CFEB4EACB81D72C" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment+2NSW Education]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment Metacognition (Chapter 4</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">more...</span></span></span>
