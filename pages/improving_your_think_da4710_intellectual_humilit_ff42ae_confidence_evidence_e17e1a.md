## Introduction

Good judgement does not require perfect certainty. It requires matching your level of confidence to the strength of the evidence available. That means being highly confident when multiple, independent, direct sources point to the same conclusion, moderately confident when the evidence is suggestive but incomplete, and cautious when the evidence is weak, indirect or conflicting. This ability—often called **confidence calibration**—is one of the practical foundations of intellectual humility. It allows you to act before certainty while avoiding both unwarranted certainty and unnecessary hesitation. Research across psychology, decision science and medicine consistently finds that people often miscalibrate their confidence, becoming either more certain or less certain than the evidence justifies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12730000/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2The American Journal of Medicine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCAn Experimental Study of Overconfidence Biases in Young Decision</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This strong alignment supports the notion that overconfidence is associated with poorer...</span></span></span>

Rather than asking, "Am I confident?", a better question is, "How much confidence does this evidence deserve?" That shift changes confidence from a personality trait into a judgement that can be adjusted as evidence improves or weakens.

## Why confidence should have a size

Confidence is not an all-or-nothing state. It is better understood as a scale. The ideal is for your certainty to increase or decrease in proportion to the reliability, independence and relevance of the evidence supporting a claim.

A useful way to think about this is that confidence should earn its way upwards. Every increase in confidence should be backed by something concrete, such as an additional independent source, a stronger method, direct observation instead of inference, or repeated successful predictions.

Calibration researchers define good judgement as a close match between expressed confidence and actual accuracy. If someone says they are 80% confident across many comparable judgements, roughly 80% of those judgements should eventually prove correct. Perfect calibration is difficult, but it is a practical target because it reduces predictable errors in decision-making.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ffo2.199" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library+2American Psychological Association]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryCalibration Feedback With the Practical Scoring Rule Does...by M Martin · 2025 · Cited by 1 — We examined the effect...</span></span></span>

This perspective also explains why intellectual humility is not the same as constant doubt. A calibrated thinker may express very high confidence when the evidence genuinely warrants it. Humility lies in allowing confidence to track evidence rather than [identity]({{ 'identity/' | relative_url }}), emotion or social [pressure]({{ 'pressure/' | relative_url }}).

## Evidence types that deserve different weight

Not all evidence deserves equal confidence. Good judgement depends on recognising which forms of evidence should move your confidence substantially and which should only nudge it.

Evidence characteristicAppropriate effect on confidenceMultiple independent sources reaching the same conclusionLarge increaseDirect measurements or observationsLarger increase than indirect inferenceEvidence replicated across different settingsLarger increaseSingle anecdote or personal experienceSmall increaseClaims from interested parties with incentives to persuadeLimited increase until independently verifiedEvidence that conflicts across high-quality sourcesReduced confidence until resolved

Several practical questions help determine how much weight evidence deserves:

* **How direct is it?** Seeing something measured directly is generally stronger than relying on several layers of interpretation.
* **How independent is it?** Ten reports that all originate from the same source do not provide ten independent pieces of evidence.
* **How consistent is it?** Agreement across unrelated studies or observers usually deserves more confidence than isolated findings.
* **How well has it survived attempts to disprove it?** [Claims]({{ 'claims/' | relative_url }}) repeatedly tested without being overturned deserve greater confidence than claims that have never faced serious scrutiny.

In evidence-based medicine, frameworks such as GRADE distinguish between stronger and weaker evidence by considering study design, consistency, directness and risk of bias. Although developed for healthcare, the underlying principle applies broadly: confidence should reflect evidence quality, not simply evidence quantity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/340889782_Calibrate_your_confidence_in_research_findings_A_tutorial_on_improving_research_methods_and_practices" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Calibrate your confidence in research findings: A tutorial on...April 24, 2020 — 24 Apr 2020 — This article provides an acce...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 24, 2020</span></span></span>

## Common signs of overconfidence and underconfidence

Poor calibration can fail in two directions. Most discussions focus on [overconfidence]({{ 'overconfidence/' | relative_url }}), but excessive caution also reduces judgement quality.

### Overconfidence

Overconfidence appears when certainty grows faster than the evidence supports. Common warning signs include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Speaking in absolutes despite limited evidence.
* Treating confidence itself as proof.
* Ignoring missing information because existing evidence feels persuasive.
* Becoming resistant to updating after new contradictory evidence appears.
* Confusing expertise in one area with expertise in another.

</div>

Studies also suggest that overconfidence is often fuelled by neglecting what is *unknown*. ### Underconfidence

Underconfidence receives less attention but can also impair thinking. Someone may hesitate to act despite strong, consistent evidence or repeatedly discount their own well-supported conclusions.

Signs include:

* Demanding impossible levels of certainty before deciding.
* Giving [weak evidence]({{ 'weak-evidence/' | relative_url }}) and strong evidence nearly identical confidence.
* Constantly reopening settled questions without new information.
* Assuming every disagreement means the evidence is evenly balanced.

Research on calibration shows that different tasks can produce different patterns. Difficult tasks often encourage overconfidence about performance, while easier tasks sometimes produce underestimation. The goal is therefore not maximum caution but accurate calibration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://healy.econ.ohio-state.edu/papers/Moore_Healy-TroubleWithOverconfidence.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: healy.econ.ohio-state.edu">[P.J. ## A practical way to scale confidence

Instead of thinking in vague terms such as "certain" or "unsure", deliberately tie confidence to identifiable properties of the evidence.

One practical framework is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Low confidence:** evidence is sparse, indirect, unverified or largely speculative.
* **Moderate confidence:** several credible sources support the claim, but important uncertainties remain.
* **High confidence:** multiple independent, direct and consistent sources converge, and plausible alternatives have been carefully examined.
* **Very high confidence:** conclusions have repeatedly survived testing, prediction and replication over time.

</div>

The precise percentages matter less than consistency. The important habit is explaining *why* your confidence sits where it does.

For example:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* "I'm only slightly confident because this relies on a single witness."
* "I'm reasonably confident because three independent studies reached similar conclusions."
* "I'm highly confident because direct measurements have been replicated by different groups."

</div>

These explanations make confidence transparent and easier to revise when new evidence appears.

## Updating confidence as evidence changes

Calibration is dynamic rather than fixed. New evidence should change confidence by an amount proportional to its quality.

A single weak study should rarely overturn years of consistent evidence. Equally, one well-designed experiment may substantially change confidence if previous evidence was weak or uncertain. The key is avoiding two common [mistakes]({{ 'mistakes/' | relative_url }}):

* refusing to update because changing your mind feels like admitting failure; or
* swinging from certainty to certainty whenever a new claim appears.

Intellectual humility encourages gradual adjustment rather than dramatic reversals. Confidence should move because the evidence has changed—not because the conversation, headlines or social environment have changed.

## Confidence is a judgement, not an identity

One of the most useful distinctions is between believing strongly because the evidence is strong and believing strongly because the belief has become part of your identity.

Confidence grounded in evidence can rise or fall without threatening self-worth. Identity-based confidence resists revision because changing the belief feels personally costly.

This distinction supports the broader aim of intellectual humility. You do not need to become less decisive. Instead, become more deliberate about *why* you are decisive. The strongest thinkers are not those who always sound certain; they are those whose certainty consistently expands and contracts in proportion to the quality, independence and directness of the evidence before them.
