## Introduction

When people say they are “mapping a decision”, they often begin with a topic: *job offer*, *working from home*, *should we introduce a new policy?* The problem is that a topic is not yet an argument. An argument map becomes useful only when it is organised around a claim that could be accepted, rejected, questioned, or supported. In [practice]({{ 'practice/' | relative_url }}), the most effective maps for everyday decisions are often conclusion-first maps: they start by stating exactly what is being defended and then work backwards to examine the reasons, evidence, objections, and [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}). Argument-mapping guidance consistently places identification of the main claim or conclusion at the centre of the mapping process because the structure of the rest of the map depends on it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.open.edu/openlearn/ocw/mod/oucontent/view.php?id=96118&amp;section=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: open.edu">[The Open University+2The Open University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">open.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">First, try to find the main claim. The main claim is the overall point or conclusion that the author of the text is making.Read more...</span></span></span>

For improving thinking and analytical skills, this seemingly small change matters. A clear conclusion prevents a map from becoming a collection of disconnected considerations. It forces the question: *what, exactly, am I trying to prove?*

## What Is Your Map Really Trying to Prove?

Many reasoning failures occur before any evidence is examined. The mistake is not weak support but an unclear target.

Consider the difference between these starting points:

* “[Remote work]({{ 'remote-work/' | relative_url }})”

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* “Job offer”
* “City transport”
* “I should accept the offer from Company A.”
* “Our team should continue remote work three days per week.”
* “The council should introduce a bus-priority scheme.”

</div>

The first group names subjects. The second group states conclusions. Only the second group can serve as the top node of an argument map because only they make a definite claim. Argument-mapping methods therefore emphasise identifying the main claim before building the rest of the structure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.open.edu/openlearn/ocw/mod/oucontent/view.php?id=96118&amp;section=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: open.edu">[The Open University+2Reasons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">open.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">First, try to find the main claim. The main claim is the overall point or conclusion that the author of the text is making.Read more...</span></span></span>

A useful test is whether someone could reasonably respond “yes”, “no”, or “I'm not convinced”. If they can, the statement is probably specific enough to function as a conclusion.

Starting with a conclusion also reduces a common analytical error: endlessly collecting information without knowing what decision the information is supposed to support. Evidence gains meaning only relative to a claim.

## Finding the Real Top-Level Conclusion

One of the most valuable skills in argument mapping is distinguishing the final conclusion from supporting conclusions.

In many everyday decisions, people accidentally place a supporting claim at the top of the map. For example:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* “The salary is higher.”
* “The commute is shorter.”
* “The company has better promotion prospects.”

</div>

These are not the decision. They are reasons.

The actual conclusion might be:

* “I should accept Job Offer A.”

Argument-mapping literature frequently distinguishes between the main conclusion and intermediate conclusions. Intermediate conclusions function both as conclusions and as premises: they are supported by evidence and then go on to support a higher-level claim.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lsatdemon.com/resources/logical-reasoning/argument-parts-and-indicators" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lsatdemon.com">[Humanities LibreTexts+3LSAT Demon+3PastPaperHero]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lsatdemon.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">argument parts and indicators</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">LSAT DemonArgument Parts and Indicators6 Mar 2022 — An intermediate conclusion is a conclusion that supports the main conclusion. Think o...</span></span></span>

A simple diagnostic question helps identify the true top-level conclusion:

**If all these reasons were proven true, what decision would follow?**

The answer is usually the main conclusion.

For example:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Evidence: Salary is 20% higher.
* Evidence: Career progression appears faster.
* Intermediate conclusion: Offer A provides stronger long-term career value.
* Main conclusion: I should accept Offer A.

</div>

Without separating these levels, maps become confused. A person may spend hours debating salary data while never clearly stating the decision the salary is supposed to justify.

## Using Job Offers as a Conclusion-First Example

Imagine choosing between two jobs.

A topic-first approach often produces a list:

* Salary
* Culture
* Location
* Flexibility
* Benefits

The list is useful but lacks direction.

A conclusion-first map begins with:

**“I should accept the offer from Company A.”**

The next level contains supporting and opposing reasons.

Supporting reasons:

* [Total compensation]({{ 'total-pay/' | relative_url }}) is higher.
* Promotion opportunities appear stronger.
* The role develops skills aligned with long-term goals.

Objections:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Longer commute.
* Greater workload uncertainty.
* Smaller professional network in the new organisation.

</div>

Each branch can then be tested independently.

Notice what changes. The discussion is no longer about whether salary is “good”. It is about whether salary contributes enough support to justify the specific conclusion. This keeps analytical effort focused on the actual decision rather than on isolated factors.

## Using Policy Choices as a Conclusion-First Example

The same principle works for public or organisational decisions.

A policy discussion often starts with a vague subject such as “school uniforms” or “congestion charging”. Participants then talk past one another because different people are defending different conclusions.

A conclusion-first map begins with a statement such as:

**“The school should introduce a uniform policy next year.”**

Supporters and critics can now attach reasons to the same claim.

Support might include:

* Reduced visible economic differences among pupils.
* Simpler dress-code enforcement.

Objections might include:

* Additional costs for families.
* Reduced student autonomy.

The advantage is not that disagreement disappears. Rather, the disagreement becomes easier to locate. Participants can identify whether they dispute the evidence, the assumptions, or the conclusion itself.

## Common Mistakes When Building Decision Claims

Even when people try to start with a conclusion, several predictable problems appear.

### Conclusions that are too vague

A claim such as “remote work is good” is difficult to evaluate because it is unclear what action follows.

A stronger conclusion is:

“Our department should continue remote work three days per week.”

The revised version identifies a decision rather than a general attitude.

### Conclusions that hide multiple decisions

Consider:

“We should move offices, restructure the team, and adopt a new software platform.”

This bundles several decisions together.

A better approach is to map each decision separately and then examine any relationships between them.

### Conclusions that merely restate evidence

People sometimes place a factual observation at the top:

“Employee satisfaction increased.”

That may be true, but it is not necessarily the decision being defended.

The real conclusion might be:

“Because employee satisfaction increased, the flexible-working policy should continue.”

The observation becomes evidence rather than the conclusion.

## Why Conclusion-First Mapping Improves Thinking

Conclusion-first maps create a discipline that ordinary discussion often lacks. By requiring a clear decision claim at the top, they prevent the map from drifting into brainstorming, information dumping, or debates over side issues.

The practical benefits are significant:

* **Better relevance:** evidence must connect to the stated conclusion.
* **Clearer disagreement:** objections can target specific [claims]({{ 'claims/' | relative_url }}).

* **Reduced ambiguity:** everyone knows what is being defended.

If the conclusion is explicit and testable, the map gains a clear purpose: showing whether the decision really deserves support.
