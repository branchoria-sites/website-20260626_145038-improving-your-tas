## Introduction

Confirmation bias turns evidence gathering into advocacy when the purpose of a search quietly shifts from discovering what is true to defending what already feels true. The change is often subtle. Instead of asking, “What does the evidence show?”, people ask, “Why am I right?” or “Where can I find support for the conclusion I already prefer?” The result is not simply an incomplete search. It is a search strategy designed—often unconsciously—to make one answer appear stronger than it really is. This matters because many important decisions in hiring, health and finance are made under uncertainty, where selective evidence can create unwarranted confidence rather than better judgement. Research consistently shows that confirmation bias affects what people look for, how they interpret ambiguous information and what they remember afterwards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pages.ucsd.edu/~mckenzie/nickersonConfirmationBias.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pages.ucsd.edu">[UCSD Pages]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pages.ucsd.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pages Confirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous Phenomenon in Many</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UCSD PagesConfirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous Phenomenon in Many...October 6, 2004 — by RS Nickerson · 1998 · Cited by 12455 — Confirmation...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 6, 2004</span></span></span>

## How a search becomes an act of self-defence

Confirmation bias is commonly described as the tendency to seek, interpret and [recall]({{ 'recall/' | relative_url }}) information in ways that support an existing belief. However, the more revealing mechanism is that the search itself changes purpose. Instead of treating evidence as something that could overturn a hypothesis, people begin treating evidence as something that should justify it. Raymond Nickerson's influential review argues that this process is usually not deliberate deception. Rather, people unknowingly become selective in how they acquire and use evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pages.ucsd.edu/~mckenzie/nickersonConfirmationBias.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pages.ucsd.edu">[UCSD Pages]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pages.ucsd.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pages Confirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous Phenomenon in Many</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UCSD PagesConfirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous Phenomenon in Many...October 6, 2004 — by RS Nickerson · 1998 · Cited by 12455 — Confirmation...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 6, 2004</span></span></span>

The shift often follows a simple sequence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. A person forms an early preference or impression.
2. That preference becomes the working hypothesis.
3. Questions are framed to confirm rather than challenge it.
4. Supporting evidence feels persuasive because contradictory evidence was never actively sought.
5. Confidence increases, even if the overall quality of evidence has not improved.

</div>

The critical point is that confidence grows because the search was one-sided, not because the evidence genuinely became stronger.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pages.ucsd.edu/~mckenzie/nickersonConfirmationBias.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pages.ucsd.edu">[UCSD Pages]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pages.ucsd.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pages Confirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous Phenomenon in Many</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UCSD PagesConfirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous Phenomenon in Many...October 6, 2004 — by RS Nickerson · 1998 · Cited by 12455 — Confirmation...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 6, 2004</span></span></span>

## One-sided questions create one-sided evidence

The wording of a question determines the evidence that can be found.

A neutral investigator asks:

* What explanations best fit the evidence?
* What evidence would distinguish between competing explanations?

An advocate tends to ask:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Why is this idea correct?
* What evidence supports this conclusion?
* Who agrees with me?

</div>

Those questions almost guarantee supportive answers because they exclude competing possibilities before the search begins.

Psychological research on hypothesis [testing]({{ 'testing/' | relative_url }}) has repeatedly shown that people often favour "positive tests"—looking for evidence expected if their hypothesis is true—rather than seeking observations that would prove it wrong. This approach is efficient in many situations but becomes misleading when several competing explanations could also fit the available evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confirmation_bias" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Confirmation bias</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Confirmation bias</span></span></span>

The internet amplifies this tendency. Search engines can return excellent information, but if someone searches for "benefits of investment X" rather than "evidence against investment X" or "risks of investment X", the initial query has already narrowed the information landscape.

## Why supportive evidence feels more convincing than it is

Confirmation bias is not simply collecting favourable information. It also changes how evidence is interpreted.

When people encounter:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* evidence supporting their belief, they often treat it as reliable and representative;
* ambiguous evidence, they interpret it in the direction they already favour;
* contradictory evidence, they scrutinise its weaknesses more aggressively than they scrutinised supporting evidence.

</div>

This creates an uneven standard of proof. Evidence that agrees with existing beliefs receives relatively little scepticism, while opposing evidence is expected to overcome a much higher bar.

Because these different standards are applied unconsciously, people often believe they are evaluating both sides equally, even when they are not.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pages.ucsd.edu/~mckenzie/nickersonConfirmationBias.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pages.ucsd.edu">[UCSD Pages]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pages.ucsd.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pages Confirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous Phenomenon in Many</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UCSD PagesConfirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous Phenomenon in Many...October 6, 2004 — by RS Nickerson · 1998 · Cited by 12455 — Confirmation...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 6, 2004</span></span></span>

## How it appears in hiring, health and finance

### Hiring

A manager may form a positive impression within the first few minutes of an interview.

From that point onward, later questions subtly become confirmation exercises:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* asking about achievements rather than failures;
* interpreting confident behaviour as evidence of competence;
* overlooking inconsistent answers;
* remembering information that fits the favourable first impression.

</div>

The interview gradually becomes an attempt to justify the initial judgement instead of testing whether it survives further evidence. This is one reason structured interviews, standardised questions and independent scoring generally produce better hiring decisions than informal conversations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confirmation_bias" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Confirmation bias</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Confirmation bias</span></span></span>

### Health

Someone who wants a particular treatment may search almost exclusively for success stories, [testimonials]({{ 'testimonials/' | relative_url }}) or articles supporting it while avoiding systematic reviews or clinical guidelines that discuss limitations.

Conversely, someone convinced a treatment is dangerous may seek only reports of adverse effects while overlooking evidence about effectiveness and risk magnitude.

In both cases, the search protects the preferred conclusion rather than evaluating the overall balance of evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pages.ucsd.edu/~mckenzie/nickersonConfirmationBias.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pages.ucsd.edu">[UCSD Pages]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pages.ucsd.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pages Confirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous Phenomenon in Many</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UCSD PagesConfirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous Phenomenon in Many...October 6, 2004 — by RS Nickerson · 1998 · Cited by 12455 — Confirmation...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 6, 2004</span></span></span>

### Finance

Investors frequently become emotionally attached to a company or investment thesis.

Once committed, searches often change from:

> "Should I own this investment?"

to

> "Why will this investment succeed?"

Negative earnings reports, changing market conditions or competing analyses may receive much less attention than optimistic forecasts that reinforce the existing position.

The danger is not simply missing information. It is gradually constructing an increasingly persuasive narrative from a selectively chosen evidence base.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pages.ucsd.edu/~mckenzie/nickersonConfirmationBias.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pages.ucsd.edu">[UCSD Pages]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pages.ucsd.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pages Confirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous Phenomenon in Many</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UCSD PagesConfirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous Phenomenon in Many...October 6, 2004 — by RS Nickerson · 1998 · Cited by 12455 — Confirmation...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 6, 2004</span></span></span>

## Looking for disconfirming evidence

The most effective safeguard is usually not trying to suppress bias but deliberately changing the search process.

A practical routine is to identify the strongest reason you might be wrong before collecting further evidence.

Useful questions include:

* What evidence would genuinely [change my mind]({{ 'change-mind/' | relative_url }})?

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Which explanation competes most seriously with mine?
* What would someone who disagrees point to first?
* If I wanted to prove my current conclusion false, where would I start?

</div>

This shifts the search from defending one explanation to comparing several plausible alternatives.

Experimental work by Lord, Lepper and Preston found that prompting people to "consider the opposite" reduced biased social judgement more effectively than simply instructing them to avoid bias. The intervention worked because it required participants to actively generate alternative explanations rather than merely intending to be objective.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6527215/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Considering the opposite: a corrective strategy for social...by CG Lord · 1984 · Cited by 1170 — It is proposed that several biase...</span></span></span>

## Practical habits that keep search analytical rather than defensive

Small procedural changes often work better than relying on willpower alone.

Before beginning a search:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Write your current conclusion separately from your confidence in it.
* State what evidence would lower your confidence.
* List at least one plausible competing explanation.

</div>

While searching:

* Look for the strongest opposing source before reading additional supporting material.
* Search symmetrical questions, such as both "benefits" and "risks".
* Compare evidence quality rather than counting supporting articles.

After searching:

* Ask whether your conclusion changed because of stronger evidence or simply because you found more agreement.
* Check whether you remember contrary evidence as clearly as supportive evidence.
* Imagine explaining your decision to someone who holds the opposite view.

These routines do not eliminate confirmation bias, but they interrupt its most important mechanism: turning evidence gathering into an exercise in protecting an answer already chosen.

## The key lesson

Confirmation bias rarely begins with ignoring evidence. It usually begins earlier, with the questions people ask. A search framed as "Help me defend this conclusion" naturally accumulates confirming information, increasing confidence without necessarily increasing accuracy. Reframing the task as "Help me discover whether this conclusion survives serious challenge" changes the purpose of evidence gathering from advocacy back to inquiry, making better judgement far more likely.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pages.ucsd.edu/~mckenzie/nickersonConfirmationBias.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pages.ucsd.edu">[UCSD Pages+2PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pages.ucsd.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pages Confirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous Phenomenon in Many</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UCSD PagesConfirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous Phenomenon in Many...October 6, 2004 — by RS Nickerson · 1998 · Cited by 12455 — Confirmation...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 6, 2004</span></span></span>
