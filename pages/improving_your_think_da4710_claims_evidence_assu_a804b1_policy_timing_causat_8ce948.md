## Introduction

When a policy is introduced and a bad outcome follows, many people instinctively connect the two. A rise in crime after a policing reform, higher prices after a regulation, or worse exam results after an education change can all seem like straightforward evidence that the policy failed. The timing feels convincing because the policy came first and the problem came second.


<img src="{{ "/assets/images/improving_your_think_da4710_claims_evidence_assu_a804b1_policy_timing_causat_8ce948-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Policy Timing illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/improving_your_think_da4710_claims_evidence_assu_a804b1_policy_timing_causat_8ce948-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/improving_your_think_da4710_claims_evidence_assu_a804b1_policy_timing_causat_8ce948-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">
Yet timing alone is rarely enough to establish cause and effect. In everyday policy debates, one of the most common reasoning errors is to treat a before-and-after sequence as proof that the policy produced the outcome. To judge whether a policy really failed, we must ask a more demanding question: what would likely have happened if the policy had not been introduced? Answering that question requires considering background trends, [rival explanations]({{ 'rival-test/' | relative_url }}), and the mechanism by which the policy is supposed to affect [outcomes]({{ 'outcomes/' | relative_url }}). Modern policy evaluation methods are built around precisely this challenge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whatworksgrowth.org/resource-library/using-impact-evaluation-evidence-in-policymaking/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whatworksgrowth.org">[What Works Growth]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whatworksgrowth.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">using impact evaluation evidence in policymaking</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">What Works GrowthUsing impact evaluation evidence in policymaking30 Jan 2025 — Impact evaluation evidence helps us understand which polic...</span></span></span>

## Did the Policy Fail, or Just Come First?

Before-and-after arguments have a simple structure:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A policy was introduced.
* An outcome occurred afterwards.
* Therefore, the policy caused the outcome.

</div>

The problem is that many things change over time. Economies expand and contract, populations age, technologies spread, weather varies, and social behaviour shifts. Any of these factors may influence the outcome being discussed.

Imagine a city introduces a new transport policy in January and traffic congestion worsens by June. The worsening congestion could be caused by the policy. But it could also reflect population growth, major roadworks, changes in commuting patterns, or a broader regional trend. The fact that congestion increased after the policy does not by itself distinguish among these possibilities.

Critical thinking therefore requires separating temporal order from causal evidence. A cause must come before its effect, but not everything that comes before an event is its cause.

## Why Timing Feels Persuasive

Humans are naturally sensitive to sequences of events. Detecting patterns is often useful, and in daily life many genuine causes do precede their effects. If a person touches a hot stove and immediately feels pain, timing is an important clue.

Policy interventions, however, occur in complex systems involving thousands or millions of people. Multiple influences operate simultaneously, and effects may emerge gradually rather than instantly. This complexity makes simple before-and-after reasoning much less reliable.

Timing becomes especially persuasive when:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The outcome is emotionally charged.
* The policy was controversial before implementation.
* The outcome matches prior expectations.
* The policy change was highly visible while other influences were less obvious.

</div>

In public debate, a dramatic event shortly after a policy change often receives disproportionate attention. A single negative outcome may be presented as proof of failure even when longer-term data point in a different direction.

This tendency illustrates a broader analytical lesson: evidence that fits a preferred story often feels stronger than it really is.

## Coincidence, Background Trends, and Rival Causes

The strongest challenge to a simple before-and-after claim is the existence of alternative explanations.

### Background trends can mislead

Suppose unemployment rises after a government introduces a job-training programme. Critics may claim the programme failed. Yet if unemployment was already rising before the programme began, or if a national recession affected many regions simultaneously, the increase may tell us little about the programme itself.

Policy analysts therefore examine trends before the intervention. If the outcome was already moving in the same direction, attributing the change solely to the new policy becomes much harder.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/heapol/article/27/1/76/659122" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicHow to do (or not to do) … Assessing the impact of a policy...by M Lagarde · 2012 · Cited by 386 — The objective of this art...</span></span></span>

### Other events may occur at the same time

Policies rarely operate in isolation. A tax change may coincide with shifts in consumer confidence. A public-health policy may overlap with seasonal disease patterns. An education reform may occur alongside curriculum changes, teacher shortages, or demographic shifts.

These competing influences are often called confounders or rival causes because they can create the appearance of a policy effect even when the policy itself played little or no role. Researchers studying policy impacts devote substantial effort to identifying and controlling for such factors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://evidenceforaction.org/blog-posts/brief-discussion-time-varying-confounding-evaluation-programs-practices-or-policies" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: evidenceforaction.org">[evidenceforaction.org+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">evidenceforaction.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A Brief Discussion on Time-Varying Confounding in...1 Feb 2021 — Confounding is a major threat to the validity of all observational stud...</span></span></span>

### Some outcomes fluctuate naturally

Many social indicators rise and fall over time even without intervention. Crime rates, inflation, employment, and health outcomes often show natural variation.

If observers focus only on two points in time—before the policy and after the policy—they may mistake ordinary fluctuation for evidence of success or failure. Looking at longer trends helps reveal whether an apparent change is unusual or simply part of an ongoing pattern.

## Evidence That Strengthens a Policy Failure Claim

A before-and-after comparison is not useless. It can provide an important clue. The question is what additional evidence makes the claim more convincing.

### A plausible mechanism

A stronger argument explains how the policy could realistically produce the outcome.

For example, if a regulation increases compliance costs for businesses, and those costs are expected to raise prices, then subsequent price increases fit a clear causal pathway. The presence of a believable mechanism does not prove the claim, but it makes it more credible than a mere coincidence in timing.

### Comparison with a credible counterfactual

The central challenge in policy evaluation is determining what would have happened without the policy. Because we cannot observe both realities at once, analysts seek comparison groups or alternative benchmarks.

One widely used approach compares changes in an affected group with changes in a similar unaffected group. If outcomes worsen only where the policy was implemented while remaining stable elsewhere, the case for [causation]({{ 'causation/' | relative_url }}) becomes stronger. This family of methods, often called difference-in-differences analysis, is designed specifically to move beyond simple before-and-after comparisons.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11305929/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2Lippincott Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Advances in Difference-in-differences Methods for Policy...by G Wang · 2024 · Cited by 121 — Difference-in-differences (DiD) is a pow...</span></span></span>

### Multiple observations over time

A stronger assessment uses many observations before and after implementation rather than a single snapshot.

If a policy is followed by a clear and sustained break from a long-established trend, that pattern provides more persuasive evidence than one isolated post-policy data point. Researchers often use interrupted time-series designs to examine whether a trend changed noticeably after a policy was introduced.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/heapol/article/27/1/76/659122" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicHow to do (or not to do) … Assessing the impact of a policy...by M Lagarde · 2012 · Cited by 386 — The objective of this art...</span></span></span>

### Ruling out rival explanations

Confidence increases when alternative causes are investigated and found insufficient.

Suppose a policy is blamed for rising prices. Analysts would ask:

* Did similar price increases occur in places without the policy?
* Were there supply shocks affecting the entire market?
* Did input costs change at the same time?
* Did broader economic conditions [explain]({{ 'explain/' | relative_url }}) the increase?

The more thoroughly competing explanations are examined, the stronger a causal claim becomes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10538408/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2RAND]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Identifying Optimal Methods for Addressing Confounding Bias...by BA Griffin · 2023 · Cited by 9 — We conducted a simulation study to...</span></span></span>

## A Practical Test for News and Public Debate

When encountering a claim that a policy succeeded or failed because conditions changed afterwards, ask four questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **What exactly changed after the policy?**
2. **Was the same trend already present beforehand?**
3. **What other events or influences occurred during the same period?**
4. **What evidence shows that a similar group without the policy experienced a different outcome?**

</div>

If these questions remain unanswered, the argument may rely mainly on timing rather than on convincing causal evidence.

This does not mean the policy worked or failed. It means the available evidence has not yet established which explanation is most likely.

## What This Teaches About Everyday Reasoning

Before-and-after evidence is often the starting point of policy evaluation, not the endpoint. A bad outcome following a policy change may signal a genuine problem, but it may also reflect background trends, coincidence, or other causes operating at the same time.

For anyone seeking to improve analytical thinking, the key lesson is simple: when someone says a policy failed because things got worse afterwards, do not stop at the timeline. Ask what evidence connects the policy to the outcome, what rival explanations have been considered, and whether the observed change differs from what would probably have happened anyway. That shift—from noticing sequence to [testing]({{ 'testing/' | relative_url }}) causation—is one of the most valuable habits in evaluating public arguments.
