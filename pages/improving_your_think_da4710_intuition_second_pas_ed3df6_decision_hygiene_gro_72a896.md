## Introduction

When fast [intuition]({{ 'intuition/' | relative_url }}) needs a second pass, groups face a special problem: a room full of intelligent people can still reach poor conclusions. The issue is not only bias—systematic errors that push judgments in one direction—but also noise, the unwanted variation that causes different people, or even the same person at different times, to make inconsistent judgments. Research on decision hygiene argues that many group failures arise from process design rather than a lack of intelligence or integrity. ## Bias Versus Noise in Judgment

Many people understand bias. If a hiring committee consistently favours candidates from a particular background, the error has a direction. Noise is different. It appears when equally qualified candidates receive very different evaluations from different interviewers, or when a project proposal receives dramatically different scores depending on who reviews it and when. Noise is often less visible than bias because inconsistency can look like normal disagreement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theuncertaintyproject.org/threads/applying-decision-hygiene-to-yield-better-judgment" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theuncertaintyproject.org">[The Uncertainty Project+2Jury Analyst]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theuncertaintyproject.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">They use an archery analogy to illustrate the difference.Read more</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Uncertainty ProjectApplying Decision Hygiene to yield better judgment12 Apr 2024 — The authors establish that good judgment can be ha...</span></span></span>

This distinction matters because eliminating bias does not automatically eliminate noise. A group can be unbiased on average yet still make unreliable decisions because individual judgments vary unpredictably. Kahneman, Olivier Sibony, and Cass Sunstein argue that organisations frequently focus on fairness and prejudice while overlooking the equally important problem of inconsistent judgment. The practical consequence is that two committees using identical information may reach different conclusions simply because members interpret evidence differently, emphasise different criteria, or are influenced by the order in which information appears. Decision hygiene seeks to reduce this variation by making judgment processes more consistent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scholarworks.bgsu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1039&amp;context=management_pub" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scholarworks.bgsu.edu">[scholarworks.bgsu.edu+2Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scholarworks.bgsu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Some assert that noise (i.e., unwanted variance) is the most neglected yet most important source of error in judgment.Read more...</span></span></span>

## Why Discussion Can Corrupt Good Judgments

Most groups assume discussion should begin immediately. Yet one of the strongest findings in decision-hygiene research is that early discussion often contaminates information.

When people exchange views before recording independent judgments, several effects can occur:

* Early speakers anchor the conversation.
* Senior or confident members gain disproportionate influence.
* Members suppress minority opinions.
* New information becomes filtered through emerging group narratives.
* Independent evidence is lost because participants converge too quickly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.barrons.com/articles/economist-daniel-kahneman-says-noise-is-wrecking-your-judgment-heres-why-and-what-to-do-about-it-51622228892" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: barrons.com">[Barron&#x27;s+2The Decision Lab]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">barrons.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Barron&#x27;s Daniel Kahneman Says Noise Is Wrecking Your Judgment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Here&#x27;s Why, and What to Do About It.March 28, 2024 — Daniel Kahneman, a renowned psychologist and behavioral economist, explored the conc...</span></span></span>

Kahneman has argued that meetings work best when participants arrive with pre-formed individual judgments. Discussion should compare those judgments rather than create them from scratch. Otherwise, the group risks replacing multiple independent signals with a single socially constructed opinion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.barrons.com/articles/economist-daniel-kahneman-says-noise-is-wrecking-your-judgment-heres-why-and-what-to-do-about-it-51622228892" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: barrons.com">[Barron&#x27;s]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">barrons.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Barron&#x27;s Daniel Kahneman Says Noise Is Wrecking Your Judgment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Here&#x27;s Why, and What to Do About It.March 28, 2024 — Daniel Kahneman, a renowned psychologist and behavioral economist, explored the conc...</span></span></span>

Research on collective decision-making supports this concern. Diverse groups can outperform individuals when they contribute genuinely independent perspectives. ## Independent Assessments Before Discussion

A core decision-hygiene principle is simple: judge first, discuss second.

Instead of opening with debate, each participant evaluates options independently. Scores, rankings, forecasts, or written assessments are collected before anyone sees other people's views. Only then does discussion begin.

This approach produces several benefits:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Preserves information diversity.** Independent judgments reveal genuine differences rather than socially influenced agreement.
2. **Reduces authority effects.** Senior members cannot shape every assessment before it is recorded.
3. **Creates an audit trail.** Groups can examine how discussion changed opinions.

</div>

4. **Improves aggregation.** Multiple independent judgments often outperform a single consensus judgment. [Barron's+2William Meller](https://www.barrons.com/articles/economist-daniel-kahneman-says-noise-is-wrecking-your-judgment-heres-why-and-what-to-do-about-it-51622228892)

The principle appears in many high-stakes settings. Investment committees often collect forecasts before meetings. Forecasting tournaments require participants to submit initial [probabilities]({{ 'probabilities/' | relative_url }}) independently. Academic peer review relies on separate reviews before reviewers see one another's comments. Even where discussion remains important, independence is preserved long enough to capture separate signals. [WCER+2PLOS](https://wcer.wisc.edu/docs/working-papers/Working_Paper_No_2015_06.pdf)

Anonymity can sometimes strengthen this effect. Studies of peer review and evaluation processes suggest that reducing status cues can lessen the influence of hierarchy and reputation on collective judgments, although anonymity alone does not solve every decision-quality problem. [PLOS+2PMC](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0315674)

## Structured Criteria for Hiring Decisions

Hiring provides one of the clearest demonstrations of decision hygiene.

Unstructured interviews often feel insightful because interviewers can follow intuition and explore whatever topics seem interesting. Yet research repeatedly finds that structured interviews tend to be more reliable predictors of performance. The reason is not that intuition disappears; rather, intuition is delayed until after evidence has been gathered systematically. [conversableeconomist.com](https://conversableeconomist.com/2022/04/15/interview-with-daniel-kahneman-noise-and-decision-making/)

[A decision-hygiene hiring process typically involves:](#endnote-15 "
<details class="endnote-snippet"><summary>Source snippet</summary><p>It&#x27;s not only noise reducing, but if you have an...Read more&quot;)...</p></details>

* Defining key competencies in advance.
* Evaluating each competency separately.
* Using common questions across candidates.
* Recording scores before discussion.
* Delaying overall impressions until the end. [conversableeconomist.com+2William Meller](https://conversableeconomist.com/2022/04/15/interview-with-daniel-kahneman-noise-and-decision-making/)

Kahneman has described structured interviewing as breaking a complex judgment into components, scoring each component independently, and postponing the global evaluation until all evidence has been reviewed. This sequencing reduces halo effects, where one strong or weak characteristic colours every other judgment. [conversableeconomist.com](https://conversableeconomist.com/2022/04/15/interview-with-daniel-kahneman-noise-and-decision-making/)

Importantly, decision hygiene does not eliminate intuition altogether. Interviewers may still form an overall impression. The difference is that intuition becomes the final step rather than the first. Structured evidence constrains instinct instead of allowing instinct to shape the evidence. [Medium](https://natematias.medium.com/bias-and-noise-daniel-kahneman-onerrors-in-decision-making-6bc844ff5194)

## Structured Criteria for Projects and Investments

Project selection committees frequently face uncertainty, competing priorities, and persuasive advocates. Under these conditions, discussions can become dominated by confidence, storytelling ability, or political influence.

Decision hygiene encourages groups to replace vague judgments such as "This feels promising" with explicit criteria. For example, project proposals might be scored separately on:

* Expected value.
* Strategic fit.
* Cost.
* Technical feasibility.
* Risk.
* Time to implementation.

Separate assessments make disagreements more visible. Committee members can identify whether they disagree about feasibility, expected benefits, or risk rather than arguing about an overall impression. [Wiley Online Library+2tosummarise.com](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/aaai.12135)

This decomposition also helps combat what researchers have called uniqueness bias: the tendency to treat a project as so special that comparisons become impossible. Structured comparison with previous cases, reference classes, and historical [outcomes]({{ 'outcomes/' | relative_url }}) provides a corrective against overconfident intuition. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.07710)

## Structured Criteria for Family Decisions

Decision hygiene is not limited to organisations. Families can benefit from the same principles when facing major choices such as moving house, selecting schools, caring for relatives, or making large purchases.

Family discussions often suffer from hidden [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}). One person may care most about cost, another about convenience, another about long-term opportunities. Without structure, conversations drift into emotional arguments because participants are debating different criteria without realising it.

A simple decision-hygiene approach can include:

* Agreeing on evaluation criteria beforehand.
* Having each person score options independently.
* Comparing scores before debating.
* Identifying the largest disagreements.
* Allowing final intuition only after evidence has been reviewed.

The goal is not to make family decisions mechanical. Rather, it is to ensure that preferences, values, and evidence become visible before personalities and emotions dominate the conversation.

## Delayed Final Intuition

One of the most misunderstood aspects of decision hygiene is that it does not seek to eliminate intuition. Instead, it changes when intuition enters the process.

The sequence matters:

1. Define criteria.
2. Gather evidence. [3. Make independent assessments.](#endnote-7 "
<details class="endnote-snippet"><summary>Source snippet</summary><p>CommoncogHow To Reduce Decision Noise9 Feb 2021 — 1) Noise Audits · 2) Reasoned Rules · 3) Aggregate Judgments · 4) Use Algorithms, But M&quot;)...</p></details>

4. Compare and discuss. [5. Aggregate judgments.](#endnote-7 "
<details class="endnote-snippet"><summary>Source snippet</summary><p>CommoncogHow To Reduce Decision Noise9 Feb 2021 — 1) Noise Audits · 2) Reasoned Rules · 3) Aggregate Judgments · 4) Use Algorithms, But M&quot;)...</p></details>

6. Apply overall intuition at the end.

This order protects intuition from contamination by first impressions while still allowing experienced decision-makers to notice patterns that formal criteria may miss. Kahneman has repeatedly argued that delaying global evaluation is a key safeguard against noisy and biased judgments. [conversableeconomist.com+2William Meller](https://conversableeconomist.com/2022/04/15/interview-with-daniel-kahneman-noise-and-decision-making/)

Groups often fear that structure will make decisions rigid or bureaucratic. In [practice]({{ 'practice/' | relative_url }}), good decision hygiene usually preserves discretion while improving consistency. The purpose is not to replace human judgment but to create conditions under which judgment performs better.

## What Good Decision Hygiene Looks Like

Groups that consistently make better judgments tend to share a few procedural habits:

* Independent evaluations before discussion.
* Explicit scoring criteria. [* Separation of evidence gathering from decision making.](#endnote-22 "
<details class="endnote-snippet"><summary>Source snippet</summary><p>(PDF) Group decision makingPDF | On Jan 1, 2003, R Scott Tindale and others published Group decision making | Find, read and cite all the&quot;)...</p></details>
* Aggregation of multiple judgments rather than reliance on a single authority.
* Delayed overall impressions.
* Regular review of past decisions to identify patterns of inconsistency. [Commoncog+2Issues in Science and Technology](https://commoncog.com/how-to-reduce-decision-noise/)

These practices matter because the quality of a group decision depends not only on who participates but also on how the decision is made. When intuition receives a structured second pass, groups become less vulnerable to both systematic bias and random noise. The result is not perfect judgment, but a process that is more reliable, fairer, and more likely to capture the collective intelligence of the people involved. [PMC+2Issues in Science and Technology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5579088/)
