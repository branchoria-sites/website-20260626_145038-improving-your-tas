## Introduction

The American Philosophical Association's Delphi Report remains one of the most influential attempts to define what counts as good critical judgement. Rather than treating critical thinking as intelligence, scepticism, or debate skill, the report defines it as **purposeful, self-regulatory judgement** aimed at deciding *what to believe or what to do*. That judgement is not a single mental act but a coordinated process involving interpretation, analysis, evaluation, inference, and explanation, guided by reflective self-correction. The definition emerged from a structured Delphi study in which experts from philosophy, education, psychology and related disciplines worked towards consensus on the skills and dispositions that should underpin critical-thinking education and assessment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED315423.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.eric.ed.gov">[ERIC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.eric.ed.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ERICFebruary 20, 2011 — by PA Facione · 1990 · Cited by 1116 — We understand critical thinking to be purposeful, self-regulatory judgment...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 20, 2011</span></span></span>

Within the broader idea of purposeful judgement, the Delphi standards are valuable because they make good thinking observable. Instead of asking whether someone is "a critical thinker", they ask whether a judgement has been reached through identifiable reasoning processes and whether the reasoning can be explained and revised when necessary.

## What Counts as a Good Critical Judgement?

The Delphi Report does not describe critical thinking as a collection of isolated techniques. It presents judgement as an integrated process in which several cognitive skills support one another. A strong judgement is one that is based on evidence, sensitive to context, open to revision, and capable of being justified to others.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED315423.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.eric.ed.gov">[ERIC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.eric.ed.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ERICFebruary 20, 2011 — by PA Facione · 1990 · Cited by 1116 — We understand critical thinking to be purposeful, self-regulatory judgment...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 20, 2011</span></span></span>

The report identifies six core cognitive skills.

* **Interpretation** – understanding the meaning of information, experiences, data or events. Before evaluating evidence, a thinker must first determine what is actually being claimed or observed.
* **Analysis** – identifying arguments, [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}), relationships and reasons. Analysis separates conclusions from supporting evidence and distinguishes relevant from irrelevant information.
* **Evaluation** – assessing the credibility of sources and the strength of arguments. This includes judging whether evidence genuinely supports a conclusion and recognising weaknesses or uncertainty.
* **Inference** – drawing reasonable conclusions from available evidence while recognising the limits of what the evidence can justify.
* **Explanation** – clearly stating both the conclusion and the reasoning that produced it, including the evidence, methods and standards relied upon.
* **[Self-regulation]({{ 'self-regulation/' | relative_url }})** – monitoring one's own thinking, recognising possible bias or error, and revising judgements when new evidence warrants it. Although often highlighted separately in later summaries, reflective self-correction is built into the report's overall definition of critical thinking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED315423.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.eric.ed.gov">[ERIC+2Insight Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.eric.ed.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ERICFebruary 20, 2011 — by PA Facione · 1990 · Cited by 1116 — We understand critical thinking to be purposeful, self-regulatory judgment...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 20, 2011</span></span></span>

These skills are intended to work together rather than in sequence. Interpretation informs analysis; analysis supports evaluation; evaluation shapes inference; explanation communicates the judgement; self-regulation continually checks every stage.

## Why Explanation Matters After Inference

One of the Delphi Report's distinctive contributions is its insistence that arriving at a conclusion is not enough. A judgement becomes intellectually accountable only when the thinker can [explain]({{ 'explain/' | relative_url }}) **why** that conclusion follows from the available evidence.

The report therefore treats explanation as more than communication. Explanation requires identifying the evidential, conceptual, methodological, contextual or evaluative considerations that justify the judgement. If two people reach the same conclusion for entirely different reasons, the quality of their critical thinking may differ substantially because only one may be able to demonstrate sound reasoning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED315423.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.eric.ed.gov">[ERIC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.eric.ed.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ERICFebruary 20, 2011 — by PA Facione · 1990 · Cited by 1116 — We understand critical thinking to be purposeful, self-regulatory judgment...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 20, 2011</span></span></span>

This emphasis has practical consequences.


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* It exposes hidden assumptions.
* It allows others to evaluate the reasoning instead of merely accepting the conclusion.
* It makes disagreement more productive because people can compare reasons rather than simply opposing opinions.
* It supports learning, since weak reasoning becomes visible and therefore improvable.

</div>

In educational settings, this explains why assessments of critical thinking often ask students to justify answers rather than merely selecting the correct option. The explanation reveals the quality of judgement that the final answer alone cannot show.

## The Ideal Critical Thinker Is More Than a Skilled Reasoner

The Delphi project did not stop with defining cognitive skills. The expert panel also described the personal dispositions associated with high-quality judgement. Their "ideal critical thinker" combines reasoning ability with habits of mind that encourage those abilities to be used consistently.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://insightassessment.com/iaresource/the-delphi-report-a-statement-of-expert-consensus-on-the-definition-of-critical-thinking/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insightassessment.com">[Insight Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insightassessment.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Insight Assessment The Delphi Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This human cognitive process of reflectively deciding what to believe or what to do...Read more...</span></span></span>

According to the consensus description, an ideal critical thinker is characterised by qualities such as:

* inquisitiveness and intellectual curiosity;
* being well informed while recognising the limits of one's knowledge;
* trust in reasoned inquiry rather than impulse or authority alone;
* [open-mindedness]({{ 'open-mindedness/' | relative_url }}) towards alternative viewpoints;

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* flexibility when considering different possibilities;
* fairness in evaluating competing arguments;
* honesty about personal biases and assumptions;
* willingness to reconsider conclusions when evidence changes;
* precision where the situation demands it;
* persistence when problems are complex.

</div>

These characteristics matter because technical reasoning skills alone do not guarantee sound judgement. Someone may be highly capable of analysing arguments yet consistently ignore evidence that challenges existing beliefs. The Delphi panel therefore argued that effective critical thinking depends on both competence and disposition: knowing how to think critically and being willing to do so.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED315423.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.eric.ed.gov">[ERIC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.eric.ed.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ERICFebruary 20, 2011 — by PA Facione · 1990 · Cited by 1116 — We understand critical thinking to be purposeful, self-regulatory judgment...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 20, 2011</span></span></span>

## Why the Delphi Standards Changed Critical-Thinking Education

Before the Delphi project, educators often agreed that critical thinking was important but disagreed about exactly what should be taught or assessed. The Delphi study used a structured expert-consensus method to identify shared principles across disciplines, producing a practical framework that could guide curriculum design and assessment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://philpapers.org/rec/FACCTA" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: philpapers.org">[PhilPapers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">philpapers.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Peter Facione, Critical Thinking: A Statement of Expert...by P Facione · 1990 · Cited by 6816 — This is the full version of th...</span></span></span>

Its influence can be seen in several areas:

* university critical-thinking courses organised around identifiable reasoning skills;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://faculty.uml.edu/robert_gamache/85.503/Facione2006CriticalThinking.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faculty.uml.edu">[faculty.uml.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faculty.uml.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The APA Delphi Report. Critical Thinking: A Statement of Expert. Consensus...Read more...</span></span></span>
* assessment instruments designed to measure interpretation, analysis, evaluation and inference rather than factual [recall]({{ 'recall/' | relative_url }}) alone;
* professional education, particularly in fields such as healthcare, where judgement under uncertainty is central to [practice]({{ 'practice/' | relative_url }});
* later research that expanded the framework by examining both cognitive skills and thinking dispositions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://faculty.uml.edu/robert_gamache/85.503/Facione2006CriticalThinking.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faculty.uml.edu">[faculty.uml.edu+2Insight Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faculty.uml.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The APA Delphi Report. ## What the Standards Mean for Everyday Thinking

The Delphi standards shift attention away from whether a conclusion happens to be correct and towards whether it has been reached responsibly. A good critical judgement is not simply the "right answer"; it is a conclusion supported by careful interpretation, sound analysis, balanced evaluation, justified inference and transparent explanation, all subject to ongoing self-correction.

This perspective also encourages appropriate confidence. A critical thinker may conclude that the evidence strongly supports a claim, weakly supports it, or is currently insufficient. In each case, the quality of judgement depends less on certainty than on whether the reasoning faithfully reflects the available evidence.
