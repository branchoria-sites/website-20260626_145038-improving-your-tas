## Introduction

Thinking can be taught directly, but not by asking learners to “be more critical” and hoping the skill appears. The strongest approach is to name the thinking move, model it, let learners practise it in real subject matter, and give [feedback]({{ 'feedback/' | relative_url }}) on the quality of their reasoning. This matters because analytical skill is often treated as a by-product of education, work experience or debate. The evidence points the other way: people improve most when reasoning is made visible and practised deliberately, especially through explicit critical-thinking goals, dialogue, authentic tasks, mentoring, metacognitive routines and feedback. A major meta-analysis of critical-thinking instruction found a positive average effect across experimental and quasi-experimental studies, while guidance on [metacognition]({{ 'metacognition/' | relative_url }}) similarly stresses explicit teaching, modelling and scaffolding of planning, monitoring and evaluation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654314551063" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[Sage Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sage JournalsStrategies for Teaching Students to Think CriticallyCT is an explicit objective in the infusion course but not in the immers...</span></span></span>

Within the broader goal of improving thinking and analytical skills, explicit instruction is the policy intervention that turns a vague aspiration into teachable [practice]({{ 'practice/' | relative_url }}). It asks schools, universities, professional training programmes and workplace learning systems to decide which reasoning skills they expect people to use, where those skills will be practised, how performance will be judged, and how learners will be helped to transfer the skill beyond the first example.

## What explicit thinking instruction changes

The central shift is from exposure to instruction. In an exposure model, learners read demanding texts, solve difficult problems, join discussions or complete projects, and the institution assumes that better thinking will emerge. Sometimes it does. Often, however, learners simply become more familiar with the content while keeping the same hidden reasoning habits: jumping from evidence to conclusion, overlooking alternatives, confusing confidence with accuracy, or failing to ask what would change their mind.

Explicit instruction makes the target skill public. Instead of saying “analyse this article”, a teacher might ask learners to identify the main claim, separate evidence from interpretation, list unstated [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}), compare two explanations, judge source reliability and [explain]({{ 'explain/' | relative_url }}) which piece of evidence carries the most weight. In science, the equivalent might be making a data-based decision under uncertainty; in history, weighing competing accounts; in law, distinguishing precedent from policy argument; in management, testing whether a diagnosis of a problem fits the available evidence.

This is not the same as reducing thinking to a rigid formula. The point is to give learners enough structure to see what good reasoning looks like before expecting independence. The Education Endowment Foundation’s guidance on metacognition describes this as helping pupils plan, monitor and evaluate their learning by explicitly teaching, modelling and scaffolding strategies, rather than assuming pupils will discover these routines on their own.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/metacognition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk">[EEF]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">EEFMetacognition and Self-Regulated Learning13 Nov 2025 — This guidance report reviews the... learning through explicitly teaching, mode...</span></span></span>

A simple lesson sequence captures the governance logic:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Name the move:** “Today we are distinguishing evidence from explanation.”
2. **Model the move:** the teacher thinks aloud through an example.
3. **Practise with support:** learners try the same move on a related problem.
4. **Give feedback:** the teacher comments on the reasoning, not just the answer.
5. **Fade support:** prompts are gradually removed.
6. **Transfer:** learners apply the move in a new subject, case or decision.

</div>

The governance challenge is that each step has to be planned. If a curriculum says critical thinking matters but assessments reward only [recall]({{ 'recall/' | relative_url }}), teachers and learners receive a mixed signal. If professional training praises analytical judgement but never defines what counts as sound evidence, novices are left guessing.

## Explicit instruction is not just “telling”

One common misunderstanding is that explicit instruction means lecturing about logic, bias or fallacies. Direct explanation can help, but it is not enough. Thinking improves when learners use the named skill repeatedly and receive information about how well they used it.

The critical-thinking meta-analysis by Abrami and colleagues is useful here because it distinguishes different course approaches. In an **immersion** approach, students engage deeply with subject matter but critical thinking is not made an explicit objective. In an **infusion** approach, critical thinking is taught within the subject and made explicit. In a **mixed** approach, learners receive explicit critical-thinking instruction as a separate strand while also applying it inside subject learning. The review found that instructional design matters: making critical thinking an explicit objective, combining it with dialogue, and anchoring it in authentic tasks were all part of the evidence base reviewed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654314551063" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[Sage Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sage JournalsStrategies for Teaching Students to Think CriticallyCT is an explicit objective in the infusion course but not in the immers...</span></span></span>

That distinction matters for policy. A school or university can add more projects, debates and inquiry tasks without improving reasoning if students are not taught how to reason within those tasks. Conversely, a standalone “thinking skills” course can become sterile if learners never use the skills on real content. The practical answer is not “skills instead of knowledge” or “knowledge instead of skills”. It is explicit thinking instruction inside knowledge-rich work.

Rosenshine’s principles of instruction help explain why. His evidence-informed account of effective teaching emphasises presenting new material in small steps, modelling, guided practice, checking understanding and obtaining a high success rate before moving to independence. Those routines are not limited to basic skills; they are especially relevant when the target is invisible cognitive work, because learners cannot copy what they cannot see.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aft.org">[American Federation of Teachers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aft.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Federation of TeachersPrinciples of Instruction: Research-Based Strategies That...March 7, 2012 — by B Rosenshine · 2012 · Cite...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 7, 2012</span></span></span>

## Practice has to happen in real subjects

Thinking skills are partly general, but they do not float free of content. A learner can understand the idea of “consider alternative explanations” in the abstract and still fail to use it well when interpreting a medical claim, a spreadsheet, a political speech or an experimental result. Transfer is hard because knowledge is often tied to the context in which it was learned. The National Academies’ work on learning and transfer notes that transfer is more likely when learners understand underlying principles and apply them across more than one context.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/9853/chapter/6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalacademies.org">[National Academies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalacademies.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National AcademiesChapter: 3 Learning and TransferOne frequently used teaching technique is to get learners to elaborate on the examples...</span></span></span>

This is why explicit instruction should not be confined to generic study-skills lessons. It works best when thinking moves are attached to the intellectual demands of a real subject:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **In science:** “What does this dataset allow us to conclude, and what uncertainty remains?”
* **In history:** “Which source is closer to the event, and what motive might shape its account?”
* **In literature:** “Which interpretation explains more of the text with fewer strained assumptions?”
* **In mathematics:** “Which representation makes the structure of the problem clearer?”
* **In civic education:** “Which claim is factual, which is evaluative, and which evidence would be needed?”

</div>

A concrete example comes from physics education research. Holmes, Wieman and Bonn redesigned introductory laboratory work so students repeatedly made quantitative comparisons between datasets and between data and models, with feedback and fading prompts. After the explicit prompts were removed, students in the experimental condition were far more likely than control students to improve their experimental methods and to identify limitations of a model using data; the gains also persisted into a later course.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.04870" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Teaching Critical Thinking</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv Teaching Critical Thinking</span></span></span>

The lesson is not that every subject should copy a physics lab. It is that explicit thinking instruction becomes powerful when it is attached to a recurring disciplinary decision: what to trust, what to compare, what counts as evidence, and what action follows.

## Feedback turns thinking into a learnable performance

Feedback is the difference between doing a difficult task and learning from it. In thinking instruction, the most useful feedback is not simply “right” or “wrong”. It tells the learner something about the quality of the reasoning route: whether the claim was clear, whether the evidence matched the claim, whether an alternative explanation was considered, whether the conclusion was too strong, or whether uncertainty was handled honestly.

Argument mapping is a good example because it makes reasoning visible. In an argument map, [claims]({{ 'claims/' | relative_url }}), reasons, objections and rebuttals are displayed in a structured visual form, so the learner and teacher can inspect the shape of the argument rather than discussing impressions. Research and reviews on argument mapping have reported promising gains in critical thinking, especially where learners receive repeated practice and feedback rather than a one-off diagramming exercise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reasoninglab.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/10/TvG-Using-argument-mapping-to-improve-critical-thinking-skills-2015.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reasoninglab.com">[Reasoninglab]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reasoninglab.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Using Argument Mapping to Improve Critical Thinking Skills</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Using Argument Mapping to Improve Critical Thinking Skills</span></span></span>

The broader principle applies beyond argument maps. Learners need to see the gap between an acceptable answer and a well-reasoned answer. A student might correctly identify that a source is “biased” but receive feedback that the word is too vague: what is the source’s interest, how might it affect the claim, and does other evidence confirm or weaken it? A trainee manager might identify a plausible cause of falling performance but receive feedback that they have not ruled out a competing explanation. A medical student might reach the right diagnosis but be challenged to explain which finding would have made that diagnosis less likely.

Good feedback also protects against a common failure of explicit teaching: learners can perform the routine when prompted but fail to use it independently. To reduce that risk, prompts should be faded. Early tasks might ask, “List two alternative explanations.” Later tasks might simply ask, “What is your conclusion and how confident are you?” Eventually, the learner should supply the checking questions without being cued.

## The transfer problem is the hardest part

The main objection to teaching thinking skills explicitly is not that it never works. It is that transfer is fragile. Learners may improve on the task they practised but fail to apply the same skill elsewhere. This is why explicit teaching must include transfer as a design goal, not as a hopeful afterthought.

Transfer improves when learners are helped to recognise the same reasoning structure across different surfaces. A history source evaluation, a product review, a scientific claim and a workplace proposal all differ in content, but each can involve asking: Who is making the claim? What evidence is offered? What alternative explanation exists? What would be costly if this judgement is wrong? The learner needs repeated chances to notice that family resemblance.

This is also where metacognition matters. A learner who has been taught only a procedure may wait for an external cue. A learner who has been taught to monitor their own thinking can ask, “What kind of problem is this? Which strategy fits? How will I know if my answer is good enough?” The EEF guidance frames metacognitive knowledge around understanding oneself as a learner, the task, and available strategies; that is exactly the bridge from taught routine to independent judgement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/metacognition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk">[EEF]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">EEFMetacognition and Self-Regulated Learning13 Nov 2025 — This guidance report reviews the... learning through explicitly teaching, mode...</span></span></span>

For policy and institutional design, the implication is clear: one-off critical-thinking weeks, posters of reasoning verbs, or generic “higher-order thinking” objectives are unlikely to be enough. Transfer requires a planned sequence across subjects and years, with common language but subject-specific application.

## What a serious institution would build

An institution that wants to teach thinking explicitly needs more than enthusiastic teachers. It needs a coherent design that aligns curriculum, assessment, teacher development and feedback.

The first requirement is a **shared vocabulary of thinking moves**. Learners should repeatedly meet terms such as claim, evidence, assumption, inference, counterexample, uncertainty, comparison, causal explanation and source reliability. The vocabulary should be small enough to use often, not a decorative list of dozens of abstract skills.

The second requirement is **subject ownership**. Thinking should not be outsourced entirely to a generic skills course. Departments and training teams need to decide what good reasoning looks like in their domain. Evidence in a chemistry experiment is not the same as evidence in a literary interpretation or an HR investigation, even though all three require disciplined judgement.

The third requirement is **assessment that rewards reasoning**. If exams, coursework or workplace evaluations reward only final answers, learners will treat reasoning as optional. Rubrics should include features such as clarity of claim, relevance of evidence, treatment of alternatives and calibration of confidence. This does not mean every task becomes long or subjective; even short answers can require learners to explain why a conclusion follows.

The fourth requirement is **teacher and trainer development**. Explicit instruction is skilled work. Teachers need to model reasoning aloud, diagnose weak reasoning, choose examples that reveal the target skill, and decide when to remove scaffolds. Recent work on explicit instruction has also warned that implementation quality matters: simply labelling an approach “explicit” does not guarantee effective teaching.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10480" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Getting Explicit Instruction Right</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv Getting Explicit Instruction Right</span></span></span>

The fifth requirement is **cumulative practice**. Thinking skills should reappear in increasingly demanding settings. A learner may begin by distinguishing claim from evidence in a short paragraph, later evaluate competing explanations in a subject unit, and later still make an evidence-based decision in an open-ended project.

## Where explicit teaching can go wrong

Explicit thinking instruction can fail in predictable ways. The most common failure is **performative terminology**: learners memorise words such as “bias”, “validity” or “assumption” without improving the quality of their judgement. Naming is necessary, but it must be tied to use.

A second failure is **over-scaffolding**. If every task comes with a checklist, learners may complete the checklist rather than think. Scaffolds should make the reasoning visible at first and then gradually disappear.

A third failure is **content-light skills training**. Generic puzzles and decontextualised exercises can be useful for introducing a move, but they do not substitute for reasoning with real knowledge. Critical thinking about vaccination evidence, climate data, historical testimony or financial risk requires enough content knowledge to know what matters.

A fourth failure is **assessment mismatch**. Institutions may publicly praise analytical thinking while privately rewarding speed, conformity or recall. Learners are quick to notice which behaviours actually count.

A fifth failure is **treating explicit instruction as anti-inquiry**. Explicit teaching does not mean learners never investigate, discuss or discover. It means they are equipped to do those things better. Inquiry without reasoning tools can become guessing; explicit instruction without inquiry can become inert routine. The strongest designs combine clear modelling with authentic problems.

## The policy choice

Teaching thinking explicitly is ultimately a governance decision about what education and training are for. If analytical skill is left implicit, institutions tend to reproduce unequal advantages: learners who already know how to argue, question, interpret evidence or seek feedback gain more from open-ended tasks, while others are told to “think harder” without being shown how.

A serious policy approach treats thinking as teachable infrastructure. It defines the skills, embeds them in subjects, trains educators to model and assess them, gives learners repeated practice, and checks whether the skills transfer beyond the original lesson. The evidence does not support the fantasy that one course, one technique or one slogan will produce broadly better thinkers. It does support a more practical claim: thinking improves when the hidden moves of good judgement are made explicit, practised in meaningful contexts, corrected through feedback and revisited until learners can use them without being prompted.
