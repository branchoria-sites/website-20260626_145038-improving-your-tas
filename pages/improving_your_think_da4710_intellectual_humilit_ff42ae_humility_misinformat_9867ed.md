## Introduction

Does intellectual humility make you harder to fool? The balance of recent evidence suggests that it does—but not because intellectually humble people automatically distrust information. Instead, they appear to be better at distinguishing accurate from inaccurate [claims]({{ 'claims/' | relative_url }}), more aware of when they might be mistaken, and more willing to investigate uncertain information before accepting or sharing it. This is an important distinction for anyone trying to improve their thinking. The goal is not blanket scepticism, where every source is treated as equally unreliable, but better calibration: matching confidence to the quality of the evidence.

This research fits naturally within the broader idea of intellectual humility without [cynicism]({{ 'cynicism/' | relative_url }}). False information spreads most easily when people are either excessively confident in their first impressions or so distrustful that they reject reliable evidence alongside unreliable claims. Studies increasingly suggest that intellectual humility helps avoid both errors by improving judgement rather than encouraging disbelief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://advances.in/psychology/10.56296/aip00026/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: advances.in">[Advances.in+2Advances.in]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">advances.in</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intellectual humility and misinformation receptivity</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A new meta-analytic review demonstrates who is most resilient...</span></span></span>

## What misinformation discernment studies actually measure

Much discussion of misinformation assumes that the important question is whether someone "believes fake news". Modern research uses a more precise approach.

Participants are typically shown a mixture of genuine and fabricated news headlines without being told which are which. Researchers then measure several separate abilities rather than treating every incorrect judgement as the same mistake.

Common measures include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* **Truth discernment:** how accurately someone separates true headlines from false ones.
* **Response bias:** whether someone has a general tendency to believe everything or dismiss everything regardless of evidence.
* **Metacognitive accuracy:** whether confidence rises when a judgement is correct and falls when it is mistaken.

</div>

This distinction matters because two people may achieve similar overall accuracy for very different reasons. One might carefully evaluate evidence, while another simply rejects nearly everything. The second strategy may occasionally avoid believing false stories, but it also causes unnecessary rejection of true information.

Recent experimental work involving news-headline judgement tasks found that intellectual humility was associated with stronger truth discernment and better metacognitive insight, but not with a general tendency to label more headlines as false. In other words, humble participants were not merely more sceptical—they were better at telling the difference.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://advances.in/psychology/10.56296/aip00025/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: advances.in">[Advances.in]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">advances.in</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">discernment of true and false... intellectual humility and pseudoscience beliefs, conspiratorial ideation, and susceptibility to fake ne...</span></span></span>

## Why humility is not the same as gullibility

One common misconception is that admitting you could be wrong makes you easier to manipulate. The evidence points in almost the opposite direction.

Intellectual humility does not ask people to lower their standards of evidence. Instead, it encourages recognising the possibility of error while continuing to evaluate claims critically. Someone high in intellectual humility may say:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* "This looks convincing, but I want another source."
* "I might be wrong, so I'll check."
* "The evidence changed, so I'll revise my view."

</div>

These responses differ sharply from gullibility, which accepts claims too readily, and from cynicism, which dismisses claims without evaluation.

A 2024 meta-analysis combining findings across multiple studies found that intellectual humility was associated with lower belief in misinformation overall, including fake news and conspiracy beliefs. The relationships were generally modest rather than dramatic, which is typical in psychological research, but they were consistent enough to suggest that humility provides a measurable protective advantage. The strongest behavioural association was with a greater willingness to investigate uncertain claims before deciding whether they were true.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://advances.in/psychology/10.56296/aip00026/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: advances.in">[Advances.in]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">advances.in</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intellectual humility and misinformation receptivity</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A new meta-analytic review demonstrates who is most resilient...</span></span></span>

Importantly, the same review found little evidence that humility simply makes people reject more information. Instead, it appears to encourage evidence-seeking behaviours that improve judgement quality.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://advances.in/psychology/10.56296/aip00026/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: advances.in">[Advances.in]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">advances.in</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intellectual humility and misinformation receptivity</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A new meta-analytic review demonstrates who is most resilient...</span></span></span>

## How metacognitive insight improves source checks

Perhaps the most interesting finding is that intellectual humility appears closely connected with **[metacognition]({{ 'metacognition/' | relative_url }})**—thinking about the quality of one's own thinking.

Researchers distinguish between making a correct judgement and knowing how likely that judgement is to be correct.

Imagine reading two unfamiliar news stories.

In one case, the evidence seems clear, several independent outlets agree, and the source has a strong reputation. High confidence may be justified.

In the second case, the claim relies on anonymous social media posts, emotional language and no supporting documentation. Even if the conclusion eventually turns out to be right, low confidence is the better intellectual position until stronger evidence appears.

Studies suggest intellectually humble individuals are better at matching confidence to actual accuracy. They are more likely to recognise when they are guessing and less likely to express unwarranted certainty. This metacognitive calibration reduces the risk that incorrect beliefs become entrenched simply because they were held confidently.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://advances.in/psychology/10.56296/aip00025/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: advances.in">[Advances.in]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">advances.in</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">discernment of true and false... intellectual humility and pseudoscience beliefs, conspiratorial ideation, and susceptibility to fake ne...</span></span></span>

In practical terms, this often translates into behaviours such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* checking whether multiple independent sources agree;
* distinguishing original reporting from repeated commentary;
* noticing when confidence exceeds available evidence;
* delaying judgement until additional information becomes available.

</div>

These habits improve information quality without requiring universal distrust.

## Important limits and unresolved questions

The current evidence is encouraging but should not be overstated.

Most observed relationships are statistically reliable but moderate in size. Intellectual humility is one influence among many. General reasoning ability, scientific literacy, media literacy, political [identity]({{ 'identity/' | relative_url }}), emotional investment, prior beliefs and social trust also affect susceptibility to misinformation.

Researchers have also found that different questionnaires capture somewhat different aspects of intellectual humility. Some emphasise recognising one's fallibility, others focus more on openness to revising beliefs or respect for opposing viewpoints. These differences partly [explain]({{ 'explain/' | relative_url }}) why studies sometimes produce varying effect sizes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9244574/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCPredictors and consequences of intellectual humility</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by T Porter · 2022 · Cited by 359 — Intellectual humility involves acknowledging the limitations of one&#x27;s knowledge and that one&#x27;s bel...</span></span></span>

Another important qualification comes from research examining belief revision. Intellectual humility often helps people update beliefs when presented with credible corrective evidence, but the effects are not universal. In some highly polarised situations, especially where participants already strongly endorse false beliefs, humility alone may not guarantee successful correction. ## What this means for everyday thinking

The research suggests that becoming harder to fool is less about becoming more suspicious and more about becoming better calibrated.

Useful habits supported by the evidence include:

* Separate confidence from certainty. Strong evidence justifies confidence; [weak evidence]({{ 'weak-evidence/' | relative_url }}) justifies caution.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Ask what evidence would change your mind before deciding that nothing could.
* Treat fact-checking as a normal part of reasoning rather than an admission of weakness.
* Pay attention not only to whether a claim is true but also to how confident you should be that it is true.
* Resist both extremes: accepting claims because they feel convincing and rejecting claims simply because they come from institutions, experts or unfamiliar sources.

</div>

Intellectual humility therefore works best as a practical form of epistemic self-awareness. Rather than encouraging people to distrust everything, it improves their ability to notice the difference between well-supported knowledge, uncertain claims and confident assertions that lack sufficient evidence.
