## Introduction

A polished website, a confident author biography and an impressive logo can all create an illusion of credibility. Professional fact-checkers know this, which is why they often do something that surprises most readers: they leave the page almost immediately. Instead of judging a source by its appearance or self-description, they search for independent information about who is behind it, what others say about it, and whether its [claims]({{ 'claims/' | relative_url }}) stand up elsewhere. This approach is known as **lateral reading**, and research consistently shows that it is one of the most effective ways to evaluate unfamiliar online sources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stacks.stanford.edu/file/druid%3Ayk133ht8603/Wineburg%20McGrew_Lateral%20Reading%20and%20the%20Nature%20of%20Expertise.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stacks.stanford.edu">[Stanford Digital Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stacks.stanford.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wineburg Mc Grew Lateral Reading and the Nature of Expertise</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">political fact-checking organizations. Lateral reading is especially valuable when you encounter a website, organisation or social media account you have never seen before. Rather than asking, "Does this page look trustworthy?", you ask, "What does the wider web reveal about this source?" That shift changes the task from evaluating presentation to evaluating reputation and evidence.

## What lateral reading means in practice

Traditional reading moves vertically through a page. You inspect the design, read the "About Us" section, look for contact information and examine the writing style. Those details can sometimes help, but they are also among the easiest features to imitate.

The goal is not to distrust every new source. It is to gather enough outside context to understand what the source is before deciding how much confidence it deserves.

## How outside checks beat surface credibility cues

Many online credibility cues are weak because they can be manufactured cheaply. A sophisticated layout, an official-sounding domain name or a lengthy mission statement says very little about whether claims are accurate.

Independent checks are stronger because they rely on evidence outside the source's own control.

### Check what others say about the source

Search for the organisation's name alongside terms such as:

* review
* criticism
* fact check
* funding
* ownership
* controversy

Independent reporting may reveal whether the organisation has recognised expertise, a history of publishing [corrections]({{ 'corrections/' | relative_url }}), undisclosed advocacy, or repeated factual errors. Equally important, you may discover that the source is widely respected within its field.

### Look for expertise, not impressive titles

A source may describe itself as an institute, academy or research centre. Lateral reading asks different questions:

* Is it recognised by other experts?
* Has its work been cited independently?
* Does it have relevant expertise for this specific topic?

Someone may be highly qualified in one discipline but commenting outside their area of competence. Likewise, an advocacy group may accurately present some evidence while selectively omitting contrary findings.

### Find better coverage

Sometimes the original page is not worth evaluating in depth because stronger reporting already exists.

If an unfamiliar website claims that a major scientific breakthrough has occurred, search directly for the underlying event rather than continuing to analyse the original article. If no reputable scientific institutions or established news organisations are reporting the same development, that absence itself becomes useful evidence.

This principle is central to Mike Caulfield's SIFT framework, which encourages readers to "find better coverage" instead of becoming trapped evaluating a questionable source in isolation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hapgood.us/2019/06/19/sift-the-four-moves/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hapgood.us">[Hapgood]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hapgood.us</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SIFT (The Four Moves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SIFT (The Four Moves) - Hapgood19 Jun 2019 — key approach is the Stop, Investigate, Find and Trace (SIFT) technique, a fact-checki...</span></span></span>

### Trace claims back to their origin

Many online articles simply repeat earlier material.

When possible, locate:

* the original research paper
* the official report
* the court judgment
* the government dataset
* the complete speech or interview

Doing so often reveals missing qualifications, uncertainty or context that disappeared as the claim spread across multiple websites.

## A practical example

Imagine you encounter an unfamiliar website claiming that a city has banned a common household product because of new scientific evidence.

A vertical reader might inspect the page carefully, notice references, see photographs and conclude that it appears professional.

A lateral reader would instead:

* search for the organisation behind the article
* look for reporting from established local news organisations
* check whether the city council or regulator has published an announcement
* search for the scientific study being cited
* compare how multiple independent sources describe the decision

Within a few minutes, it often becomes clear whether the claim is well supported, exaggerated or entirely fabricated.

## Why reputation alone is not enough

Lateral reading is not simply replacing one authority with another.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.scienceofboosting.org/project/lateral-reading/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scienceofboosting.org">[scienceofboosting.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scienceofboosting.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lateral Reading</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BoostingMay 4, 2023 — Lateral reading is a simple heuristic for online fact-checking: Open multiple tabs in your browser and search the W...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 4, 2023</span></span></span>

Finding that a source has a good reputation does not automatically validate every claim it makes. Likewise, discovering that an organisation has political views or commercial interests does not automatically invalidate everything it publishes.

Instead, reputation provides context for interpreting evidence.

The key questions remain:

* Does this source have relevant expertise?
* Can its claims be independently verified?
* Are primary sources available?
* Do multiple independent sources reach similar conclusions?

The method encourages proportional confidence rather than absolute trust.

## Common mistakes when judging unfamiliar websites

Several habits repeatedly lead readers astray.

**Reading too deeply before knowing the source.** Spending ten minutes analysing an article from an unknown website before spending thirty seconds checking who produced it often wastes time.

**Trusting appearance over reputation.** Modern website templates allow almost anyone to create pages that resemble established institutions.

**Reading only the "About" page.** Organisations naturally present themselves positively. Independent descriptions provide a more balanced perspective.

**Assuming search ranking equals credibility.** Search engines optimise for many factors besides reliability. High placement is not evidence that claims are accurate.

**Stopping after finding one confirming source.** Reliable verification usually requires genuinely independent confirmation rather than multiple sites repeating the same original report.

**Confusing criticism with disproof.** A source may have critics yet still produce accurate work. The question is whether criticism concerns evidence, methodology or simply disagreement.

## Building the habit

Lateral reading becomes faster with [practice]({{ 'practice/' | relative_url }}) because most investigations take only a few targeted searches.

A useful routine is:

* Pause before reacting emotionally.
* Search the source before reading it deeply.
* Look for independent descriptions.
* Find stronger reporting on the same claim.
* Trace important evidence back to its original source.
