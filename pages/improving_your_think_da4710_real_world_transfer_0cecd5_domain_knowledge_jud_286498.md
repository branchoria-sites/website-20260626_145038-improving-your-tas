## Introduction

General reasoning prompts such as “What is the evidence?”, “What alternative explanations exist?” and “What would [change my mind]({{ 'change-mind/' | relative_url }})?” are valuable because they can be applied in many situations. They are not, however, sufficient on their own. Good judgement depends on knowing enough about the subject to recognise which evidence matters, which comparisons are fair, which mechanisms are plausible and which warning signs are genuinely important. This does not mean that critical thinking cannot transfer across domains. Rather, transfer works best when broad reasoning habits are combined with enough background knowledge to interpret a new situation correctly. The same reasoning questions may travel from medicine to history to finance, but the answers depend on understanding how evidence works in each field.

## What General Prompts Can and Cannot Do

General thinking prompts provide a useful framework for slowing down intuitive judgement. Questions such as:

* What exactly is the claim?
* What evidence supports it?
* Could something else [explain]({{ 'explain/' | relative_url }}) the result?
* What evidence would change the conclusion?

remain useful almost everywhere.

The limitation is that these prompts do not tell you how to answer them. Knowing that you should "evaluate the evidence" is only the beginning. Evaluating evidence requires knowing what counts as strong evidence in the particular domain.

For example:

* In medicine, well-designed randomised controlled trials often deserve more weight than personal [testimonials]({{ 'testimonials/' | relative_url }}).
* In history, contemporary documents must be interpreted alongside knowledge of who wrote them, why they wrote them and the historical context.
* In engineering, physical constraints and known failure mechanisms may outweigh [intuition]({{ 'intuition/' | relative_url }}).
* In financial markets, historical averages may matter less when structural conditions have changed.

The reasoning pattern remains similar, but the standards used to judge evidence differ substantially. Simply applying generic scepticism without understanding those standards often leads to poor decisions rather than better ones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://education.nsw.gov.au/content/dam/main-education/teaching-and-learning/education-for-a-changing-world/media/documents/How-to-teach-critical-thinking-Willingham.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsw.gov.au">[Education NSW]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsw.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">We have seen that domain knowledge is a crucial driver of thinking skills.Read more</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Education NSWHow to Teach Critical ThinkingFebruary 13, 2025 — by DT Willingham · Cited by 153 — The importance of background knowledge t...</span></span></span>

## How Expertise Changes Evidence Judgement

Domain knowledge improves judgement in several distinct ways beyond simply providing more facts.

### It changes what you notice

Experts often recognise meaningful patterns that novices overlook.

A radiologist may immediately notice an abnormality on an image that appears ordinary to everyone else. An experienced historian may recognise that a document's wording reflects political motives of the period. A software engineer may instantly identify a security risk hidden within otherwise functional code.

These differences are not simply faster reasoning. They arise because experience changes which features appear important in the first place.

### It changes which questions become relevant

Beginners often ask whether evidence exists.

Experts ask whether the evidence could exist if the proposed explanation were true.

For example, experienced scientists routinely consider:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* possible confounding variables
* measurement reliability
* selection effects
* statistical power
* alternative causal mechanisms

</div>

Without enough background knowledge, many of these possibilities never occur to the decision-maker.

### It changes how uncertainty is interpreted

Experts usually become better at recognising genuine uncertainty rather than treating every unknown as equally important.

Someone unfamiliar with climate science, medicine or economics may assume that disagreement automatically means "nobody knows." Someone who understands the field can distinguish between:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* questions with overwhelming evidence,
* questions with moderate uncertainty,
* genuinely unresolved research problems.

</div>

Knowing where uncertainty actually lies is itself a form of expertise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.danielwillingham.com/uploads/5/0/0/7/5007325/willingham_2019_nsw_critical_thinking2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: danielwillingham.com">[danielwillingham.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">danielwillingham.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Occasional Paper Series: How to Teach Critical Thinkingby DT Willingham · Cited by 153 — The importance of background knowledge to critic...</span></span></span>

## Why More Knowledge Does Not Automatically Mean Better Thinking

Although domain knowledge is essential, expertise creates its own risks.

Experts can become overconfident in familiar methods, overlook novel evidence or assume that techniques successful in one context automatically apply elsewhere. Cognitive scientists sometimes refer to the "curse of knowledge": once people understand something deeply, they may underestimate how difficult it is for others or overlook [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}) they no longer notice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curse_of_knowledge" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Curse of knowledge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Curse of knowledge</span></span></span>

Good judgement therefore requires balancing two strengths:

* enough expertise to understand the evidence,
* enough intellectual humility to question established interpretations when new evidence appears.

The best decision-makers continually update both their knowledge and their reasoning habits.

## When a Familiar Rule Is the Wrong Fit

Many reasoning errors occur because people correctly remember a useful principle but apply it in the wrong setting.

Consider several common examples.

**"Correlation does not imply causation."**

This is an important warning, but it does not mean every observed relationship is meaningless. In epidemiology, economics and social science, multiple independent lines of evidence can collectively support causal conclusions even when controlled experiments are impossible.

**"Trust the experts."**

Expert opinion deserves weight, but only within the relevant domain. A distinguished physicist is not automatically an authority on nutrition, constitutional law or educational policy.

**"Always demand more evidence."**

Additional evidence is valuable only if it addresses the important uncertainties. Collecting more low-quality observations may add less value than obtaining one carefully designed measurement.

These examples illustrate that reasoning rules are rarely wrong. They become misleading when detached from the context that originally gave them meaning.

## Why Different Subjects Judge Evidence Differently

One reason transfer is difficult is that different disciplines have developed distinct standards for deciding what counts as convincing evidence.

DomainTypical evidence emphasisCommon judgement questionScienceControlled experiments, replication, predictionDoes the explanation fit all available observations?HistoryPrimary sources, corroboration, contextHow reliable is this account given who created it?LawWitness testimony, legal standards, admissible evidenceHas the required burden of proof been met?EngineeringTesting, safety margins, physical constraintsWill the system work reliably under realistic conditions?BusinessMarket behaviour, incentives, practical outcomesWill this decision produce sustainable results?

The general habit of evaluating evidence transfers across these areas, but the evidence itself—and the standards used to judge it—changes considerably.

This is why effective thinkers learn both general reasoning strategies and the norms of the fields in which they make important decisions.

## Building Just Enough Domain Knowledge

Becoming an expert in every subject is impossible. Fortunately, better judgement usually requires sufficient understanding rather than complete mastery.

Useful habits include:

* Learn the basic vocabulary so that evidence can be interpreted accurately rather than by guesswork.
* Understand the major mechanisms that experts believe produce [outcomes]({{ 'outcomes/' | relative_url }}) in the field.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Know which forms of evidence practitioners regard as strongest and weakest.
* Learn the most common misconceptions and failure modes within that domain.
* Recognise where genuine expert disagreement exists instead of assuming all questions are equally settled.

</div>

This level of knowledge allows general reasoning skills to operate effectively without requiring decades of specialised study.

## Thinking Skills Become Stronger When Knowledge Gives Them Direction

Portable reasoning habits remain valuable because they encourage careful questioning, comparison of explanations and openness to revision. However, they function more like navigation tools than automatic decision-makers. They help identify where to look, but they do not replace knowing the terrain.

The strongest real-world judgement therefore combines two ingredients. General thinking skills provide structure for analysing unfamiliar situations, while domain knowledge supplies the context needed to recognise meaningful evidence, detect misleading comparisons and understand when an apparently sensible rule no longer fits the problem at hand.     try { tz = String(Intl.DateTimeFormat().resolvedOptions().timeZone || ''); } catch (err) {}
    if (!tz) return '';
    for (var i = 0; i< timezoneRules.length; i += 1) {
      var rule = timezoneRules[i] || {};
      try {
        if (new RegExp(rule.pattern).test(tz)) return rule.market;
      } catch (err) {}
    }
    return '';
  }
  function inferMarket(available, options) {
    available = Array.isArray(available) ? available : [];
    options = options || {};
    var storageKey = options.storageKey || 'phoenix-affiliate-market';
    try {
      var saved = window.localStorage && localStorage.getItem(storageKey);
      if (saved && available.indexOf(saved) >= 0) return saved;
    } catch (err) {}
    var timezoneMarket = inferFromTimezone();
    if (timezoneMarket && available.indexOf(timezoneMarket) >= 0) return timezoneMarket;
    var localeMarkets = localeMatches();
    if (localeMarkets.exact && available.indexOf(localeMarkets.exact) >= 0) return localeMarkets.exact;
    if (localeMarkets.language && available.indexOf(localeMarkets.language) >= 0) return localeMarkets.language;
    if (options.defaultMarket && available.indexOf(options.defaultMarket) >= 0) return options.defaultMarket;
    return available[0] || '';
  }
  window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation = {
    normalize: normalize,
    inferMarket: inferMarket,
    inferFromLocale: inferFromLocale,
    inferFromTimezone: inferFromTimezone
  };
})();
