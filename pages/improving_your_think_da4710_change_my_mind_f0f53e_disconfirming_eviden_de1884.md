## Introduction

Asking “What would [change my mind]({{ 'change-mind/' | relative_url }})?” is only useful if you genuinely look for evidence that could reduce your confidence rather than simply collecting more reasons to believe you are already correct. Once a belief has enough supporting evidence to be plausible, additional confirming examples often add surprisingly little information. By contrast, a single piece of reliable contradictory evidence can reveal that an assumption is incomplete, a causal explanation is wrong, or the belief only holds under limited conditions. This does not mean treating every objection as decisive. It means deliberately searching for evidence that has the power to prove your current explanation wrong, because such evidence is usually more informative than another example that merely fits what you already expected.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confirmation_bias" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Confirmation bias</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Confirmation bias</span></span></span>

## Why contradictory evidence is more useful than more proof

One of the most common reasoning [mistakes]({{ 'mistakes/' | relative_url }}) is assuming that confidence should grow simply because more supporting evidence has been collected. In reality, evidence has unequal value.

Suppose you believe that a new training programme improves employee performance. Ten managers who already favour the programme may report positive experiences. Those reports increase confidence only slightly because they are exactly what your hypothesis predicted. However, discovering that comparable teams using the programme consistently perform worse under carefully measured conditions would force a much deeper rethink. The contradictory finding tests the belief in a way that repeated agreement cannot.

This principle explains why psychologists distinguish between **confirmation** and **diagnostic [testing]({{ 'testing/' | relative_url }})**. Confirming evidence often tells you what you already expected to observe. Disconfirming evidence asks a different question: *what would I see if my explanation were mistaken?* Evidence that can answer that question carries much greater informational value.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confirmation_bias" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2lakens.github.io]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Confirmation bias</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Confirmation bias</span></span></span>

## Types of disconfirming evidence

Not every disagreement counts as meaningful contradictory evidence. The most useful forms directly challenge the [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}) on which the belief depends.

Common examples include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Failed predictions.** A belief predicts that a specific outcome should occur, but it repeatedly does not.
* **Reliable counterexamples.** Even one well-established case can show that a supposedly universal claim is false.
* **Competing explanations.** Another hypothesis explains the same observations equally well or better.
* **Boundary cases.** The belief works only under narrow conditions rather than generally.
* **Independent replication failures.** Other investigators cannot reproduce the expected results despite following comparable methods.

</div>

The key question is whether the evidence would genuinely lower your confidence if it turned out to be true. If the answer is no, then the evidence is not functioning as a real test.

## Finding rival explanations instead of defending one explanation

People naturally [explain]({{ 'explain/' | relative_url }}) new information in ways that preserve existing beliefs. This tendency, often called confirmation bias or myside bias, affects what information we search for, how we interpret ambiguous evidence, and even what we remember later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confirmation_bias" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Confirmation bias</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Confirmation bias</span></span></span>

A stronger approach is to generate competing explanations before collecting more evidence.

Instead of asking:

> "What evidence shows my conclusion is correct?"

ask questions such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* What else could explain these observations?
* What prediction differs between the two explanations?
* What observation would favour the alternative instead?
* If I had to argue against my own position, what would be my strongest point?

</div>

For example, if customer satisfaction rises after launching a new website, the obvious explanation is that the redesign worked. A [rival explanation]({{ 'rival-test/' | relative_url }}) might be seasonal demand, a pricing change, or improved customer support. The next step is not to defend either explanation but to identify evidence capable of distinguishing between them.

This shifts reasoning away from advocacy and towards discrimination between competing hypotheses.

## Lessons from the classic Wason experiments

Psychologist Peter Wason demonstrated this problem in his famous rule-discovery experiments.

Participants were shown the sequence **2, 4, 6** and asked to discover the underlying rule by proposing additional number triples. Most quickly guessed a specific rule—for example, "numbers increasing by two"—and then tested more examples that fit that idea, such as **8, 10, 12**. Those examples almost always received confirmation, reinforcing the incorrect hypothesis.

Far fewer participants proposed triples designed to prove themselves wrong, such as **3, 5, 4** or **8, 6, 4**. Those disconfirming tests would have revealed much sooner that the actual rule was simply **any ascending sequence**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Cathcart_Wason" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Peter Cathcart Wason</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Peter Cathcart Wason</span></span></span>

The lesson extends well beyond laboratory puzzles. People often design "tests" that can only produce supporting evidence. A better test deliberately creates the possibility of discovering that the current explanation is mistaken.

## Updating confidence without overreacting

Seeking contradictory evidence does not mean abandoning a belief whenever criticism appears.

Instead, think of confidence as something that moves gradually.

Useful questions include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* Is the contradictory evidence reliable?
* Does it directly challenge a core assumption or only a minor detail?
* Could measurement error explain the apparent contradiction?
* Does the evidence repeat across independent observations?
* Does the alternative explanation now account for more of the facts?

</div>

This approach avoids two opposite mistakes:

* **Ignoring contradictory evidence**, which leads to unwarranted certainty.
* **Overreacting to isolated anomalies**, which creates instability and constant opinion changes.

Good judgement means allowing confidence to increase or decrease in proportion to the quality of the evidence, not simply the quantity.

## Practical ways to search for disconfirming evidence

The habit becomes easier if it is built into decision-making before a conclusion feels psychologically committed.

Useful practices include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* Write down one observation that would genuinely change your mind before gathering additional evidence.
* Assign someone to argue for the strongest competing explanation rather than simply criticising details.
* Search specifically for studies, examples or datasets that contradict your preferred conclusion.
* Compare predictions from multiple explanations before deciding which evidence to collect.
* Periodically ask whether new evidence would still count if it supported the opposite conclusion.

</div>

These habits make it less likely that evidence collection becomes an exercise in self-validation.

## A useful shift in mindset

Collecting more supporting evidence often increases confidence without substantially increasing understanding. Looking for evidence that could prove a belief wrong does the opposite: it exposes hidden assumptions, distinguishes between competing explanations, and reveals where confidence is genuinely deserved.

Within the broader [practice]({{ 'practice/' | relative_url }}) of asking "What would change my mind?", this is the central mechanism. The goal is not to become sceptical of everything, but to make beliefs answerable to evidence capable of changing them. That is what turns a belief from something merely defended into something genuinely tested.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confirmation_bias" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Confirmation bias</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Confirmation bias</span></span></span>
