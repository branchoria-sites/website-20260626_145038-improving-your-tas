## Introduction

Prediction logs turn reasoning into something that can be measured instead of merely remembered. Rather than asking whether a decision “felt sensible”, they require you to state a forecast before the outcome is known, [explain]({{ 'explain/' | relative_url }}) why you believe it, record your confidence as a probability, and later compare that judgement with reality. This creates a permanent record of how well your thinking matches the world rather than your memory of it.

Within [deliberate practice]({{ 'deliberate-practice/' | relative_url }}) for reasoning, prediction logs are valuable because they generate repeated, scoreable judgements. They expose patterns that are otherwise difficult to notice, such as chronic [overconfidence]({{ 'overconfidence/' | relative_url }}), excessive caution, reluctance to update beliefs, or relying on weak evidence. Research from forecasting tournaments, including the Good Judgment Project, has shown that keeping score and repeatedly refining probabilistic judgements can improve forecasting performance over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://goodjudgment.com/about/the-science-of-superforecasting/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: goodjudgment.com">[Good Judgment+2AI Impacts]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">goodjudgment.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Good JudgmentThe Science Of SuperforecastingProven methods. ## What to record before the outcome is known

The usefulness of a prediction log depends far more on what is written before an outcome is known than on commentary afterwards. Once people know the result, hindsight bias makes previous beliefs seem more obvious than they really were.

A practical prediction entry usually includes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **The prediction itself.** Phrase it so that it will clearly be true or false. Ambiguous predictions cannot be scored consistently.
* **A probability estimate.** Instead of "probably", assign a number such as 30%, 65% or 90%. Probability estimates reveal calibration—whether events assigned a 70% chance really happen about seven times in ten.
* **A deadline.** Specify exactly when the prediction will be judged.
* **The evidence available now.** Separate observations from interpretations. Record the facts you relied upon rather than reconstructing them later.
* **Alternative explanations.** Briefly note what would make the opposite outcome plausible. This discourages tunnel vision.
* **Conditions for revision.** State what new evidence would cause you to increase or decrease your confidence before the deadline.

</div>

For example, instead of writing, "I think sales will improve", a prediction log might record:

> "There is a 65% probability that quarterly sales will exceed last year's equivalent quarter. Confidence is based on current order volume and announced product launches. If two major customers cancel before month-end, confidence falls below 50%."

The important feature is that every part of the reasoning is timestamped before reality provides the answer.

## How scoring reveals recurring judgement errors

Prediction logs become useful only when forecasts are scored systematically rather than remembered selectively.

Forecasting research commonly uses the **Brier score**, a proper scoring rule that compares predicted [probabilities]({{ 'probabilities/' | relative_url }}) with actual outcomes. Lower scores indicate better probabilistic forecasting because they reward both accuracy and well-calibrated confidence. Proper scoring rules encourage honest probability estimates rather than exaggerated certainty designed to appear impressive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brier_score" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2scattered-thoughts.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Brier score</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Brier score</span></span></span>

Over dozens or hundreds of predictions, scores begin to reveal stable reasoning habits rather than isolated mistakes. Typical patterns include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--benefit" markdown="1">

* **Overconfidence.** Events predicted with 90% confidence occur far less frequently than expected.
* **Underconfidence.** Correct forecasts are consistently assigned probabilities that are too low.
* **Poor discrimination.** Almost every forecast receives similar probabilities, regardless of evidence quality.
* **Failure to distinguish evidence strength.** Weak and strong cases receive nearly identical confidence levels.

</div>

Calibration is especially informative. Suppose every forecast assigned an 80% probability is later examined. If only about half actually occur, confidence is inflated. If nearly all occur, confidence may be too conservative. Good calibration means long-run frequencies match stated probabilities, not that every individual prediction succeeds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12818272/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title"># 4: A Brier</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">On misconceptions about the Brier score in binary prediction...by L Hoessly · 2026 · Cited by 18 — Calibration refers to how well pre...</span></span></span>

It is also important not to overinterpret a single score. Researchers emphasise that the Brier score combines several aspects of forecast quality and is influenced by the difficulty of the prediction task. ## Using updates as practice rather than embarrassment

Many people resist recording prediction updates because changing one's mind can feel like admitting failure. Forecasting research suggests the opposite.

Good forecasters revise beliefs whenever meaningful new evidence appears. The goal is not consistency with previous opinions but increasing correspondence with reality. Forecasts are therefore living judgements rather than declarations of loyalty to an initial view.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://goodjudgment.com/about/the-science-of-superforecasting/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: goodjudgment.com">[Good Judgment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">goodjudgment.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Good JudgmentThe Science Of SuperforecastingProven methods. Powerful results. Good Judgment research discovered four keys to accurate for...</span></span></span>

A useful update records three things:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* what new evidence appeared;
* how much confidence changed;
* why the evidence justified that change.

</div>

For instance:

DateProbabilityReason2 June45%Initial evidence mixed10 June62%New economic data stronger than expected18 June54%Competitor announcement weakens original case

Reviewing these updates later often exposes recurring habits. Some people hardly update until the answer is obvious. Others swing dramatically after every headline. Both patterns become visible only when changes are logged over time.

The objective is not to minimise revisions but to make them evidence-based. A prediction log rewards thoughtful updating because every revision itself becomes observable [practice]({{ 'practice/' | relative_url }}) in reasoning under uncertainty.

## What prediction logs teach that memory cannot

Human memory edits past beliefs surprisingly well. After outcomes become known, people frequently remember having been "almost certain" of whatever happened, even when earlier confidence was much lower. This hindsight bias makes informal self-assessment unreliable.

Prediction logs bypass this problem because they preserve:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* original confidence levels;
* original reasoning;
* the evidence available at the time;
* every subsequent revision.

</div>

Instead of debating whether a decision was "reasonable", the learner can inspect exactly what information was available and whether confidence was proportional to that evidence.

Over time, this archive becomes a personalised dataset of thinking errors. Unlike general advice about cognitive biases, it identifies the biases that repeatedly affect one individual's judgements.

## Building a useful prediction log

The most effective logs favour many small predictions over a few dramatic ones. Frequent forecasts provide enough observations to detect patterns and shorten the [feedback]({{ 'feedback/' | relative_url }}) cycle.

Suitable practice questions include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* Will this project finish by its stated deadline?
* Will a published study replicate?
* Will a policy proposal pass within six months?
* Will an investment thesis outperform a benchmark over a specified period?
* Will a software feature be completed by the next release?

</div>

The questions matter less than their clarity. Good prediction questions have an observable outcome, a defined resolution date and a probability estimate recorded in advance.

Consistency also matters more than complexity. A simple spreadsheet or notebook that captures forecasts, evidence, updates and outcomes is usually sufficient. The cumulative record—not any single prediction—is what gradually improves judgement.

As forecasting researchers often note, the discipline of keeping score distinguishes genuine learning from the comforting illusion of improvement.
