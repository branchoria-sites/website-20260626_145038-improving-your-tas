## Introduction

Purposeful judgement in critical thinking is not only about evaluating other people's claims. It also requires monitoring your own reasoning while you are using it. This process is known as [self-regulation]({{ 'self-regulation/' | relative_url }}): deliberately checking whether your standards are consistent, whether you are giving evidence the weight it deserves, and whether your confidence still matches what you actually know. Rather than assuming bias only affects other people, self-regulation assumes that every thinker is vulnerable to predictable distortions such as confirmation bias, motivated reasoning and [overconfidence]({{ 'overconfidence/' | relative_url }}). The practical goal is not to become perfectly objective, but to catch these distortions early enough to improve the quality of a judgement before acting on it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9966223/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCActively Open-Minded Thinking and Its Measurement</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by KE Stanovich · 2023 · Cited by 133 — Actively open-minded thinking (AOT) is measured by items that tap the willingness to consider...</span></span></span>

Within critical thinking, self-regulation is the mechanism that turns good intentions into reliable decisions. It asks not, "Can I find flaws in someone else's argument?" but, "Am I applying the same standards to my own conclusion?"

## Bias checks inside the thinking process

Self-regulation is a continuous monitoring process rather than a final inspection. Instead of waiting until a decision has been made, effective thinkers repeatedly compare their reasoning against a few simple questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--benefit" markdown="1">

* Have I looked for evidence that could prove me wrong?
* Would I accept this evidence if it supported the opposite conclusion?
* Am I interpreting ambiguous information differently because of what I already believe?
* Is my confidence greater than the strength of the available evidence?

</div>

These questions interrupt automatic thinking before it hardens into certainty. Research on [metacognition]({{ 'metacognition/' | relative_url }})—the ability to think about one's own thinking—shows that people who monitor the quality of their reasoning are generally better at recognising uncertainty and adjusting their confidence appropriately.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12103939/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCMetacognitive sensitivity: The key to calibrating trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by D Lee · 2025 · Cited by 25 — Metacognitive sensitivity, measured by meta-d&#x27;, estimates how effectively confidence judgments disting...</span></span></span>

The aim is not constant self-doubt. Instead, it is calibration: making confidence proportional to evidence. A well-calibrated thinker can be highly confident when evidence is strong and openly uncertain when evidence is incomplete.

### Why confirmation bias survives intelligence

One of the most important findings in cognitive psychology is that intelligence alone does not eliminate bias. Confirmation bias encourages people to seek, remember and interpret information that supports an existing belief while discounting conflicting evidence. Because this often happens unconsciously, simply intending to be objective is usually insufficient.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11495861/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCStop Fooling Yourself!</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Diagnosing and Treating Confirmation...by RT Born · 2024 · Cited by 9 — Confirmation bias (CB) is a cognitive bias that allows us to fo...</span></span></span>

Self-regulation works by inserting deliberate pauses into reasoning:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* separate gathering evidence from interpreting it;
* actively search for disconfirming information;
* delay commitment to a preferred explanation until competing explanations have been considered.

</div>

Studies of judicial decision-making illustrate this principle clearly. Once judges or investigators form an early theory of a case, later evidence is more likely to be interpreted in ways that reinforce that theory. Deliberately separating evidence collection from conclusion formation reduces this risk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pure.eur.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/48276679/Repub_124724.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pure.eur.nl">[Erasmus University Rotterdam]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pure.eur.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Repub 124724</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Erasmus University RotterdamEUR Research Information PortalToday — by S van Brussel · 2020 · Cited by 42 — The confirmation bias appears...</span></span></span>

## Questions that expose uneven standards

Double standards are often easier to detect through comparison than through introspection. Instead of asking whether your reasoning is "objective", compare how you treat similar situations.

Useful self-checks include:

**Would I accept this evidence if it supported the opposite side?**

Many reasoning errors become visible when beliefs are reversed. If identical evidence suddenly seems weak only because it favours a different conclusion, your evaluation may be driven by preference rather than evidence.

**Am I demanding different levels of proof?**

People frequently require overwhelming evidence before changing a favoured belief while accepting relatively [weak evidence]({{ 'weak-evidence/' | relative_url }}) for beliefs they already hold. Self-regulation looks for symmetry in evidential standards.

**Have I considered realistic alternatives?**

Actively generating competing explanations weakens the tendency to stop searching once the first plausible answer appears. Research on [actively open-minded]({{ 'open-mindedness/' | relative_url }}) thinking consistently links willingness to consider alternatives with better judgement and more accurate confidence calibration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/judgment-and-decision-making/article/role-of-actively-openminded-thinking-in-information-acquisition-accuracy-and-calibration/1D78BE16863F3F6B2D1C8B5307C9C3B3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe role of actively open-minded thinking in information...by U Haran · 2013 · Cited by 427 — We...</span></span></span>

**Could someone explain my conclusion without mentioning the evidence?**

If the explanation depends mainly on identity, emotion, loyalty or preference, evidence may be playing a smaller role than assumed.

These questions are effective because they test the reasoning process itself rather than merely inspecting its outcome.

## Revising confidence without losing face

One reason people resist correcting themselves is psychological rather than intellectual. Changing a judgement can feel like admitting failure or inconsistency. Self-regulation reframes revision as evidence that the thinking process is functioning properly.

Critical thinkers distinguish between:

* changing an opinion because new evidence appeared;
* changing confidence because evidence became stronger or weaker;
* abandoning a conclusion because its supporting [assumptions]({{ 'assumptions/' | relative_url }}) proved false.

These are signs of disciplined reasoning, not weakness.

Research on overconfidence shows that people commonly overestimate the accuracy of their own judgements across domains ranging from medicine to business and everyday decision-making. Better performance often comes not from becoming less confident overall, but from matching confidence more closely to actual accuracy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/features/cou-0000105.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apa.org">[APA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apa.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This bias is thought to be robust...Read more...</span></span></span>

A useful habit is expressing conclusions in graduated language:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* "The evidence strongly supports..."
* "The balance of evidence currently suggests..."
* "There are several plausible explanations..."
* "I do not yet know."

</div>

This approach preserves intellectual honesty without requiring false certainty or unnecessary hesitation.

## Building habits that reduce bias over time

Self-regulation is more effective when it becomes routine rather than occasional. Research on metacognition and actively open-minded thinking suggests several habits that strengthen resistance to biased reasoning:

* **Seek contradiction deliberately.** Before finalising a judgement, spend time looking specifically for credible evidence that challenges it rather than only confirming it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11495861/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCStop Fooling Yourself!</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Diagnosing and Treating Confirmation...by RT Born · 2024 · Cited by 9 — Confirmation bias (CB) is a cognitive bias that allows us to fo...</span></span></span>
* **Separate observation from interpretation.** Record what is directly known before explaining what it might mean.
* **Estimate confidence numerically when appropriate.** Assigning approximate confidence levels encourages calibration instead of treating every conclusion as simply true or false.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12103939/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCMetacognitive sensitivity: The key to calibrating trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by D Lee · 2025 · Cited by 25 — Metacognitive sensitivity, measured by meta-d&#x27;, estimates how effectively confidence judgments disting...</span></span></span>
* **Review significant decisions afterwards.** Compare predictions with actual [outcomes]({{ 'outcomes/' | relative_url }}). Looking back for calibration errors often reveals recurring personal biases more effectively than relying on memory alone.
* **Treat disagreement as diagnostic.** When informed people reach different conclusions, ask which assumptions differ rather than assuming one side is obviously irrational.

These habits do not eliminate bias, but they make biases easier to detect before they influence important decisions.

## The real test of critical thinking

Spotting weaknesses in another person's reasoning is valuable, but it is only half of critical thinking. The more demanding skill is applying exactly the same standards to your own beliefs.

Self-regulation transforms critical thinking from an outward-facing activity into an inward discipline. It replaces the question "Where is someone else mistaken?" with the more productive question "What would convince me that I might be mistaken?"
