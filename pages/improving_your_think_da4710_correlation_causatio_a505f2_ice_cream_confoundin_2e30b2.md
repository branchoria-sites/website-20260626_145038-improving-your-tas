## Introduction

The relationship between ice cream sales and drowning deaths is one of the best-known examples of why correlation alone cannot establish [causation]({{ 'causation/' | relative_url }}). The two variables often rise and fall together, yet there is no credible mechanism by which buying or eating ice cream causes people to drown. Instead, both are influenced by a shared background factor: warm weather. Higher temperatures encourage people to buy more ice cream while also increasing swimming, boating and other water activities, which raise the opportunity for drowning incidents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://otexts.com/fpp3/causality.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: otexts.com">[OTexts: Online, open-access textbooks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">otexts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">They are both caused by a third variable (temperature).Read more</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Online, open-access textbooks7.8 Correlation, causation and forecastingSo the two variables (ice-cream sales and drownings) are c...</span></span></span>

This example is memorable because it demonstrates a common analytical trap. When two trends move together, the real explanation may be a hidden third factor rather than a direct cause-and-effect relationship. Learning to recognise this pattern is an essential part of improving critical thinking and evaluating everyday [claims]({{ 'claims/' | relative_url }}).

## When the obvious causal story is wrong

At first glance, the data appear persuasive. Plot monthly ice cream sales alongside monthly drowning incidents and both usually peak during the summer before falling again in colder months. A simple statistical analysis may reveal a strong positive correlation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://otexts.com/fpp3/causality.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: otexts.com">[OTexts: Online, open-access textbooks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">otexts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">They are both caused by a third variable (temperature).Read more</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Online, open-access textbooks7.8 Correlation, causation and forecastingSo the two variables (ice-cream sales and drownings) are c...</span></span></span>

The mistake is to leap from "these variables are associated" to "one causes the other". If ice cream really caused drownings, then increasing ice cream consumption while holding everything else constant should increase drowning risk. There is no plausible biological or behavioural mechanism supporting such a claim, and the association disappears once the underlying seasonal conditions are taken into account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.discovermagazine.com/statistics-when-confounding-variables-are-out-of-control-9159" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovermagazine.com">[Discover Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovermagazine.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover MagazineStatistics: When Confounding Variables Are Out of Control2 Apr 2016 — The ice cream/drownings correlation would cease to...</span></span></span>

The lesson extends far beyond this example. A striking statistical relationship may be genuine, but the explanation behind it may be completely different from the most obvious story.

## How common causes create misleading links

The key mechanism is **confounding**, where a third variable influences both the supposed cause and the observed outcome.

In this example:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Higher temperatures increase demand for cold foods such as ice cream.
* The same higher temperatures encourage more people to visit beaches, lakes and swimming pools.
* More people entering the water creates more opportunities for accidental drowning.

</div>

Temperature therefore acts as a **common cause** of both variables. Ice cream sales and drowning incidents become statistically linked even though neither directly produces the other.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://otexts.com/fpp3/causality.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: otexts.com">[OTexts: Online, open-access textbooks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">otexts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">They are both caused by a third variable (temperature).Read more</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Online, open-access textbooks7.8 Correlation, causation and forecastingSo the two variables (ice-cream sales and drownings) are c...</span></span></span>

This is sometimes called a **spurious relationship**: the observed association is real, but interpreting it as direct causation is incorrect because the common cause has not been accounted for.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spurious_relationship" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Spurious relationship</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Spurious relationship</span></span></span>

An important subtlety is that the correlation may still have predictive value. If unusually high ice cream sales reliably occur on very hot days, they might help forecast periods when drowning risk is also elevated. That does not mean ice cream is the mechanism causing drownings; it is acting as an indirect indicator of conditions associated with greater water exposure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://otexts.com/fpp3/causality.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: otexts.com">[OTexts: Online, open-access textbooks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">otexts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">They are both caused by a third variable (temperature).Read more</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Online, open-access textbooks7.8 Correlation, causation and forecastingSo the two variables (ice-cream sales and drownings) are c...</span></span></span>

## Why this example remains so useful

The ice cream example has survived for decades because it strips away emotional or political distractions and lets the reasoning stand on its own.

Readers immediately recognise that:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--example" markdown="1">

* the correlation exists;
* the proposed causal explanation sounds absurd;
* a better explanation naturally emerges once the hidden variable is considered.

</div>

This makes the example an effective [teaching]({{ 'teaching/' | relative_url }}) tool for introducing confounding, hidden variables and causal reasoning in statistics, epidemiology, economics and data science. It has also been used in educational activities that ask students to identify the unseen variable before revealing temperature as the common cause.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confounding" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Because the example is so intuitive, it encourages people to apply the same scepticism to less obvious situations where the hidden factor is much harder to detect.

## Questions that expose hidden third factors

Whenever two variables appear strongly related, a few simple questions help uncover possible confounders.

**Could both variables be responding to the same external condition?**

Season, weather, economic conditions, age, income, education or underlying health frequently influence multiple [outcomes]({{ 'outcomes/' | relative_url }}) at once.

**Does a believable causal mechanism exist?**

A statistical pattern is more convincing when supported by an explanation of how one variable could physically, biologically or socially influence the other.

**What happens if the suspected confounder is held constant?**

If the relationship weakens or disappears after accounting for temperature, season or another common cause, then the original association was probably driven largely by that factor rather than by a direct causal effect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.discovermagazine.com/statistics-when-confounding-variables-are-out-of-control-9159" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovermagazine.com">[Discover Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovermagazine.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover MagazineStatistics: When Confounding Variables Are Out of Control2 Apr 2016 — The ice cream/drownings correlation would cease to...</span></span></span>

**Would an intervention change the outcome?**

If people were randomly encouraged to buy more ice cream without changing weather or swimming behaviour, there is no reason to expect drowning rates to increase. Thinking in terms of interventions helps distinguish prediction from genuine causation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://otexts.com/fpp3/causality.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: otexts.com">[OTexts: Online, open-access textbooks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">otexts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">They are both caused by a third variable (temperature).Read more</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Online, open-access textbooks7.8 Correlation, causation and forecastingSo the two variables (ice-cream sales and drownings) are c...</span></span></span>

## The broader thinking habit

The ice cream and drowning example teaches a habit that applies far beyond statistics. Many everyday claims—about health, education, business performance or public policy—can appear convincing because two measures move together over time. Before accepting a causal explanation, ask whether both outcomes could simply be responding to the same underlying influence.

That small shift in thinking changes the question from "What caused this?" to "What else could [explain]({{ 'explain/' | relative_url }}) both observations?" In analytical reasoning, that search for hidden common causes is often the difference between a persuasive story and a sound explanation.
