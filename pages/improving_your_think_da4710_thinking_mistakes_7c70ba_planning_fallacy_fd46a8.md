## Introduction

One of the most reliable ways to reduce the planning fallacy is to stop treating a project as if it were unique and instead ask what happened to similar projects in the past. This approach, known as **reference-class forecasting**, replaces optimistic guesses with evidence from comparable cases. Rather than asking, "How long should this take if everything goes well?", it asks, "How long did similar work actually take?" Research by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that people systematically underestimate time, costs and risks because they focus on the details of the current plan instead of the statistical record of comparable projects. Later work by Bent Flyvbjerg translated this insight into a practical forecasting method used in public planning and large infrastructure projects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_class_forecasting" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2IDEAS/RePEc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Reference class forecasting</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reference class forecasting</span></span></span>

Within the broader goal of improving analytical thinking, reference-class forecasting is valuable because it provides a disciplined reality check. It does not replace detailed planning; it tests whether the plan is plausible when judged against experience rather than hope.

## Why detailed plans create false confidence

Detailed plans often feel convincing because they contain many concrete steps, milestones and dependencies. Ironically, this detail can make forecasts less reliable if it encourages people to imagine an ideal sequence of events while overlooking the delays that routinely arise in real work.

Common sources of error include:

* Unexpected technical problems.
* Coordination between multiple people or teams.
* Waiting for approvals or external dependencies.
* Rework after [mistakes]({{ 'mistakes/' | relative_url }}) or changing requirements.
* Interruptions from unrelated priorities.

Most planners recognise these risks in principle, yet still assume that this particular project will avoid them. Kahneman and Tversky argued that this happens because people rely on an **inside view**: they mentally simulate the current project instead of considering the distribution of [outcomes]({{ 'outcomes/' | relative_url }}) from previous, similar projects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_class_forecasting" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Reference class forecasting</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reference class forecasting</span></span></span>

This explains why experience alone does not eliminate the planning fallacy. Someone may have repeatedly missed deadlines yet still believe that the next project will be different because its individual features appear more favourable.

## Inside view versus outside view

The distinction between the inside and outside view is the central practical idea behind reference-class forecasting.

The **inside view** asks questions such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* What tasks must be completed?
* Which obstacles can we already identify?
* What is our current schedule?

</div>

These questions are necessary for execution but tend to encourage optimism because unknown risks receive little weight.

The **outside view** begins somewhere else. It asks:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* What projects genuinely resemble this one?
* How long did they actually take?
* How often did they exceed their budgets?
* Where did they typically encounter delays?

</div>

Instead of building an estimate from imagined future events, the outside view starts with observed historical outcomes and adjusts only when there is convincing evidence that the current case is genuinely unusual. This shifts attention from stories to statistics, making forecasts more resistant to wishful thinking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_class_forecasting" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Reference class forecasting</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reference class forecasting</span></span></span>

A classic illustration comes from Kahneman's account of a team writing a curriculum. Team members initially predicted completion within roughly two years. When one member was asked how long similar curriculum projects had taken, he recalled that most required seven to ten years and many were never completed. Despite recognising this, the group ignored the outside view and continued with the optimistic estimate. The project ultimately took around eight years. The episode has become a well-known demonstration of the planning fallacy in [practice]({{ 'practice/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://conversableeconomist.com/2023/04/17/reference-class-forecasting-an-origin-story-from-daniel-kahneman/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: conversableeconomist.com">[Conversable Economist]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">conversableeconomist.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">reference class forecasting an origin story from daniel kahneman</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Conversable EconomistReference Class Forecasting: An Origin Story from Daniel...Apr 17, 2023 — And one technique used by the superior fo...</span></span></span>

## Choosing a useful reference class

Reference-class forecasting is only as good as the comparison group. Choosing the wrong reference class can produce misleading forecasts.

A useful reference class should be:(#endnote-1 "Endnote 1")<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_class_forecasting" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Reference class forecasting</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reference class forecasting</span></span></span>

* **Similar enough** to share important characteristics with the current project.
* **Large enough** to reveal meaningful patterns rather than isolated [anecdotes]({{ 'anecdotes/' | relative_url }}).
* **Based on completed projects**, using actual rather than planned outcomes.
* **Relevant to the decision**, matching the type, scale and organisational context where possible.

For example, estimating the completion time for a software migration by comparing it with previous software migrations is generally more informative than comparing it with all technology projects. Conversely, choosing only projects that resemble the current one in every detail can make the sample so small that it loses statistical value.

Researchers sometimes describe this balancing act as the "reference class problem": the comparison group must be neither too broad nor too narrow.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_class_forecasting" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Reference class forecasting</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reference class forecasting</span></span></span>

## Applying reference-class forecasting in everyday decisions

The same reasoning works well beyond large engineering projects.

A student estimating the time needed for a dissertation should examine how long previous dissertations of similar scope actually took, rather than relying solely on a personal work schedule.

Someone renovating a house should compare expected costs with completed renovations of similar size, including delays caused by permits, supply shortages and unexpected repairs.

A business launching a new product should review historical launch schedules across comparable products before accepting ambitious internal deadlines.

A practical process is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Define the decision clearly.
2. Identify a genuinely comparable group of past cases.
3. Gather actual outcomes rather than original estimates.
4. Use the average or probability distribution as the starting forecast.
5. Adjust only when there is strong evidence that the current case differs in ways that matter.

</div>

The important shift is psychological as much as statistical. Instead of asking, "Why will we succeed?", the decision-maker asks, "What normally happens?"

## What organisations have learned

Reference-class forecasting has had its greatest impact in large public investments, where cost overruns and schedule delays can have substantial financial consequences.

Bent Flyvbjerg developed practical procedures that adapted Kahneman and Tversky's research for transport planning. The approach has influenced forecasting guidance for major infrastructure projects, including work commissioned by the UK government, and has been applied in countries including Denmark, the Netherlands and Hong Kong. Rather than relying solely on engineering estimates, planners compare proposed projects with large databases of completed projects and adjust budgets or schedules according to observed historical risk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_class_forecasting" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[arXiv+3Wikipedia+3IDEAS/RePEc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Reference class forecasting</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reference class forecasting</span></span></span>

Research consistently shows that projects judged exceptional are often less exceptional than their planners believe. Recent work has highlighted **uniqueness bias**—the tendency to treat one's own project as fundamentally different from previous ones—as another reason forecasts become overly optimistic. Reference-class forecasting works partly because it counters this instinct by forcing planners to search for similarities before emphasising differences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.07710" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Uniqueness Bias: Why It Matters, How to Curb It</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Uniqueness Bias: Why It Matters, How to Curb ItAugust 13, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 13, 2024</span></span></span>

## Limits and good practice

Reference-class forecasting is not a substitute for detailed planning. It cannot [predict]({{ 'predict/' | relative_url }}) unique events that have no historical precedent, nor can it identify exactly which specific problems will arise. It is best viewed as a calibration tool rather than a complete forecasting system.

It is also important to recognise that some inaccurate forecasts arise from organisational incentives rather than honest optimism. Projects may be presented in an unrealistically favourable light to secure approval or funding. In such cases, better forecasting methods alone cannot eliminate bias unless institutions also reward accurate forecasting instead of attractive forecasts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/eurpls/v16y2006i1p3-21.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ideas.repec.org">[IDEAS/RePEc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ideas.repec.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">v16y2006i1p3 21</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">IDEAS/RePEcCurbing Optimism Bias and Strategic Misrepresentation in...by B Flyvbjerg · 2006 · Cited by 698 — The American Planning Assoc...</span></span></span>

Used appropriately, however, reference-class forecasting offers one of the most practical forms of "[decision hygiene]({{ 'decision-hygiene/' | relative_url }})". By checking ambitious plans against the historical performance of comparable work, it reduces [overconfidence]({{ 'overconfidence/' | relative_url }}), produces more realistic schedules and budgets, and helps people distinguish between what they hope will happen and what experience suggests is most likely.
