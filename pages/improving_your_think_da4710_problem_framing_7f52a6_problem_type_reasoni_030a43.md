## Introduction

One of the fastest ways to improve your thinking is to stop assuming that every problem can be solved in the same way. A broken machine, a software architecture decision, a cultural change programme, and a national housing shortage may all be called “problems”, but they differ fundamentally in how causes work, how evidence accumulates, and what success looks like. When people apply the wrong reasoning style to the wrong problem type, they often become trapped in cycles of [overconfidence]({{ 'overconfidence/' | relative_url }}), endless analysis, or ineffective action. Research on decision-making, complexity, and policy design repeatedly shows that effective reasoning begins with recognising what kind of problem you are facing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thecynefin.co/about-us/about-cynefin-framework/?srsltid=AfmBOop-bBFC2mZVwkpg2mtq1Kz76DQxmE_oRYog_7WG0ZhL-EEMR4XE" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thecynefin.co">[The Cynefin Co]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thecynefin.co</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Cynefin Co About</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Cynefin CoAbout - Cynefin FrameworkThe Cynefin Framework was developed to help leaders understand their challenges and to make decisi...</span></span></span>

## Clear and Complicated Problems

Clear and complicated problems both have underlying cause-and-effect relationships, but they differ in how difficult those relationships are to understand.


<img src="{{ "/assets/images/improving_your_think_da4710_problem_framing_7f52a6_problem_type_reasoni_030a43-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Problem Types illustration 3" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/improving_your_think_da4710_problem_framing_7f52a6_problem_type_reasoni_030a43-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/improving_your_think_da4710_problem_framing_7f52a6_problem_type_reasoni_030a43-Illustration-3-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">
### When best practice is enough

Clear problems are situations where the correct response is already known. The cause-and-effect relationship is stable, repeatable, and widely understood. A missing file, a standard payroll calculation, or a routine safety checklist usually falls into this category.

In these cases, the most effective reasoning approach is often not deep analysis but reliable execution. [Checklists]({{ 'checklists/' | relative_url }}), standard operating procedures, and established best practices outperform creativity because the challenge is consistency rather than discovery. Frameworks such as Cynefin describe this as a domain where people should identify the situation, categorise it correctly, and apply proven [practice]({{ 'practice/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cynefin_framework" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

The common mistake is overcomplicating a clear problem. Organisations sometimes hold lengthy meetings to debate issues that already have known solutions.

### When expertise and analysis matter

Complicated problems are different. The answer exists, but finding it requires investigation, specialist knowledge, or careful analysis.

Examples include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Designing a bridge.
* Diagnosing a rare engineering fault.
* Planning a spacecraft mission.
* Determining the cause of a manufacturing defect.

</div>

In these situations, evidence gathering and expert judgement become valuable. Multiple valid solutions may exist, but rigorous analysis can identify better and worse options. The relationship between cause and effect is discoverable even if it is not immediately obvious. Cynefin characterises this domain as one where leaders should analyse, consult experts, and compare alternatives before acting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://txm.com/making-sense-problems-cynefin-framework/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: txm.com">[TXM Lean Solutions+2Open Practice Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">txm.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lean Solutions Cynefin Framework Explained</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">TXM Lean SolutionsCynefin Framework Explained - The 5 Domains with...Approach for Complicated Problems: · Assess the situation and Sense...</span></span></span>

A useful historical comparison illustrates the distinction. A routine aircraft maintenance procedure is largely a clear problem. Designing a new aircraft engine is a complicated problem. Both are technical, but one depends primarily on following established knowledge while the other depends on generating and applying expert knowledge.

The failure mode here is assuming that more expertise automatically solves every problem. That assumption works reasonably well in complicated domains but often breaks down elsewhere.

## Complex Problems and Small Experiments

Complex problems look similar to complicated ones at first, yet they behave differently in an important way: the future cannot be fully predicted from existing knowledge.

In complex systems, many interacting elements influence one another. Human behaviour, organisational culture, markets, ecosystems, and social networks frequently display these characteristics. Cause and effect may only become visible after events unfold.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cynefin_framework" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2castintelligence.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Consider launching a new product. A technical team may analyse customer preferences, pricing, and competitors, but no amount of modelling can perfectly predict how consumers will react. Unexpected [feedback]({{ 'feedback/' | relative_url }}) loops, trends, and social influences can reshape [outcomes]({{ 'outcomes/' | relative_url }}).

Because prediction is limited, the reasoning method changes.

Rather than asking, “What is the optimal solution?”, the more useful question becomes, “What can we learn through safe-to-fail experiments?”

Researchers and practitioners working with complexity frameworks often recommend a pattern of:

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Run small probes or experiments.
2. Observe what emerges.
3. Adapt based on feedback.
4. Expand successful approaches.
5. Stop unsuccessful ones quickly. [Wageningen University & Research eDepot+3SI Labs+3Open Practice Library](https://www.si-labs.com/en/articles/cynefin-framework/)

</div>

This differs sharply from the reasoning used for complicated problems. If an engineer is calculating the load-bearing capacity of a bridge, experimentation is usually secondary to analysis. If a company is trying to change workplace culture, experimentation is often primary because the system itself changes in response to intervention.

One sign that a problem is complex is that experts disagree despite having access to the same information. Another is that successful solutions in one context fail when copied elsewhere. In these environments, learning matters more than certainty.

## Wicked Problems and Contested Success

Wicked problems occupy an even more challenging category. The concept emerged from the work of Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber, who argued that many social and policy challenges differ fundamentally from technical problems. [sympoetic.net+2Springer](https://www.sympoetic.net/Managing_Complexity/complexity_files/1973%20Rittel%20and%20Webber%20Wicked%20Problems.pdf)

The defining feature of a wicked problem is not merely complexity. It is disagreement about what the problem actually is and what success should mean.

Examples often include:

* Housing affordability.
* Climate policy.
* Healthcare reform.
* Educational inequality.
* Urban development.

In these cases, stakeholders frequently disagree on goals, causes, [trade-offs]({{ 'trade-offs/' | relative_url }}), and acceptable outcomes. A proposed solution may improve one measure while worsening another. There is rarely a definitive stopping point at which everyone agrees the problem has been solved. Rittel and Webber argued that solutions to wicked problems are often judged as better or worse rather than true or false. [sympoetic.net+2Wikipedia](https://www.sympoetic.net/Managing_Complexity/complexity_files/1973%20Rittel%20and%20Webber%20Wicked%20Problems.pdf)

The British housing shortage provides a useful illustration. Different groups attribute the problem to planning rules, construction costs, infrastructure constraints, skills shortages, financing conditions, environmental regulation, population growth, or political choices. Each explanation implies a different solution. Because stakeholders value outcomes differently, even defining success becomes contested. [The Times](https://www.thetimes.com/comment/columnists/article/this-wicked-problem-needs-bold-solutions-rjc33dc6p)

Reasoning about wicked problems therefore requires methods that go beyond technical analysis:

* Stakeholder mapping.
* Deliberation and negotiation.
* Explicit discussion of values.
* Scenario exploration.
* Iterative policy adjustment.
* Recognition of trade-offs rather than optimisation.

The failure mode is treating a wicked problem as if it were merely a difficult engineering challenge. More data and better models can help, but they cannot eliminate disagreements about values, priorities, or acceptable risks. [ResearchGate+2Sage Journals](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/320883976_The_enduring_challenge_of_%27wicked_problems%27_revisiting_Rittel_and_Webber)

## Why Misclassification Creates Bad Decisions

Many reasoning failures occur because people correctly analyse the wrong type of problem.

Several recurring [mistakes]({{ 'mistakes/' | relative_url }}) appear across organisations and public life:

* **Using procedures for complex situations.** Leaders impose rigid rules on evolving human systems and become surprised when behaviour changes.
* **Using expert analysis for wicked problems.** Technical reports answer factual questions while political and value conflicts remain unresolved.
* **Using experimentation where reliability is required.** Trial-and-error approaches create unnecessary risk in domains where proven practices already exist.
* **Treating every challenge as unique.** Teams repeatedly reinvent solutions to problems that are actually clear and well understood.

These errors are costly because they create an illusion of progress. People work hard, gather evidence, and produce plans, yet the reasoning method does not match the nature of the task.

## A Quick Diagnostic Before You Begin

Before starting detailed analysis, ask four questions:

QuestionLikely problem typeIs there an established best practice that reliably works?ClearCan experts discover the answer through analysis?ComplicatedWill the answer emerge only through experimentation and adaptation?ComplexDo stakeholders disagree about goals, definitions, or what counts as success?Wicked

Many real-world situations contain elements of more than one category. A healthcare system, for example, includes clear procedures, complicated medical expertise, complex organisational dynamics, and wicked policy disputes simultaneously.

The practical lesson is not to force every challenge into a single box. It is to recognise that different parts of a problem may require different reasoning methods. Good [problem framing]({{ 'problem-framing/' | relative_url }}) begins by identifying which kind of uncertainty you are facing, because the quality of your thinking depends as much on choosing the right mode of reasoning as on reasoning well.
